1 · Big picture: stroke as a syndrome

Adams & Victor's defines stroke by three converging criteria: (1) sudden temporal profile, (2) focal neurological deficit in a vascular territory, and (3) a clinical setting consistent with vascular disease (atrial fibrillation, hypertension, atherosclerotic risk factors).

The first division is ischaemic versus haemorrhagic — you cannot reliably distinguish them clinically; the CT scanner does that. About 85% are ischaemic, 15% are haemorrhagic (10% intracerebral, 5% subarachnoid).

2 · The first hour — door-to-needle

Modern stroke care resembles trauma resuscitation. Merritt's outlines five parallel priorities for the ED team:

  1. Assess level of consciousness, airway, breathing, circulation.
  2. Take a focused history — the single most important fact is time of symptom onset (or last known well).
  3. Establish large-bore (18-gauge) IV access; draw labs including CBC, electrolytes, glucose, INR/PTT, troponin.
  4. Perform the NIHSS.
  5. Get the patient to non-contrast CT ± CT angiography as fast as possible.
Time targets (NINDS): door-to-CT < 25 min · door-to-needle < 60 min (ideally < 45 min). The Helsinki model achieves < 20 min. 1.9 million neurons die every minute the brain remains ischaemic.

3 · Imaging strategy

Non-contrast CT (NCCT) is the only mandatory study before reperfusion. It excludes haemorrhage. Two important early signs:

  • Hyperdense vessel sign (dense MCA / basilar) — visible thrombus, marker of large-vessel occlusion.
  • Loss of grey-white differentiation / sulcal effacement / hypoattenuation — early ischaemic change. Quantified by ASPECTS (10-point MCA score, 1 point subtracted per region affected). ASPECTS < 7 predicts haemorrhagic transformation if tPA is given.

CT angiography (CTA) immediately after NCCT identifies large-vessel occlusion (LVO) — the population that benefits from thrombectomy. CT perfusion identifies salvageable penumbra and extends thrombectomy eligibility to 6–24 hours.

MRI / DWI is the gold standard for detecting infarction but is rate-limiting; reserve for unclear cases or wake-up strokes (DWI–FLAIR mismatch identifies recent infarcts eligible for tPA).

4 · Acute ischaemic stroke — reperfusion therapy

Adjuvant antithrombotics with IVT — DO NOT GIVE (2026 update)

Argatroban and eptifibatide should NOT be added to tPA to improve outcomes — multiple RCTs show no benefit (MOST trial). Class 3: No Benefit.

Post-IVT management

  • Admit to ICU or stroke unit.
  • Neuro checks + BP q15 min during/after IVT × 2 h, then q30 min × 6 h, then hourly to 24 h.
  • SBP 140–180 mmHg for the first 24 h (do not drive below 140 — no benefit, may harm).
  • No antithrombotics for 24 h; repeat NCCT or MRI at 24 h before starting any antithrombotic.
  • Delay NG tubes, indwelling bladder catheters, intra-arterial lines if patient can be safely managed without.
  • If severe headache, acute hypertension, vomiting, or neurological worsening occurs → stop alteplase infusion (TNK already complete), STAT NCCT to exclude haemorrhage.
Contraindications have become more relative over time. Absolute exclusions: blood on CT, completed infarct > 1/3 MCA, unknown time of onset (without DWI–FLAIR mismatch). Strong relative exclusions: INR > 1.7, platelets < 100,000, recent surgery/trauma/GI bleed. Mild ischaemic changes on CT (ASPECTS 7–9) are NOT a contraindication.

Mechanical thrombectomy — expanded eligibility (2026 update)

The 2026 AHA/ASA guideline substantially expands EVT eligibility, including patients with larger ischaemic cores and basilar artery occlusion. EVT and IVT are not mutually exclusive: give IVT if eligible and proceed to EVT.

Endovascular technique

Stent retriever, contact aspiration, or combination are equivalent (Class 1, ASTER2 / COMPASS). Preoperative tirofiban before EVT is NOT useful (2026 Class 3: No Benefit).

Time targets

Door-to-puncture < 90 min at thrombectomy-capable centre. Door-in-door-out (DIDO) < 60 min from spoke hospital (newly emphasized in 2026). Each hour of delay reduces good outcomes by ~6.7% in successfully reperfused patients (HERMES).

Three questions now decide reperfusion eligibility: (1) Is there a large-vessel occlusion? (CTA answers it.) (2) Is there a salvageable core? (NCCT ASPECTS ± CT perfusion answers it — and the 2026 guideline now accepts much lower ASPECTS.) (3) For basilar — is PC-ASPECTS ≥ 6 and NIHSS ≥ 10? (also EVT-eligible within 24 h.)

Acute medical management beyond reperfusion (2026-aligned)

  • Aspirin 160–300 mg within 24–48 h (after IVT: wait 24 h, repeat NCCT first to exclude bleed).
  • Dual antiplatelet (aspirin + clopidogrel) for 21 days then SAPT — 2026 update extends evidence to atherosclerotic minor stroke (NIHSS ≤ 5) or high-risk TIA (ABCD² ≥ 4) within 24–72 h (CHANCE, POINT, INSPIRES). Class 2a.
  • Permissive hypertension first 24–48 h if no IVT given (SBP up to 220/120). After IVT: SBP 140–180. After successful EVT: SBP 140–180 (do NOT push < 140 — 2026 guideline).
  • Statin — start high-intensity early (no change).
  • Glucose — target 140–180 mg/dL. 2026: do NOT use intensive control to 80–130 mg/dL (SHINE trial — no benefit, more hypoglycaemia). Treat hypoglycaemia < 60 promptly.
  • Temperature — nurse-driven protocol to maintain normothermia (treat > 37.5 °C); identify infection source. Prophylactic hypothermia in normothermic patients is NOT recommended.
  • DVT prophylaxis — sequential compression devices immediately; pharmacological once bleeding excluded.
  • Dysphagia screen before oral intake. 2026 update: pharyngeal electrical stimulation (PES) is a Class 2a option to reduce dysphagia severity and aspiration risk.
  • AF + new ischaemic stroke: 2026 update — early oral anticoagulation is reasonable in carefully selected (milder severity) patients (Class 2a, based on ELAN, OPTIMAS); older "wait 1-day-per-NIHSS-point" rules no longer mandatory.

5 · Vascular territory syndromes

Anterior circulation

Middle cerebral artery (MCA) — the most common stroke territory

Lenticulostriate (deep penetrators)"Striatocapsular" infarct — dense pure motor hemiparesis (face/arm/leg) without cortical signs.

Anterior cerebral artery (ACA)

Contralateral leg > arm > face weakness, sensory loss in same distribution, urinary incontinence, abulia, transcortical motor aphasia (dominant), grasp reflex, alien-hand. Bilateral ACA (azygos or vasospasm in SAH) produces paraplegia & akinetic mutism.

Internal carotid artery (ICA)

Spectrum from asymptomatic (good Willis) to large MCA ± ACA territory infarction. Amaurosis fugax (transient monocular blindness) is a classic ipsilateral TIA from ophthalmic artery embolism.

Posterior circulation

Posterior cerebral artery (PCA)

Proximal (P1): midbrain & thalamic syndromes — Weber (III + contralateral hemiparesis), Claude (III + contralateral ataxia), Benedikt (III + ataxia + tremor + hemiparesis); top-of-the-basilar syndrome with bilateral thalamic infarct, vertical gaze palsy, coma. Cortical (P3): contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing; alexia without agraphia if dominant. Bilateral PCA → cortical blindness (± Anton syndrome — denial of blindness).

Basilar artery

Bilateral long-tract signs, cranial nerve palsies, cerebellar signs, decreased consciousness. The classic locked-in syndrome (ventral pontine infarction): quadriplegia, lower cranial-nerve palsies, preserved vertical eye movement and consciousness. Top-of-the-basilar: bilateral thalami / midbrain / occipital lobes.

Lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg) — vertebral or PICA

The most testable and one of the most distinctive brainstem syndromes. Lesion in lateral medulla, posterior to inferior olive.

6 · TIA, mimics, and the stroke differential

The contemporary, tissue-based definition of TIA (AHA/ASA 2009): a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischaemia without acute infarction. Duration is typically < 1 hour; symptoms lasting longer almost always show DWI lesions.

A TIA carries a high short-term stroke risk (≈ 5% in 48 hours; up to 10% in 90 days). Estimate with the ABCD² score (Age ≥ 60, BP ≥ 140/90, Clinical features, Duration, Diabetes) but admit anyone with crescendo TIAs, atrial fibrillation, or carotid stenosis regardless of score.

Treating a stroke mimic with tPA is uncommon and safe (rate < 1% symptomatic ICH); delay to exclude a mimic is more dangerous than the small risk of erroneous treatment.

7 · Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)

ICH carries 30-day mortality of 30–40%, the worst of any stroke subtype. The most useful early prognostic marker is haemorrhage volume (ABC/2 method): a hematoma < 30 mL has good potential; 30–70 mL high deterioration risk; > 70 mL traditionally lethal (but salvageable with surgery in selected cases).

Aetiology by location

LocationTypical cause
Putamen, thalamus, pons, cerebellum, deep white matterChronic hypertension (Charcot–Bouchard microaneurysms)
Lobar (cortical)Cerebral amyloid angiopathy in elderly; AVM, tumour, anticoagulant in younger
Any location in younger or atypical patternAVM, dural fistula, cavernoma, vasculitis, drugs (cocaine, methamphetamine), tumour, RCVS, venous infarction

Acute management priorities

  1. Reverse anticoagulation immediately. Warfarin → 4-factor PCC (preferred over FFP) plus IV vitamin K. Dabigatran → idarucizumab. Apixaban/rivaroxaban → andexanet alfa or 4-factor PCC. UFH → protamine. LMWH → protamine partial reversal.
  2. BP control — nicardipine or clevidipine infusion to systolic 130–150 mmHg. Avoid nitroprusside (raises ICP).
  3. ICP management for mass effect: head of bed 30°, brief hyperventilation (PCO₂ 30) as a bridge, mannitol 1–1.5 g/kg or 23.4% hypertonic saline 30 mL (central access). Avoid steroids.
  4. External ventricular drain for intraventricular extension with hydrocephalus.
  5. Surgery — cerebellar haemorrhage > 3 cm or with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus is a neurosurgical emergency. Supratentorial evacuation: less clear benefit; case by case.
  6. Seizures — treat clinical or electrographic seizures; do not give prophylactic antiseizure drugs.
  7. Admit to ICU.
Cerebellar ICH is a surgical emergency. Patient may look stable for hours then collapse from brainstem compression. Call neurosurgery early.

8 · Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

80% of spontaneous SAH is from rupture of a saccular (berry) aneurysm at the circle of Willis. Other causes: AVMs, perimesencephalic (benign) SAH, RCVS, trauma. Risk factors: hypertension, smoking, family history, polycystic kidney disease.

Classic presentation

  • Thunderclap headache — "worst of my life", peak in < 1 minute.
  • Transient loss of consciousness in 50%.
  • Meningism (neck stiffness, photophobia) develops over hours.
  • Focal signs uncommon unless an ICH or vasospasm has occurred.
  • Sentinel/warning leak (1–2 weeks earlier) in 10–40%.

Diagnosis

  1. Non-contrast CT — sensitivity > 99% within 6 hours of onset on a modern scanner; falls to < 90% after 24 hours.
  2. If CT negative within 6 h and clinical suspicion is high, modern guidelines accept CT alone in many centres. Otherwise lumbar puncture for xanthochromia (visible after 12 hours).
  3. CT angiography (or DSA) to identify the aneurysm.

Acute management

  • Secure the aneurysm within 24 hours — endovascular coiling or surgical clipping (ISAT favours coiling when both are feasible).
  • BP systolic < 160 mmHg until secured.
  • Nimodipine 60 mg PO every 4 hours for 21 days — reduces secondary deficits from vasospasm.
  • Watch for delayed cerebral ischaemia from vasospasm (day 4–14), hydrocephalus, hyponatraemia (cerebral salt wasting), rebleeding, seizures, and neurogenic stunned myocardium.
Hunt–Hess grading on presentation predicts outcome: I asymptomatic; II moderate headache, no deficit; III drowsy, mild deficit; IV stupor, hemiparesis; V coma.

9 · Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST)

An uncommon but eminently treatable cause of stroke, predominantly in younger women. Risk factors: oral contraceptives, pregnancy/puerperium, thrombophilia, dehydration, sepsis (especially otitis or sinusitis), malignancy, COVID-19 / adenovirus-vectored vaccines.

Presents with one or more of: headache (most common, often subacute or worsening), seizures, focal deficits that do not respect arterial territories, encephalopathy, papilloedema (idiopathic-intracranial-hypertension-like picture).

Diagnosis is by CT venography or MR venography. Plain CT may show a dense vessel (cord sign) or empty delta sign on contrast study, or haemorrhagic infarcts not in arterial distributions (especially bilateral parasagittal or temporal).

Treatment is anticoagulation (heparin then warfarin or DOAC) even in the presence of haemorrhagic infarction. Endovascular thrombectomy in deteriorating patients (TO-ACT was neutral but useful in selected cases). Treat underlying cause; consider thrombophilia workup.

11 · Red flags & when to refer

Activate stroke pathway immediately for:
  • Any acute focal neurological deficit, however mild — "time is brain".
  • Sudden severe headache (especially thunderclap or first-of-life).
  • Acute monocular vision loss.
  • Crescendo TIAs.
  • Acute vertigo with any central HINTS feature.
  • Suspected SAH or ICH on CT — emergency neurosurgical and neuro-ICU referral.
  • Cerebellar infarct or haemorrhage of any significant size — risk of delayed swelling and brainstem compression.
  • Spinal cord deficit with possible spinal-cord stroke.
