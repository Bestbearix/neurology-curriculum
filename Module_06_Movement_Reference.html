1 · Big picture: phenomenology first, then diagnosis

The cardinal rule of movement disorders is: describe the phenomenology before you name the disease. The same disease can produce different phenomenologies, and one phenomenology has many causes. Merritt's framework:

  1. Is the movement hypokinetic (parkinsonism — bradykinesia, hypokinesia, akinesia) or hyperkinetic (excess movement)?
  2. If hyperkinetic, name the phenomenology by its rhythmicity, voluntariness, pattern, and texture: tremor, dystonia, chorea, ballism, athetosis, tic, myoclonus, stereotypy, akathisia.
  3. Then ask: primary or secondary (drug, structural, metabolic, autoimmune, paraneoplastic, infectious, genetic)?

The single most important habit: name the phenomenology first ("this is chorea") before reaching for a diagnosis ("this is Huntington"). Mislabelling the phenomenology is the most common error in this field.

2 · Approach to the patient with abnormal movements

History

  • Location — patients sometimes feel movements in body parts that aren't visibly shaking.
  • Tempo & periodicity — sudden vs gradual onset; paroxysmal (paroxysmal dyskinesias, episodic ataxia) vs continuous.
  • Triggers & relievers — exercise (PKD), stress, alcohol (reduces ET, may worsen dystonia), specific tasks (writer's cramp), sleep (most disappear).
  • Sensory tricks — touching chin lessens cervical dystonia (geste antagoniste).
  • Rest vs action — central to tremor classification.
  • Inner urge / suppressibility — tics, restless legs, akathisia all share these.
  • Family history — Huntington (AD), Wilson (AR), ET (familial often), DYT mutations (variable).
  • Drug history — current and historical exposure to dopamine-blocking agents (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine), lithium, valproate, AEDs, SSRIs, immunosuppressants, methadone, stimulants.
  • Nonmotor symptoms — anosmia, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, orthostasis (PD prodrome); cognitive decline (dementia with Lewy bodies, atypical PD); behaviour change (Huntington).

Examination

  1. Watch first, intervene second. Sit and observe; tell the patient "let your body do what it wants."
  2. Activation manoeuvres: writing, drawing spirals, holding arms out, finger-to-nose, finger taps, foot taps, walking (with attention to arm swing, stride, turning, freezing), specific tasks the patient says trigger the movement.
  3. Tone: rigidity (lead-pipe or cogwheel = parkinsonism), spasticity (clasp-knife = pyramidal), paratonia (frontal/dementia).
  4. Postural reflexes: pull test for retropulsion (early loss → PSP; later in PD).
  5. Oculomotor: slow saccades, square-wave jerks, supranuclear gaze palsy (PSP — especially downgaze).
  6. Cognition & mood screen.
  7. Look for skin/eye signs: Kayser–Fleischer rings (Wilson), acanthocytes on blood smear (neuroacanthocytosis), elevated CK.

3 · Tremor

Rhythmic oscillation about a joint. Classified by context (rest / postural / action / intention / task-specific) and frequency.

Essential tremor — most common adult movement disorder

  • Prevalence ~1% (rising with age); bilateral postural and kinetic hand tremor > 3 years; absent or minimal head/voice tremor in early disease; symmetric.
  • 50–70% family history (AD with variable penetrance).
  • Worsens with stress, caffeine, fatigue. Many patients note alcohol responsiveness.
  • Treatment: propranolol 60–320 mg/d or primidone 25–250 mg/d first-line. Add-on: topiramate, gabapentin, alprazolam (intermittently). Botulinum toxin for focal head or voice tremor. MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy and DBS (VIM nucleus) for refractory cases.

4 · Parkinson disease

Diagnostic essentials

PD is a clinical diagnosis. The defining lesion is loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta projecting to the putamen, with Lewy body pathology. Cardinal motor features (TRAP):

  • T remor at rest (4–6 Hz; asymmetric onset; pill-rolling)
  • R igidity (lead-pipe or cogwheel)
  • A kinesia / bradykinesia (essential criterion)
  • P ostural instability (later; not at presentation)

Diagnosis (MDS clinical criteria, simplified): bradykinesia plus rest tremor and/or rigidity, with supportive features (asymmetric onset, levodopa responsiveness, characteristic dyskinesia) and absence of exclusion features (e.g. early severe autonomic failure → MSA; early downgaze palsy → PSP).

Nonmotor features (often appear years before motor)

ProdromalEstablished disease
Anosmia · constipation · REM sleep behaviour disorder · depression · orthostatic hypotension · subtle cognitive slowingCognitive impairment / dementia (40% at 10 y) · psychosis (often levodopa-related) · autonomic dysfunction · sleep disorders · pain · fatigue · sialorrhoea · seborrhoea

Imaging

Brain MRI usually normal in PD (exclude structural causes and Parkinson-plus features like "humming bird" sign in PSP or putaminal T2 hypointensity in MSA). DaT scan (¹²³I-FP-CIT SPECT) shows reduced presynaptic dopamine transporter binding in PD & Parkinson-plus, normal in essential tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism, and SWEDD — useful when diagnosis uncertain.

Three drugs that must not be given to PD patients for nausea or psychosis: metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, haloperidol (and most typical antipsychotics). All block dopamine receptors and worsen PD. Safer alternatives: ondansetron for nausea, domperidone (peripheral antidopaminergic; check QT), quetiapine/clozapine/pimavanserin for psychosis.

5 · Atypical parkinsonism / Parkinson-plus syndromes

When parkinsonism doesn't behave like idiopathic PD, think Parkinson-plus. Red flags for Parkinson-plus:

  • Symmetric onset (PD is usually asymmetric).
  • Poor or absent levodopa response (despite adequate trial of 600–1000 mg/d for 1 month).
  • Early postural instability / falls (within 3 years).
  • Early dementia or hallucinations.
  • Early prominent autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction).
  • Cerebellar signs.
  • Vertical gaze palsy (especially down-gaze).
  • Apraxia, cortical sensory loss, alien-limb phenomenon.
  • Rapid progression.

7 · Dystonia

Sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing abnormal, often twisting, postures and movements; typically action-induced, often with a sensory trick (geste antagoniste). Cocontraction of agonist and antagonist.

Always trial levodopa in any childhood- or adolescent-onset dystonia — dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa, DYT5, GTP cyclohydrolase deficiency) dramatically improves with low-dose levodopa (50–100 mg/day) and is otherwise misdiagnosed as cerebral palsy.

8 · Chorea, ballism, athetosis

Chorea = brief, irregular, unpredictable, flowing movements that flit from one body part to another. Ballism = large-amplitude proximal flinging (usually hemiballism). Athetosis = slow, writhing distal movements (usually combined with chorea = choreoathetosis). All localise to the basal ganglia (typically striatum).

Causes

CategoryExamples
GeneticHuntington disease (AD, CAG trinucleotide repeat in HTT; chorea + dementia + psychiatric features); Huntington-like disorders (DRPLA, neuroacanthocytosis, McLeod, SCA17); Wilson disease; benign hereditary chorea
AutoimmuneSydenham chorea (post-streptococcal); chorea of pregnancy (chorea gravidarum); SLE / antiphospholipid syndrome; autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA-R, CASPR2)
Drug-inducedLevodopa-induced dyskinesia, oral contraceptives, anticonvulsants (phenytoin), stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines), tardive (chronic neuroleptics)
StructuralStroke (hemiballism = contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion; hemichorea); tumour; abscess
Metabolic / endocrineHyperthyroidism, polycythemia vera, non-ketotic hyperglycaemia (asymmetric chorea/ballism with T1 striatal hyperintensity), hyponatraemia, hypoparathyroidism
InfectiousHIV (cerebral toxoplasmosis), encephalitis

Huntington disease

Autosomal dominant. CAG repeat > 39 in HTT (chromosome 4p). Onset typically 35–50. Triad: chorea + cognitive decline (subcortical dementia) + psychiatric (depression, irritability, psychosis, suicide). Juvenile form (Westphal variant) presents as parkinsonism + seizures + ataxia rather than chorea. MRI: caudate atrophy. Treatment is symptomatic — tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, or valbenazine (VMAT2 inhibitors) for chorea; antipsychotics (sometimes both calm chorea and treat psychiatric features); SSRIs for depression. Genetic counselling is essential.

Hemiballism

Sudden large-amplitude flinging on one side. Classical cause: contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion, most often lacunar stroke. Improves spontaneously over weeks-months in most cases. Symptomatic treatment with dopamine-blocking agents or tetrabenazine. Always check glucose: non-ketotic hyperglycaemia is a treatable cause (with characteristic T1 striatal hyperintensity).

9 · Tics & Tourette syndrome

Brief, stereotyped, semi-voluntary movements (motor tics) or sounds (vocal/phonic tics). Patients describe a premonitory urge; tics can be briefly suppressed but at the cost of mounting tension, then relief upon release.

Tourette syndrome

  • Both multiple motor tics AND at least one vocal tic, present > 1 year, with onset before age 18 (typically 5–7 y).
  • Frequently coexists with ADHD (~50%), OCD (~30%), anxiety, depression.
  • Tics wax and wane; often improve by adulthood.

Treatment

  • First-line: education, reassurance, comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics (CBIT — habit reversal therapy).
  • Pharmacological (only if tics cause functional impairment): alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) first-line for mild/moderate; VMAT2 inhibitors (tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine) or low-dose atypical antipsychotics (risperidone, aripiprazole) for moderate–severe; topiramate; botulinum toxin for focal/severe tics.
  • Treat comorbid ADHD (stimulants are not contraindicated despite older teaching) and OCD (SSRI ± CBT).

10 · Myoclonus

Sudden brief shock-like jerks. Positive myoclonus = muscle contraction; negative myoclonus = brief loss of tone (e.g. asterixis — the flap of metabolic encephalopathy).

Valproate, clonazepam, levetiracetam, piracetam. Always treat the underlying cause when identifiable.

Drug history clinches it (recent serotonergic vs antipsychotic).

12 · Movement-disorder emergencies

  • Acute dystonic reaction — IV/IM anticholinergic immediately (benztropine, diphenhydramine).
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome — stop drug, ICU, dantrolene/bromocriptine/amantadine.
  • Serotonin syndrome — stop drug, supportive, cyproheptadine if severe.
  • Status dystonicus — severe generalised dystonia with rhabdomyolysis, renal failure, respiratory compromise; ICU; deep sedation; consider DBS.
  • Parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome (NMS-like in PD patient after abrupt withdrawal or under-dosing of dopaminergic therapy) — restore dopaminergic therapy + supportive ICU care.
  • Acute hemichorea / hemiballism — exclude structural lesion (stroke, tumour) and check glucose (non-ketotic hyperglycaemia).
  • Wilson disease crisis — fulminant hepatic failure + neuropsychiatric features in young patient; urgent transplant evaluation.

13 · When to refer to a neurologist / movement-disorder clinic

Refer:
  • Diagnostic uncertainty about parkinsonism (PD vs Parkinson-plus vs drug-induced).
  • Suspected PD before starting therapy (some clinicians; varies by health system).
  • PD with motor fluctuations or dyskinesia refractory to oral therapy → DBS evaluation.
  • Any patient under ~50 with parkinsonism or movement disorder (rule out Wilson, genetic, autoimmune).
  • Suspected Huntington disease (genetic testing requires counselling).
  • Acute / subacute progressive chorea or movement disorder of unknown cause.
  • Severe focal dystonia for botulinum toxin therapy.
  • Tardive dyskinesia requiring VMAT2 inhibitor.
  • Movement-disorder emergencies above.
