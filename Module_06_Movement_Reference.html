1 · Big picture: phenomenology first, then diagnosis

The cardinal rule of movement disorders is: describe the phenomenology before you name the disease. The same disease can produce different phenomenologies, and one phenomenology has many causes. The working sequence is:

  1. Hypokinetic or hyperkinetic? Hypokinetic = parkinsonism (bradykinesia with decrement, rigidity, rest tremor, postural instability). Hyperkinetic = excess movement.
  2. If hyperkinetic, name it by rhythmicity, voluntariness, suppressibility, speed and pattern: tremor, dystonia, chorea, ballism, athetosis, tic, myoclonus, stereotypy, akathisia, ataxia (a disorder of coordination rather than an involuntary movement, but it sits in the same clinic).
  3. Then ask: primary or secondary? Drug, structural, metabolic, autoimmune, paraneoplastic, infectious, genetic, functional.
  4. Then ask: what is treatable here? In every hyperkinetic presentation there is a short list of reversible causes that must be excluded before a degenerative label is applied.

The single most important habit: name the phenomenology first ("this is chorea") before reaching for a diagnosis ("this is Huntington"). Mislabelling the phenomenology is the most common error in this field, and it propagates into every subsequent test you order.

Four traps in naming

  • Tic vs stereotypy vs compulsion. Tics have a premonitory urge and brief suppressibility; stereotypies are longer, more rhythmic, start earlier (before age 3) and carry no urge; compulsions are performed to neutralise an idea.
  • Dystonic tremor vs parkinsonian tremor. Dystonic tremor is jerky, direction-specific, worse in certain postures, and has a null point. See §4.
  • Myoclonus vs tremor. Myoclonus is discontinuous and shock-like even when it repeats rapidly; a video or EMG burst duration (<100 ms in myoclonus) settles it.
  • Asterixis is not a tremor. "Flapping tremor" is negative myoclonus — a brief silent period in a sustained contraction — and points to a metabolic or toxic cause. It is usually asymptomatic and only found if you look for it.

2 · Approach to the patient with abnormal movements

History

  • Location — patients sometimes feel movements in body parts that are not visibly shaking (orthostatic tremor is felt rather than seen).
  • Tempo & periodicity — hyperacute (stroke, drug), subacute (autoimmune, metabolic, paraneoplastic), chronic-progressive (degenerative, genetic); continuous vs paroxysmal (paroxysmal dyskinesias, episodic ataxia).
  • Triggers & relievers — sudden movement (paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia), prolonged exercise (paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia), fasting, stress, caffeine, specific tasks (writer's cramp), alcohol (relieves essential tremor, myoclonus-dystonia and SCA27B episodes; may worsen dystonia).
  • Sensory tricks (geste antagoniste) — touching the chin lessens cervical dystonia. Nearly pathognomonic for dystonia.
  • Rest vs action — the backbone of tremor classification.
  • Inner urge / suppressibility — shared by tics, restless legs and akathisia; the three are distinguished by what relieves them (movement itself relieves RLS; performance of the tic relieves the urge; akathisia is relieved only partially and is drug-linked).
  • Family history — three generations. Huntington and most SCAs are autosomal dominant; Wilson, Friedreich, ARSACS and ataxia-telangiectasia recessive; ET often dominant with variable penetrance; myoclonus-dystonia (SGCE) shows maternal imprinting so transmission appears paternal.
  • Drug history — current and historical. Ask specifically about metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, antipsychotics (including "for sleep" or "for stomach"), lithium, valproate, SSRIs/SNRIs, amiodarone, calcium-channel blockers used for vertigo (cinnarizine, flunarizine), immunosuppressants, opioids and stimulants. Exposure may have ended months before onset.
  • Non-motor symptoms — anosmia, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, orthostasis (PD/synucleinopathy prodrome); cognitive or behavioural change (Huntington, DLB, PSP); psychiatric change with liver disease (Wilson).
  • Functional impact — the treatment threshold in almost every condition here is impairment, not severity of the movement.

Examination

  • Watch first, intervene second. Sit and observe for a full minute with the patient's hands in the lap, then on the knees palms-down, then outstretched. Say "let your body do what it wants."
  • Activation manoeuvres: writing, Archimedes spirals (both hands, same page — this is your permanent record), pouring water, arms outstretched and in the "wing-beating" posture, finger-to-nose, rapid finger and foot taps looking for decrement, walking with attention to arm swing, stride, turning and freezing, and any task the patient names as a trigger.
  • Distraction and entrainment: ask the patient to tap a rhythm with the unaffected hand. Functional tremor entrains, pauses or changes frequency; organic tremor does not.
  • Tone: rigidity (lead-pipe or cogwheel = parkinsonism, enhanced by contralateral activation), spasticity (velocity-dependent, clasp-knife = pyramidal), paratonia (frontal/dementia), and the continuous co-contraction of dystonia.
  • Postural reflexes: pull test. Retropulsion within 3 years of onset argues against PD and for PSP.
  • Oculomotor: saccade velocity (slow vertical saccades precede frank gaze palsy in PSP), square-wave jerks, supranuclear gaze palsy with preserved oculocephalic reflex, downbeat nystagmus (CANVAS, SCA27B, Chiari, drugs), oculomotor apraxia (AOA, ataxia-telangiectasia).
  • Gait, balance and posture — a sign-based approach. Do not wait for a complete classic syndrome. Each single axial sign carries its own differential: a widened base (cerebellar, sensory, vestibular, frontal, functional), a narrowed base (dystonia, functional, spastic), festination and freezing (PD, vascular, NPH, PSP-PGF), a magnetic gait (NPH, vascular), truncal lateral flexion (Pisa syndrome), camptocormia, retropulsion.
  • Systemic and eye signs: Kayser–Fleischer rings on slit lamp (Wilson — present in >95% of neurological Wilson), conjunctival telangiectasia (ataxia-telangiectasia), cataracts (myotonic dystrophy, CTX), acanthocytes on a fresh wet-film smear (neuroacanthocytosis), hepatosplenomegaly, orofacial self-mutilation (chorea-acanthocytosis, Lesch-Nyhan).

A default first-tier work-up

Tailor, but in an unexplained movement disorder the following rarely wastes money: FBC with film for acanthocytes, U&E, calcium, magnesium, glucose/HbA1c, LFTs, TFTs, CK, ESR/CRP, ANA, antiphospholipid antibodies, B12, and — in anyone under 50 — serum caeruloplasmin, serum copper and 24-hour urinary copper plus slit-lamp examination. MRI brain with iron-sensitive (SWI/GRE) sequences. Add ASO/anti-DNase B if post-streptococcal chorea is plausible, HIV and syphilis serology where prevalence justifies, and anti-GAD65, anti-neuronal and paraneoplastic panels if the tempo is subacute.

Do not order genetic testing before you have excluded the acquired causes. A negative gene panel in a patient with untreated hyperthyroidism, polycythaemia or antiphospholipid syndrome has cost the patient months.
If those tests are not available in your hospital, read section 3 next. It grades every investigation in this module by urgency — what justifies sending the patient today, what can wait for a planned referral visit, what you can do locally that is often forgotten (slit lamp, blood film for acanthocytes, bladder scan, 24-hour urine collection), and which treatments must be started before the result arrives.

3 · Investigation strategy when the advanced tests are not on site

In a hospital without same-day MRI, a neuroimmunology laboratory, or copper studies in the building, the question is never simply which test. It is: does this result change what I do today, this week, or this month — and what do I do while I wait? This section grades every investigation in the module by urgency, states the action that must not wait for the result, and says what to send with the patient if they have to travel.

The organising principle. Movement disorders are rarely diagnosed by a test. They are diagnosed at the bedside and confirmed or narrowed by a test. So the urgency of an investigation is set almost entirely by one question: is there a treatment I would start, stop or change within the next few hours or days if I knew the answer? If yes, the test is urgent and may justify transfer. If no, it can be batched into a planned referral visit — and the patient is not harmed by the wait.

The five urgency tiers

3.3 · Wilson disease — the workup you can largely do locally, and why it is urgent

Urgency: tier C for the tests, tier B if there is hepatic dysfunction. Wilson disease is the one condition in this module where a delayed diagnosis is both catastrophic and entirely preventable. A young patient with a movement disorder plus jaundice, deranged LFTs, Coombs-negative haemolysis, or a low alkaline-phosphatase-to-bilirubin ratio may be in evolving acute liver failure and needs the same-day conversation with a transplant centre — not a copper result in three weeks.

3.4 · B12 and the other nutritional tests — cheap, local, and safe to treat empirically

B12 is the easiest win in this whole section. The assay is cheap and usually available locally; the treatment is safe, cheap and parenteral; and the harm of waiting is irreversible cord damage. So the rule is simple: in a patient with a plausible clinical picture, send the level and start treatment the same day. You can always stop it. The rule that matters most in a resource-limited setting: do not let a pending antibody result delay immunotherapy in a deteriorating patient. Autoimmune encephalitis is diagnosed on clinical criteria supported by MRI, EEG and CSF — and antibody-negative disease is real, so even a returned negative panel does not close the case. In a patient with a subacute movement disorder plus encephalopathy, seizures, psychiatric change or dysautonomia, take the samples (paired serum and CSF where you can), exclude and cover infection, and start first-line immunotherapy — corticosteroids, IVIg or plasma exchange — on clinical grounds. Outcomes are better the earlier treatment starts, and the antibody result will arrive to confirm or reclassify a decision you have already had to make.

Practical sample handling that saves a repeat trip:

  • Take paired serum and CSF at the same sitting for suspected autoimmune encephalitis or stiff-person spectrum disease. Sending serum alone is a common and costly error — some antibodies are detected only in CSF, and intrathecal synthesis is what makes a GAD65 result meaningful.
  • Store an extra aliquot of serum and CSF frozen before immunotherapy, if your laboratory can. Once steroids or IVIg are given, later sampling may be uninterpretable — and pre-treatment samples are the only chance to answer the question retrospectively.
  • Send the LP request with the clinical question written on it. Cell count, protein, glucose with a paired plasma glucose, oligoclonal bands, cytology, and microbiology are different requests; the tube volume needed differs, and repeating an LP because a tube was short is avoidable.
  • For GAD65, ask explicitly for a titre, not a positive/negative. Low titres are common in type 1 diabetes and thyroid disease and mean little; stiff-person spectrum disease is associated with very high titres.

3.8 · Making one referral trip do the work of three

When a patient must travel, the cost to them is a day of work, a bus fare and often a family member's day as well. Plan the visit so it is not repeated.

  • Send a written summary that states the phenomenology in the first line — "asymmetric rest tremor with decremental bradykinesia" tells the receiving clinician more than "Parkinson's for review". Include the drug history with dates, the levodopa trial dose and duration, lying and standing blood pressures, and the post-void residual.
  • Send your own results with the patient, on paper: bloods, ECG, CT images or report, and the slit-lamp report. A referral centre that cannot see your results will repeat them.
  • Ask for the tests by name in the letter — "MRI brain with iron-sensitive SWI/GRE sequences, for putaminal signal change and midbrain atrophy" gets a different scan from "MRI brain". The MDS criteria explicitly note that these features need a radiologist who has been told to look for them.
  • Batch the day: imaging, LP with paired serum, neurophysiology and the specialist opinion in one visit, not four.
  • Start the 24-hour urine collection before the trip, in the correct container, so the sample travels with the patient.
  • Say explicitly what you have already excluded and what you have already started. It prevents duplication and it stops your empirical treatment being stopped on arrival.
  • Use the telephone before the transfer for anything in tier B. A five-minute conversation with the receiving neurologist or neurosurgeon reorders the whole plan more often than a referral letter does.
The summary you can teach in one sentence. Tier A costs nothing and should already be done; tier B decides whether the patient travels today; tier C is where most treatable disease is found, so send those tests this week and treat empirically where it is safe; tier D should be batched into a single planned visit; and tier E should never delay treatment that is already indicated.

4 · Tremor

Rhythmic oscillation of a body part about a joint axis, produced by alternating or synchronous contraction of reciprocally innervated muscles. Tremor is the commonest movement disorder in clinical practice.

The MDS two-axis classification

The current framework classifies every tremor along two independent axes and only then assigns a syndrome:

  • Axis 1 — clinical characteristics: historical features (age at onset, family history, temporal course), tremor characteristics (body distribution, activation condition — rest / postural / kinetic / intention / task-specific / position-specific, frequency), associated signs (other neurological signs, systemic signs), and laboratory tests (electrophysiology, imaging, tissue markers).
  • Axis 2 — aetiology: acquired, genetic, or idiopathic.

Axis 1 gives you a tremor syndrome (e.g. isolated tremor syndrome, combined tremor syndrome); axis 2 gives the cause. The practical value is that it forces you to state the activation condition and the associated signs before you say "essential tremor".

Essential tremor

  • Prevalence ~1%, rising steeply with age; the most common adult movement disorder.
  • Definition: isolated bilateral upper-limb action tremor of at least 3 years' duration, with or without tremor in other locations (head, voice, lower limb), and without other neurological signs.
  • ET-plus is the MDS term for ET with additional soft signs (questionable dystonic posturing, impaired tandem gait, mild memory impairment, rest tremor). It remains contested: a Lancet Neurology personal view argues the label lacks pathological or prognostic basis and may simply describe advanced-stage ET. Use it descriptively, not as a separate disease.
  • Worsens with stress, caffeine, fatigue and sleep deprivation. Many patients note transient alcohol responsiveness — helpful historically, useless therapeutically.
  • Rising evidence links ET epidemiologically with later PD risk; a patient with ET who develops asymmetry, bradykinesia or a rest tremor needs re-evaluation, not a dose increase.

Drug-induced tremor

Under-recognised, and usually reversible. The commonest offenders are amiodarone, SSRIs and SNRIs, amitriptyline, lithium, valproate, β-adrenoceptor agonists, dopamine-receptor antagonists, VMAT2 inhibitors, and drugs of abuse (ethanol, cocaine). The phenotype usually mimics either essential or parkinsonian tremor depending on the drug class. Risk factors: polypharmacy, older age, male sex, higher doses, immediate-release preparations and toxic levels.

  • Features that should redirect you away from a simple drug cause: unilateral tremor, task-specific or position-dependent tremor, sudden onset, distractibility, entrainment, arrest with contralateral movement — these point to dystonic or functional tremor.
  • Pre-existing tremor is frequently not documented before the drug starts, so also consider hyperthyroidism, physiological tremor, ET, PD and cerebellar disease.
  • A temporal relation to starting or escalating the drug is the strongest diagnostic evidence. Most resolve on withdrawal; a minority persist ("tardive tremor").

Cerebellar and cerebellar-outflow tremor

Generated by network instability in the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit rather than by a single focal lesion: the dentate nucleus is the output gateway and a potential oscillator, the superior cerebellar peduncle the conduit, the red nucleus–inferior olive loop a rhythm generator, and the thalamic VIM the relay and amplifier. Clinically low-frequency, high-amplitude, worst on approach to target, and combined with ataxia — which is why it is among the most disabling and most therapy-resistant tremors. Drugs are generally disappointing; VIM DBS or thalamotomy helps selected patients (a documented case of post-traumatic Holmes tremor after bilateral dentate haemorrhage and thalamic axonal injury responded to radiofrequency VIM thalamotomy with benefit sustained at 18 months).

Orthostatic tremor

A rare, disabling condition of the sixth decade onwards: a fast (≥13 Hz) tremor in the legs on standing, producing a fear of standing still rather than a visible tremor. The largest multidimensional cohort (58 patients: 52 OT, 6 orthostatic myoclonus) found OT and orthostatic myoclonus overlap clinically and are separated electrophysiologically; dementia, polyneuropathy and diabetes were commoner in orthostatic myoclonus and secondary OT. Clonazepam is the most prescribed drug. Perampanel was continued long-term by 17 of 31 patients in a prospective cohort (mean 32 months' follow-up), and continuers had better social functioning, mental health and pain scores — but adverse effects were common and persistent in those who stopped. Always check thyroid function: a fully reversible pseudo-orthostatic tremor has been described in Graves disease.

Functional tremor

The most common functional movement disorder, roughly half of all FMD; reported in up to 11% of tremor referrals to movement-disorder clinics. Most common in young adults, particularly women, but described across all ages including children and the elderly. Psychiatric comorbidity is frequent but not required for diagnosis. Diagnosis is positive and rule-in (see §15): abrupt onset, variable frequency and direction, distractibility, entrainment, co-activation sign, tremor arrest on contralateral ballistic movement, and "whack-a-mole" migration when one limb is restrained.

5 · Parkinson disease

Diagnostic essentials

PD remains a clinical diagnosis. The defining lesion is loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta projecting to the putamen, with α-synuclein Lewy pathology. Cardinal motor features (TRAP):

  • Tremor at rest — 4–6 Hz, asymmetric, pill-rolling, re-emergent
  • Rigidity — lead-pipe or cogwheel, enhanced by contralateral activation
  • Akinesia / bradykinesia — the essential criterion: slowness with decrement in amplitude or speed on repetitive movement
  • Postural instability — a late feature; if it appears within 3 years, question the diagnosis

MDS clinical criteria, simplified: bradykinesia plus rest tremor and/or rigidity; then look for supportive criteria (clear and dramatic levodopa response, levodopa-induced dyskinesia, rest tremor in a limb, olfactory loss or cardiac MIBG denervation), absolute exclusion criteria (cerebellar signs, downgaze supranuclear palsy, behavioural-variant FTD or primary progressive aphasia in the first 5 years, parkinsonism restricted to the legs for >3 years, dopamine-blocker exposure consistent with drug-induced parkinsonism, absence of response to high-dose levodopa, cortical sensory loss, normal functional dopaminergic imaging), and red flags (rapid gait deterioration requiring a wheelchair within 5 years, complete absence of progression over 5 years, early bulbar dysfunction, inspiratory stridor, severe autonomic failure in the first 5 years, recurrent falls within 3 years, disproportionate antecollis or contractures, absence of any non-motor features after 5 years, unexplained pyramidal signs, bilateral symmetric onset).

Risk and protective factors — what to tell patients who ask "why me?"

An epidemiological review of the field summarises the associations. Report them honestly as associations, several of which may reflect reverse causation:

  • Inverse associations (apparently protective): tobacco use and coffee/tea/caffeine intake — the best-validated of all; also physical activity, higher serum urate, NSAID use, calcium-channel blocker use, statin use, some α1-adrenergic antagonists. Increased urate, tobacco use and caffeine intake may partly be products of reverse causation (prodromal PD changes behaviour). There is an inverse relationship between PD and the risk of most cancers.
  • Risk associations: traumatic brain injury, pesticide exposure, organic solvents, lead, air pollution, type 2 diabetes, some dairy products, cardiovascular disease, and some infections (hepatitis C, H. pylori, COVID-19). Non-environmental: bipolar disorder, essential tremor, bullous pemphigoid, inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Bottom line: no prevention trial has succeeded. PD incidence would most plausibly be reduced by mitigating general public-health risks — type 2 diabetes, air pollution, TBI, physical inactivity. Never advise a patient to smoke.

Genetics — when to think about it

Monogenic and risk-conferring variants now include SNCA, LRRK2, GBA1, PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1, VPS35 and a growing list of loci; the frequency of each varies markedly by ancestry, and most published data come from European populations. LRRK2 G2019S is common in North African Berber and Ashkenazi Jewish populations; GBA1 variants are the commonest genetic risk factor overall and predict faster cognitive decline. Genetics-informed trials (LRRK2 inhibitors, GBA1-directed therapies) are under way, so a genetic diagnosis is beginning to have therapeutic consequences.

Consider referral for genetic counselling if: onset before 50 (before 40 makes it a priority), a first-degree relative with PD, consanguinity, or an ancestry with a known high-frequency founder variant.

Imaging

Brain MRI is typically normal in PD; its role is to exclude structural causes and to look for Parkinson-plus signatures (midbrain atrophy in PSP, putaminal T2 hypointensity and hot-cross-bun in MSA, asymmetric perirolandic atrophy in CBS, basal-ganglia calcification, iron accumulation). DaT-SPECT (¹²³I-FP-CIT) shows reduced presynaptic dopamine-transporter binding in PD and Parkinson-plus, and is normal in essential tremor, dystonic tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism, functional parkinsonism and SWEDD. It does not distinguish PD from PSP/MSA/CBS. Cardiac ¹²³I-MIBG scintigraphy is reduced in PD, DLB and pure autonomic failure (postganglionic) and normal in MSA (preganglionic) — the single most useful discriminator between PD and MSA when the question is close.

Three questions about levodopa that the literature has now settled

  • Does delaying levodopa prevent motor complications? No. The LEAP trial randomised early PD to levodopa/carbidopa 300/75 mg daily for 80 weeks versus 40 weeks of placebo then 40 weeks of active drug; at 5 years (n=321) there was no difference in total UPDRS at 3 or 5 years, and dyskinesia occurred in 46/160 early-start vs 62/161 delayed-start (p=0.06) — if anything favouring early treatment. A meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (n=1149) found no difference in overall motor complications (OR 1.39, p=0.37) or dyskinesias (OR 1.52, p=0.11), and fewer motor fluctuations in early starters (OR 0.70, p=0.02).
  • Does starting with a dopamine agonist help? It delays dyskinesia at the cost of worse motor scores and more agonist-specific adverse effects. In the subgroup analysis of the same meta-analysis, dyskinesia was less frequent when a dopamine agonist was used to spare levodopa dose — but this is bought with impulse-control disorders, somnolence and hallucinations. CALM-PD established the framework for this trade-off.
  • Is rasagiline disease-modifying? Unresolved, and probably unanswerable with this design. ADAGIO (n=1176, delayed-start) found rasagiline 1 mg/day met all three prespecified endpoints for disease modification but 2 mg/day did not — a dose-discordance that undermines the claim. A companion critique lists the confounders intrinsic to delayed-start designs: very small effect sizes, probable incomplete blinding by study end, subjective UPDRS components, and practice effects from repeated scoring. Prescribe rasagiline for symptom control, not for neuroprotection.

Pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and eating, punding, hobbyism and dopamine dysregulation syndrome.   • Screen every PD patient regularly and involve a partner or family member — patients frequently conceal these behaviours and the consequences are financial and marital, not just medical.
  • First-line for mild through severe ICBD: gradual reduction of the dopamine agonist, then reduction of other PD medications. Beware dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome (anxiety, panic, pain, diaphoresis, drug craving) — taper slowly.
  • Second-line: reverse the last medication change; cognitive behavioural therapy; STN-DBS; quetiapine or clozapine; antidepressants.

Pisa syndrome and postural deformity

Pisa syndrome is a reversible lateral trunk flexion (conventionally ≥10°) that lessens on lying supine or with passive mobilisation. It is a frequent and disabling complication of PD, also described in atypical parkinsonism and after drug exposure — including antiparkinsonian drugs themselves, cholinesterase inhibitors and antipsychotics. Pathophysiology is contested between a central hypothesis (altered sensorimotor integration and body-alignment perception) and a peripheral one (myopathic and musculoskeletal change). The practical point is that it may be reversible if recognised early: review recent drug changes, treat concurrent osteoporosis and arthrosis, and start physiotherapy promptly, because a reversible postural syndrome can become a fixed contracture.

Deep brain stimulation — the timing question

EARLYSTIM randomised 251 patients (mean age 52, mean disease duration 7.5 years) with early motor complications to STN-DBS plus medical therapy or medical therapy alone. At 2 years, DBS was superior for quality of life (PDQ-39 summary index, mean difference −7.8 points, p<0.001) and for motor function, activities of daily living and time with good mobility. The message is that DBS should not be reserved for end-stage disease; the trigger is troublesome motor complications despite optimised medication, in a patient with preserved levodopa responsiveness, no dementia and no uncontrolled psychiatric illness.

Three drugs that must not be given to a patient with Parkinson disease for nausea or agitation: metoclopramide, prochlorperazine and haloperidol (and most typical antipsychotics). All block D2 receptors and can precipitate severe, sometimes prolonged, deterioration. Safer alternatives: ondansetron or domperidone (peripherally acting — check the QT interval) for nausea; quetiapine, clozapine or pimavanserin for psychosis. Write this on the drug chart of every admitted PD patient.
Never abruptly stop dopaminergic therapy — including when a patient is nil by mouth. Missed doses in hospital are a common, avoidable cause of parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome (§20). Use a rotigotine patch or nasogastric levodopa if the oral route is lost, and prescribe the patient's own timings rather than standard drug rounds.

PSP — phenotypes and prognosis

PSP is not one clinical picture. A global systematic review reports prevalence 5–6.4 per 100 000 with substantial geographic variation driven by criteria and healthcare infrastructure, and describes the main phenotypes:

  • PSP-Richardson syndrome (PSP-RS) — the classic picture; rapid progression, median survival ~5–7 years.
  • PSP-parkinsonism (PSP-P) — asymmetric onset, tremor, an initial levodopa response; frequently misdiagnosed as PD for years; milder course, survival ~8–12 years.
  • PSP-progressive gait freezing (PSP-PGF) — pure freezing of gait and micrographia, with other features appearing late.
  • PSP-CBS, PSP with speech/language disorder, PSP-frontal presentation.

Cognition in PSP is not a late add-on. A dysexecutive syndrome from dorsolateral prefrontal atrophy and fronto-subcortical disconnection is often an early sign, with deficits extending to attention, memory, language, visuospatial function and social cognition. Apathy is the commonest behavioural symptom (medial prefrontal and subcortical atrophy), while impulsivity and disinhibition reflect orbitofrontal involvement. Dementia in PSP is common and under-diagnosed; it is distinguishable from frontotemporal and subcortical dementias by its motor context and the subcortical 4R-tau distribution. Assess cognition early — it drives quality of life, prognosis and care planning more than the motor score does.

Practical additions for the general clinic. (1) Measure a post-void residual by bladder scan in every suspected MSA — it is now a formal criterion and it is a bedside test. (2) Ask specifically about nocturnal stridor; partners describe it as a harsh crowing on inspiration and it carries a risk of sudden death. (3) Request iron-sensitive (SWI/GRE) sequences explicitly, and tell the radiologist you are looking for MSA — the MDS criteria state that these features need a neuroradiologist who has been briefed to look for them.

Beyond the criteria, resting-state fMRI is emerging as a subtype-specific biomarker: MSA-P shows predominant basal ganglia–cortical disruption, MSA-C cerebellar–cortical disconnection, and the patterns can separate MSA from PD and PSP where atrophy measures are still insensitive. This remains research-grade.

Corticobasal degeneration — the specificity problem

The Armstrong 2013 consensus criteria usefully widened the recognised phenotype of CBD beyond the classic corticobasal syndrome (to include frontal-behavioural/spatial syndrome, non-fluent primary progressive aphasia and a PSP-like syndrome). But a clinicopathological validation study found the criteria lack the specificity needed for antemortem diagnosis: all 14 patients with a corticobasal syndrome but non-CBD pathology met criteria for probable or possible CBD, and none of the clinical features was more common in pathologically proven CBD (n=19) than in the mimics. In pathologically confirmed CBD, 47% met probable-CBD criteria at presentation and 68% by last assessment.

What this means clinically: "corticobasal syndrome" is a defensible clinical diagnosis; "corticobasal degeneration" is a pathological one you cannot make at the bedside. Counsel patients in terms of the syndrome, and keep the differential (PSP, Alzheimer disease, FTLD-TDP, CJD, DLB) open. Treatment is symptomatic — levodopa trials are usually disappointing, botulinum toxin helps focal dystonia, clonazepam or levetiracetam help myoclonus, and occupational therapy for the apraxic limb matters more than any drug.

Deep brain stimulation in atypical parkinsonism

A PRISMA systematic review identified 25 studies covering only 66 patients with atypical parkinsonism treated with DBS (31 PSP, 22 MSA, 12 DLB), of which just 3 were randomised and most were at high risk of bias. Current evidence does not support DBS in atypical parkinsonism. Notable signals: a high frequency of cognitive and neurobehavioural problems in MSA patients given STN-DBS; a low complication rate with nucleus basalis of Meynert DBS in DLB; and possible benefit for dystonic symptoms with GPi-DBS in PSP. Treat this as a research question, and be explicit with families who have read about DBS online.

Postencephalitic parkinsonism

Rarely considered today, but still described. It follows an encephalitic illness and is pathologically distinct from PD: tau/neurofibrillary tangle pathology without α-synuclein or Lewy bodies, and without the iron accumulation seen in PD — which means iron chelators have no rationale. Levodopa response is usually limited, though an adolescent with severe post-encephalitic parkinsonism rendered bedbound has been reported to regain independence on levodopa, so a trial is still warranted. Consider it in a young patient with parkinsonism after a documented encephalitis, particularly with oculogyric crises, sleep–wake inversion or behavioural change.

8 · Dystonia

Sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing abnormal movements, postures, or both — typically patterned, often twisting, frequently initiated or worsened by voluntary action, with overflow to adjacent muscles. Agonist and antagonist co-contract. Non-motor manifestations (pain, depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, sensory and perceptual changes) are part of the clinical spectrum, not incidental.

Four clinical clues that a movement is dystonic

  1. Patterned phenomenology — the same muscles, in the same direction, every time.
  2. Alleviating manoeuvres (sensory tricks/geste antagoniste) — a light touch to the chin, cheek or back of the head reduces cervical dystonia; the effect is disproportionate to the force applied.
  3. Worsening by voluntary movement, sometimes only for a specific task.
  4. Overflow into adjacent muscles not needed for the task (mirror dystonia in the other hand while writing is a useful confirmatory sign).

Botulinum toxin — what the recent trials add

  • DaxibotulinumtoxinA (DAXI), the first long-acting BoNT-A, was tested in two phase 3 RCTs in cervical dystonia (ASPEN-1 in North America and Europe plus ASPEN-1-CN in China; n=357, randomised 3:3:1 to 125 U, 250 U or placebo). TWSTRS total improved by −12.0 (125 U) and −11.9 (250 U) versus −4.6 for placebo (p<0.0001), with a median duration of effect of about 24 weeks — roughly double conventional formulations — and lower rates of dysphagia and muscle weakness than in prior pivotal BoNT trials.
  • Quality of life is a valid endpoint. A liquid abobotulinumtoxinA RCT showed CDIP-58 quality-of-life scores improved significantly (−9.5 with 500 U vs −0.9 with placebo, p<0.0001), maintained across four open-label cycles, with positive correlation between TWSTRS and CDIP-58 domains. Ask about pain, sleep and social withdrawal, not just head position.
  • Dose, muscle selection and injection technique matter more than the brand. Secondary non-response should prompt review of muscle selection before assuming neutralising antibodies.

Dystonic tremor

There has never been a trial designed specifically for dystonic tremor. A systematic review pooled 487 patients from 43 papers: drug efficacy was generally disappointing, with only moderate effects from anticholinergics, tetrabenazine, clonazepam, β-blockers and primidone; levodopa worked only in dopa-responsive dystonia. The largest and best evidence is for botulinum toxin, particularly for axial (head and voice) tremor. For refractory cases, DBS of GPi, thalamus or the subthalamic area produced marked improvement in most reported cases.

Always trial levodopa in any childhood- or adolescent-onset dystonia. Dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa disease, GTP cyclohydrolase I deficiency, DYT5/GCH1) responds dramatically and durably to low-dose levodopa (start 25–50 mg/day, usually adequate at 50–100 mg/day, occasionally up to 300 mg/day in divided doses). Classic clues: onset in a leg with gait disturbance, diurnal fluctuation (worse by evening, better after sleep), and a family history. It is regularly misdiagnosed as cerebral palsy or spastic diplegia for decades. Give a trial of at least 1 month before concluding it is not dopa-responsive.
Status dystonicus — increasingly frequent and severe generalised dystonic spasms causing pain, exhaustion, rhabdomyolysis, renal failure and respiratory compromise. It is a medical emergency: ICU admission, sedation (midazolam, propofol, occasionally general anaesthesia with neuromuscular blockade), aggressive hydration and CK/renal monitoring, treatment of the trigger (infection, drug change, surgery), and urgent consideration of GPi-DBS or intrathecal baclofen. 9 · Chorea, ballism, athetosis

Chorea = brief, irregular, unpredictable movements that flow randomly from one body part to another; the defining property is unpredictability, and patients often camouflage them with semi-purposeful movements (parakinesia). Motor impersistence — the milkmaid grip, the darting tongue that cannot be held out — is a useful bedside sign. Ballism = large-amplitude proximal flinging, usually hemiballism. Athetosis = slow, writhing, distal movements, usually combined with chorea. All localise to the basal ganglia, typically the striatum and its outflow.

The diagnostic principle in new-onset chorea

Treatable and reversible causes take priority over genetic ones. A structured review of 243 published sources on asymmetric and hemichorea found that the vast majority of unilateral or markedly asymmetric chorea is acquired, not genetic or degenerative — the commonest single cause being non-ketotic hyperglycaemic hemichorea-hemiballism, followed by Sydenham chorea, antiphospholipid syndrome and drug-induced chorea. And crucially: symmetric generalised chorea does not exclude a reversible cause, so investigate both patterns.

Non-ketotic hyperglycaemic chorea (diabetic striatopathy) — the one you must not miss

A narrative review of 80 publications covering 100 adult patients: mean age 66.6 years, female predominance (67%), hemichorea in 71% with a left-sided preponderance, and 75% already known to have diabetes. Mean presenting glucose 468 mg/dL (~26 mmol/L) and mean HbA1c 13.7%. Basal ganglia hyperintensity on MRI (T1) in 80% — CT may show contralateral striatal hyperdensity. Management centres on prompt glycaemic control, with a dopamine blocker (haloperidol is the most reported) or a VMAT2 inhibitor for symptom relief while glucose is corrected; most improve over days to weeks, though a minority persist.

Check a capillary glucose in every patient with acute chorea or hemiballism, before anything else. It is free, immediate, and identifies the single commonest reversible cause.

Sydenham chorea, PANDAS and chorea gravidarum

  • Sydenham chorea — the commonest acquired chorea of childhood worldwide and a major criterion of acute rheumatic fever. Latency of 1–8 months after group A streptococcal pharyngitis; generalised or hemichorea with emotional lability, obsessive features, hypotonia and motor impersistence. Investigate with ASO/anti-DNase B, echocardiography for carditis, and treat with secondary penicillin prophylaxis. Symptomatic control: valproate, carbamazepine or a dopamine blocker; corticosteroids or IVIg for severe or refractory cases.
  • PANDAS — the hypothesis that group A streptococcus triggers autoimmunity against basal-ganglia dopaminergic circuits, producing OCD, tics or chorea depending on the receptor subtype involved. A 2026 review concludes the biology is plausible and advancing — anti-D1R autoantibodies as candidate neuropsychiatric biomarkers, anti-D2R as candidate movement biomarkers, and striatal cholinergic interneuron dysfunction as a candidate mechanism — but that PANDAS has remained a diagnostic conundrum since 1998, with unresolved uncertainty about criteria, the interpretation of streptococcal serology (a raised titre proves exposure, not causation), and the distinction from primary neurodevelopmental tic and OCD disorders. Practical stance: treat proven streptococcal infection, do not treat serology alone, avoid repeated antibiotic courses and immunotherapy outside specialist care, and manage the tics/OCD on their own merits.
  • Chorea gravidarum — chorea appearing in pregnancy, historically linked to previous rheumatic fever/Sydenham chorea and now also to antiphospholipid syndrome and SLE. In a case report with literature review, a 16-year-old primigravida with prior Sydenham chorea developed chorea at 8 weeks; symptoms were controlled by 14 weeks with an antipsychotic plus benzodiazepine and benzathine penicillin for the aetiology, treatment was stopped at 35 weeks, and she delivered normally at 39 weeks. Management is largely expectant and symptom-directed, and maternal and fetal prognosis is good. Screen for antiphospholipid antibodies, lupus and thyroid disease, and review any oestrogen exposure.

Huntington disease

Autosomal dominant; CAG repeat expansion in HTT on 4p16.3. Repeat length: <27 normal; 27–35 intermediate (not disease-causing but unstable in paternal transmission); 36–39 reduced penetrance; ≥40 full penetrance. Typical onset 35–50 years, with anticipation in paternal transmission. The triad is chorea + cognitive decline (a subcortical, dysexecutive dementia) + psychiatric disturbance (depression, irritability, apathy, obsessionality, psychosis; suicide risk is elevated, particularly around the time of diagnosis and loss of independence). Motor impersistence, ocular motor apraxia with slowed saccade initiation, and later dystonia and parkinsonism. The juvenile (Westphal) variant presents with parkinsonism, seizures, ataxia and cognitive decline rather than chorea, and is usually paternally transmitted. MRI shows caudate atrophy with box-car ventricles.

Predictive genetic testing requires a formal protocol with pre- and post-test counselling — never send the test from a general clinic. Testing an asymptomatic at-risk person, and testing minors, are governed by international guidelines.

Hemiballism

Sudden large-amplitude flinging of one side. Classically a contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion, most often a lacunar infarct, but striatal and thalamic lesions produce it too. Most cases improve spontaneously over weeks to months. Symptomatic treatment with a dopamine blocker or tetrabenazine while it settles; severe unremitting ballism can cause exhaustion, dehydration and rhabdomyolysis and rarely needs GPi-DBS. Always check glucose and image the brain.

Neuroacanthocytosis and the ataxia-chorea overlap

  • Chorea-acanthocytosis (VPS13A, chorein) is the core neuroacanthocytosis syndrome. Suspect it in an HD phenocopy with orofacial dyskinesia, tongue- and lip-biting, feeding dystonia with tongue protrusion on eating, epilepsy, peripheral neuropathy, raised CK and raised liver enzymes, plus acanthocytes on a properly prepared smear. Imaging shows caudate atrophy and striatal hypometabolism. The related syndromes are McLeod (XK, X-linked, with a Kell-null red-cell phenotype relevant to transfusion), HDL2, PKAN, abetalipoproteinaemia and aceruloplasminaemia.
  • Chorea plus cerebellar ataxia is a powerful narrowing combination. The differential is short: spinocerebellar ataxias (notably SCA17, SCA1, SCA2, SCA3), Huntington disease, DRPLA, ataxia-telangiectasia, disorders of brain iron metabolism, Wilson disease, Niemann-Pick type C, ataxia with oculomotor apraxia, GLUT1 deficiency, and mitochondrial cytopathies. Several of these are treatable — which is the reason to recognise the combination.

10 · Tics & Tourette syndrome

Tics are repetitive, patterned, non-rhythmic movements (motor tics) or sounds (phonic/vocal tics) that are misplaced in context. Two features separate them from every other hyperkinesia: a premonitory urge (an uncomfortable sensation relieved by performing the tic) and brief suppressibility at the cost of mounting tension. They wax and wane, migrate, worsen with stress, excitement and fatigue, and often persist in light sleep.

Tourette syndrome

  • Definition: at least two motor tics and at least one phonic tic, present for more than one year, with onset before age 18 (typically 5–7 years). Prevalence about 1% of school-aged boys; highly heritable; male predominance ~3–4:1.
  • Natural history: tics peak around 10–12 years and improve in most patients by early adulthood; a minority have persistent, disabling tics.
  • Comorbidity is the rule, not the exception: around 85–90% have at least one mental-health comorbidity — ADHD (~50%), OCD (~30%), anxiety, depression, rage attacks, learning difficulties. These comorbidities usually affect quality of life more than the tics do, and must be screened for and treated in their own right.
  • Pathophysiology centres on cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit dysfunction with dopaminergic hyperactivity, though multiple transmitter systems are implicated.

When does a tic need drug treatment?

A critical appraisal of the literature identifies three indications for pharmacotherapy, and they are all about impact rather than tic count:

  1. Physical discomfort — pain or injury (cervical tics with radiculopathy or, rarely, myelopathy; self-injurious tics).
  2. Emotional or social problems — depression, bullying, isolation, avoidance.
  3. Functional interference — impaired academic achievement or work.

The goal is to reduce tic severity enough to improve daily functioning and quality of life — not to eliminate tics. Expert consensus favours patient-centred decision-making integrating a disease-specific quality-of-life measure with an objective tic severity scale (YGTSS).

Functional tics and tic-like behaviours new

Until the early 2010s functional tic disorders were rare in the differential. Since then — peaking through the COVID-19 pandemic and social-media exposure — cases have risen steeply, and the field has defined clinical clues and moved toward consensus criteria. Features that point to functional rather than neurodevelopmental tics:

  • Abrupt onset with severe symptoms from the start, typically in late adolescence or early adulthood (rather than age 5–7), often in girls and women.
  • Complex repetitive behaviours and phrases rather than simple motor tics; a high prevalence of phonations and vocalisations with frequent swearwords (coprolalia is common here, whereas it occurs in only a minority of true Tourette).
  • Large variability — spontaneous remissions and re-emergence, marked situational dependence, and often no premonitory urge or a qualitatively different one.
  • Predominantly arm and trunk involvement; absence of the typical rostro-caudal progression and childhood tic history; poor response to antipsychotics.

Management follows functional neurological disorder principles (§15): a confident, positive, non-dismissive explanation; withdrawal of ineffective antipsychotics; targeted psychological therapy and specialist physiotherapy; treatment of comorbid anxiety and depression.

Cannabinoids. Patients and families frequently ask about cannabis for tics. A 2026 Lancet Psychiatry systematic review of cannabinoids for mental and substance use disorders found the efficacy and safety evidence remains weak overall. There are small trials of THC in Tourette, but the evidence base does not support routine use; discuss legal status, cognitive effects in adolescents, and psychosis risk before entertaining it.

Asterixis — the sign to go looking for

Asterixis is a subtype of negative myoclonus: brief arrhythmic lapses of sustained posture caused by involuntary pauses in muscle contraction, elicited by asking the patient to extend the wrists with fingers spread, or by dorsiflexing the feet or holding the tongue out. It is not specific to any single process, but it is most often reported in hepatic encephalopathy, renal failure and respiratory failure/hypercapnia, in cerebrovascular disease (unilateral asterixis suggests a contralateral thalamic or midbrain lesion), and with drugs that raise ammonia — valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin. It should be distinguished from epileptic negative myoclonus, because management differs. It is usually asymptomatic and never volunteered by the patient — which is exactly why you must elicit it deliberately in every encephalopathic patient. It reverses when the cause is treated.

Myoclonus-dystonia

A distinctive combined syndrome: childhood-onset subcortical myoclonus, isolated or predominating over a milder dystonia, mostly involving the upper body (neck, arms, trunk), typically action-induced. The main gene is SGCE (ε-sarcoglycan), which is maternally imprinted — so a positive family history usually shows apparent paternal transmission. Alcohol responsiveness of the motor symptoms is characteristic and carries a real risk of alcohol misuse. Psychiatric manifestations — anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder — often accompany the motor features and are part of the phenotype.

Drug response is variable and tolerability limits most options (clonazepam, valproate, levetiracetam, zonisamide, trihexyphenidyl). Perampanel, a selective non-competitive AMPA-receptor antagonist, has been reported to benefit subcortical myoclonus in a patient with a novel SGCE mutation who had failed a wide range of antiseizure drugs, without adverse effects. GPi-DBS is effective in refractory cases.

Other causes worth remembering

  • Post-hypoxic myoclonus. Acute post-anoxic status myoclonus in the first day after cardiac arrest is a poor-prognosis marker (as one component of multimodal prognostication — see Module 13/22). Lance-Adams syndrome is the chronic action myoclonus that emerges in survivors who regain consciousness, and it is compatible with good functional recovery — do not confuse the two.
  • Opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome: chaotic multidirectional saccades with myoclonus and ataxia; paraneoplastic (neuroblastoma in children; small-cell lung and breast cancer in adults) or parainfectious. Requires tumour search and immunotherapy.
  • Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease: rapidly progressive dementia with startle-sensitive myoclonus, ataxia and visual symptoms; DWI cortical ribboning, EEG periodic sharp-wave complexes, CSF RT-QuIC.
  • Post-infectious and post-COVID myoclonus: generalised myoclonus with or without ataxia following SARS-CoV-2 has been reported, with a generally good prognosis, though some patients have persistent myoclonus for months. Proposed mechanisms include systemic illness (stroke, post-hypoxic), direct CNS invasion, and post-infectious immune-mediated damage.
  • Metabolic: hepatic and uraemic encephalopathy, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperthyroidism, Hashimoto encephalopathy.

An imaging-led approach to chronic ataxia

A useful practical framework sorts chronic ataxias into three MRI categories:

ILOCA versus MSA-C — the question that matters most in an adult ataxia clinic

Many patients presenting with late-onset cerebellar ataxia and no family history are labelled idiopathic (ILOCA/SAOA). A substantial proportion later declare themselves as MSA-C.

  • In a retrospective cohort of 72 ILOCA patients, 32 converted to MSA-C, with a median time-to-conversion of 5.0 years. Baseline REM sleep behaviour disorder or orthostatic dizziness predicted conversion; the hot-cross-bun sign did not (it appears late). Plasma neurofilament light chain measured before conversion had auxiliary value in estimating conversion time.
  • The "phalanx sign" — sought during the finger-to-nose test — discriminated MSA-C from ILOCA with sensitivity 61.9%, specificity 95.7%, PPV 92.9%, NPV 73.3% and substantial interrater reliability (κ=0.73) in a study of 21 MSA-C, 23 ILOCA and 20 controls. A highly specific bedside sign of this kind is most useful for ruling in.
Practical consequence: in every patient with late-onset cerebellar ataxia, ask about dream enactment and postural dizziness, measure lying and standing blood pressure, and check a post-void residual. These three bedside items — not the MRI — are what identify the patients who are converting to MSA-C, and they change prognostic counselling, driving advice and follow-up interval.

Adult-onset hereditary ataxias — what has changed

The genetic landscape has shifted substantially, and the change has direct diagnostic consequences.

  • SCA27B — FGF14-GAA repeat expansion. Now recognised as one of the commonest causes of late-onset ataxia. Phenotype: episodic gait unsteadiness (often precipitated by alcohol or exertion) evolving to progressive ataxia, downbeat nystagmus, predominant tremor and cognitive decline in some series, and frequent response to 4-aminopyridine or acetazolamide.
  • CANVAS — RFC1 repeat expansion. Cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy and vestibular areflexia; chronic cough often precedes the neurology by years; sensory neuronopathy with a non-length-dependent pattern; abnormal head-impulse test in all directions.
  • SCA4 has been resolved as a ZFHX3 repeat expansion; THAP11 expansions are a newly described cause.
  • Diagnostic blind spot. Repeat expansions are not detected by standard exome or, in many pipelines, genome sequencing. A published case describes a man in his late sixties with five years of episodic gait unsteadiness triggered reliably by alcohol, whose FGF14 expansion was missed after negative genome sequencing — with the added lesson of "forced diagnostic unification", where a single diagnosis is stretched to explain findings that actually reflect dual pathology.
Rule for the general clinic: "The genetic tests were negative" does not close an adult-onset ataxia. Ask which test was done. If a repeat-expansion panel (FGF14, RFC1, SCA1/2/3/6/7/17, FXTAS/FMR1 premutation, Friedreich FXN, DRPLA) was not specifically performed, the workup is incomplete.

Post-infectious cerebellitis new

An immune-mediated cerebellar inflammatory syndrome following an infection (or rarely a vaccination) after a latent interval, spanning a spectrum:

  • Acute cerebellar ataxia (ACA) — benign, self-limited, classically post-varicella in a young child, with normal or minimal imaging findings.
  • Acute cerebellitis (AC) — imaging-positive, with cerebellar swelling and leptomeningeal enhancement, encephalopathy, and risk of hydrocephalus and persistent deficits.
  • Acute fulminant cerebellitis (AFC) — rare and life-threatening: rapid cerebellar oedema, obstructive hydrocephalus and brainstem compression.

A PRISMA systematic review pooled patient-level data from 73 individuals across 47 studies (mean age 24.5 years, range 7 months–74 years; 40 paediatric, 28 adult; 73% male), confirming that the condition affects adults as well as children.

Fulminant cerebellitis is a neurosurgical emergency. A three-case paediatric series makes the point sharply: a 12-year-old deteriorating despite corticosteroids recovered fully after emergent posterior fossa decompression; a 2-year-old initially misdiagnosed as a posterior-fossa tumour, given high-dose steroids with delayed EVD, progressed to brainstem failure and died; a 10-year-old treated with urgent EVD plus high-dose steroids and intensive monitoring recovered completely. The determinant of outcome is the speed of CSF diversion and decompression, not the steroid dose. Any child or adult with cerebellitis, declining consciousness and a fourth ventricle under pressure needs a neurosurgeon now.

Symptomatic management of ataxia

There is no disease-modifying therapy for most ataxias, but there is a great deal to offer: intensive coordinative physiotherapy and balance training (the best-evidenced intervention of all), occupational therapy and home adaptation, speech and swallowing therapy, weighted utensils and walking aids, treatment of coexisting neuropathy and spasticity, driving assessment, and management of the non-motor burden (depression, fatigue, sleep, bladder). Drug options are limited but worth trying in the right phenotype: 4-aminopyridine for downbeat nystagmus and for episodic ataxia type 2 and SCA27B, acetazolamide for EA2, riluzole and N-acetyl-leucine with modest evidence in mixed ataxias, and vitamin E for AVED and abetalipoproteinaemia (where it is curative if started early).

13 · Restless legs syndrome & periodic limb movement disorder new section

RLS (Willis-Ekbom disease) is a common sensorimotor disorder — prevalence quoted between 5% and 25% depending on definition, roughly twice as common in women, and the commonest movement disorder in pregnancy (which carries a four-fold increased risk of developing RLS later in life). It is poorly recognised, frequently misdiagnosed as cramps, neuropathy or anxiety, and often mismanaged.

Diagnosis — the five essential criteria (IRLSSG)

  1. An urge to move the legs, usually accompanied by uncomfortable sensations.
  2. The urge begins or worsens during rest or inactivity.
  3. The urge is partially or totally relieved by movement, for as long as the movement continues.
  4. The urge is worse in the evening or night, or occurs only then.
  5. These features are not solely accounted for by a mimic — leg cramps, positional discomfort, myalgia, venous stasis, oedema, arthritis, habitual foot tapping, akathisia, or neuropathic pain.

Supportive features: a family history, a response to dopaminergic treatment, and periodic limb movements of sleep. Periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD) is diagnosed only when periodic limb movements on polysomnography are accompanied by sleep disturbance or daytime impairment and are not better explained by another condition (including RLS itself).

Evaluate before you prescribe

  • Iron studies in everyone: serum ferritin and transferrin saturation (calculated from serum iron and TIBC), at presentation and again whenever symptoms worsen or augmentation develops. The RLS thresholds are not the general-population thresholds.
  • Secondary causes: iron deficiency of any cause, chronic kidney disease/dialysis, pregnancy, peripheral neuropathy, and comorbid cardiovascular disease, diabetes, rheumatological and respiratory disease.
  • Exacerbating drugs: antihistamines (including over-the-counter sleep aids), antidopaminergic agents (metoclopramide, antipsychotics), most antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, mirtazapine, tricyclics), alcohol and caffeine. Bupropion is the antidepressant least likely to worsen RLS — but the AASM specifically suggests against using bupropion as a treatment for RLS.
  • Screen for and treat untreated obstructive sleep apnoea, which both mimics and aggravates RLS.

Augmentation — the reason dopamine agonists fell from first-line. Augmentation is a drug-induced worsening of RLS: symptoms become more intense, start earlier in the day, appear with shorter periods of rest, and spread from the legs to the arms and trunk. It is dose-dependent, occurs with all dopaminergic agents (highest with levodopa and short-acting agonists), and can be catastrophic — patients frequently escalate the dose, which worsens it further. Management: re-check iron status and repleteby; do not increase the dopaminergic dose; cross-taper to an α2δ ligand; use a low-dose opioid to cover the transition if needed; and expect several difficult weeks. The best treatment is prevention — do not start a dopamine agonist first-line.

Practical treatment sequence

  1. Remove aggravators (drugs, caffeine, alcohol, untreated OSA) and address sleep hygiene, moderate regular exercise, and non-pharmacological measures (massage, temperate baths, pneumatic compression) — some patients need nothing more.
  2. Repletion of iron to the RLS-specific thresholds above. Oral iron is best absorbed as alternate-day dosing with vitamin C on an empty stomach; use IV iron when oral is not tolerated, in malabsorption, or when ferritin is 75–100 ng/mL.
  3. α2δ ligand first-line — gabapentin enacarbil, gabapentin or pregabalin, taken 1–3 hours before symptom onset. Particularly suited to patients with pain, insomnia or anxiety. Watch sedation, dizziness, weight gain, ankle oedema, and dose-reduce in renal impairment.
  4. Refractory disease — specialist referral; low-dose extended-release oxycodone or methadone under a structured opioid-stewardship protocol; peroneal nerve stimulation; consider re-checking for augmentation and for a comorbid sleep disorder.

14 · Stiff-person spectrum disorders new section

A group of rare neuroimmunological conditions characterised by CNS neuronal hyperexcitability: fluctuating axial and limb muscle stiffness, painful spasms, gait disturbance and hypersensitivity to triggers (noise, touch, emotional stress). Historically called a "one-in-a-million" disorder, recent epidemiology suggests it is more common than previously recognised. There are still no consensus diagnostic criteria; proposed criteria combine the clinical picture, high antibody titres and electrophysiology.

The spectrum

  • Classic SPS — axial and limb muscles; lumbar hyperlordosis; a stiff, cautious, "tin soldier" gait; falls without protective reflexes.
  • Partial / focal SPS — stiff-limb syndrome (one limb, often a leg with fixed foot dystonia) or stiff-torso syndrome.
  • SPS-plus — with brainstem or cerebellar dysfunction (nystagmus, ataxia, dysarthria).
  • PERM — progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus: the severe end, with encephalopathy, brainstem signs, dysautonomia and respiratory compromise; often glycine-receptor antibody positive.
  • Paraneoplastic SPS — most often anti-amphiphysin with breast cancer or small-cell lung cancer.

Antibodies and comorbidity

Up to 70% of SPSD patients are seropositive for GAD65; others carry antibodies to amphiphysin, the glycine receptor, DPPX or GABA-A receptor. GAD65 is the rate-limiting enzyme for GABA synthesis, which links the antibody to the neuronal disinhibition that produces the phenotype. Titre matters: low-titre GAD65 antibodies are non-specific and common in type 1 diabetes and thyroid disease; SPSD is associated with very high titres, and CSF testing with intrathecal synthesis increases specificity.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 38 studies (1166 patients) confirms that autoimmune comorbidities are highly prevalent in SPSD — particularly in PERM, classic SPS and GAD65-positive patients — which should drive routine screening for type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo and coeliac disease at diagnosis and during follow-up.

Investigation

  • Serum and CSF GAD65 (with titre), amphiphysin, glycine receptor, DPPX; CSF oligoclonal bands.
  • EMG: continuous motor unit activity in agonist and antagonist muscles simultaneously, abolished by sleep, general anaesthesia, benzodiazepines or peripheral nerve block — the electrophysiological hallmark.
  • MRI brain and spine to exclude structural and demyelinating mimics.
  • Age- and antibody-appropriate tumour screening (mammography, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, FDG-PET where indicated) — mandatory with amphiphysin antibodies.
  • Screen for the associated autoimmune diseases above.

Treatment — three parallel arms

  1. Symptomatic (GABAergic). Benzodiazepines are the backbone — diazepam (first reported in 1963) and clonazepam, started low and titrated against symptoms and sedation. Most patients need a maintenance dose plus intermittent rescue doses for breakthrough spasms; short-acting agents such as lorazepam are best reserved for rescue. Add baclofen (GABA-B agonist) and/or tizanidine; second- and third-line, gabapentin (spasm relief) and pregabalin (more useful for neuropathic pain). Intrathecal baclofen for severe refractory cases.
  2. Immunotherapy. IVIg (the best-evidenced), corticosteroids, plasma exchange, then steroid-sparing agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate), rituximab, and — in refractory disease — newer approaches including anti-CD19 CAR-T. Subcutaneous immunoglobulin is an option for maintenance.
  3. Non-pharmacological. Careful physiotherapy (aggressive stretching can provoke spasms), heat, aquatic therapy, desensitisation to triggers, falls prevention, and psychological support — task-specific phobias of open spaces and street crossing are part of the disorder, not a separate anxiety disorder.
Two safety points. (1) Benzodiazepine doses in SPS are often far higher than usual; abrupt withdrawal can precipitate life-threatening spasms and autonomic instability — never stop them suddenly, including on hospital admission. (2) Patients with SPS fall like felled trees, without protective reflexes; fractures and head injuries are common and falls prevention is a core part of management.

15 · Functional movement disorders new section

Functional movement disorders (FMD) are a common, potentially reversible cause of abnormal motor function and one of the main subtypes of functional neurological disorder (FND). Incidence of FND is 10–22 per 100 000 per year; FMD specifically 4–5 per 100 000 person-years, accounting for 3–8% of movement-disorder clinic visits. In the largest multicentre cohort (n=4905) the commonest phenotypes were mixed FMD (23%), tremor (22%), weakness (18%), then gait disorders and dystonia.

The paradigm shift: rule-in, not rule-out

FMD is no longer a diagnosis of exclusion. It is made positively, on clinical signs of internal inconsistency (variability, distractibility, entrainment) and incongruence with recognised neurological disease. At least 37 positive clinical signs have been described. The most useful:

Mechanism — what to tell the patient

Current models suggest FMD arise from interacting abnormalities in predictive coding and sensorimotor integration, an altered sense of agency, maladaptive attentional focus on the affected body part, and disrupted limbic–motor network connectivity. These mechanisms explain both symptom generation and its maintenance — and they give you an honest, non-dismissive explanation: the movement system's hardware is intact; the problem is in how movement is being predicted, attended to and owned. That is why the symptom changes with attention and distraction, and it is also why it can improve.

Psychological stressors are not required for the diagnosis. DSM-5 removed that requirement, and psychiatric comorbidity — though common — is absent in a substantial minority. Never make the diagnosis contingent on finding a trauma, and never communicate it as "there is nothing wrong" or "it's stress".

Treatment

  1. The explanation is the first treatment. Deliver a clear, confident, validating diagnosis: name the condition, say how you made it (demonstrate Hoover sign or entrainment to the patient), state that it is common, real and potentially reversible, and provide a resource (neurosymptoms.org).
  2. Individualised rehabilitation focused on movement retraining by a physiotherapist familiar with FND — redirecting attention away from the affected part, retraining normal movement patterns, avoiding aids that reinforce abnormal movement.
  3. Tailored psychotherapy addressing perpetuating cognitive, behavioural and emotional factors (CBT is the best studied), where relevant.
  4. Pharmacotherapy is adjunctive only — for comorbid depression, anxiety, pain and sleep disturbance. There is no drug for FMD itself.
  5. Prognosis is variable and often guarded, which is precisely why early diagnosis and early intervention matter. Long delays to diagnosis worsen outcome.
FMD and organic disease coexist. Roughly 10–15% of patients have both — functional overlay in Parkinson disease, functional tremor superimposed on essential tremor, functional seizures with epilepsy. Diagnosing one never excludes the other, and treating both is the point. Cross-reference Module 21.

16 · Paroxysmal dyskinesias new section

Rare conditions of recurrent, brief episodes of involuntary movement — dystonic, choreic, ballistic or mixed — with a normal neurological examination between attacks. They are frequently misdiagnosed as epilepsy, functional attacks or panic attacks for years. Classification is by precipitant, not by phenomenology.

  • Marked pleiotropy. Mutations in PRRT2, PNKD, SLC2A1 and others produce still-expanding clinical spectra — PRRT2, for example, also causes benign familial infantile epilepsy, hemiplegic migraine and episodic ataxia within the same family.

How to separate paroxysmal dyskinesia from epilepsy at the bedside: consciousness is preserved throughout, there is no postictal confusion, attacks are often preceded by a sensory aura in the affected limb, and the precipitant is stereotyped (sudden movement, prolonged exercise, coffee). Video from the family's phone is the single most valuable investigation.

17 · Hemifacial spasm and peripheral movement disorders new section

Hemifacial spasm

Involuntary, unilateral, intermittent clonic then tonic contractions of the muscles innervated by the facial nerve, usually beginning in the orbicularis oculi and spreading downward over years. It is a peripherally generated movement disorder, most often caused by vascular compression at the root exit zone of CN VII (typically anterior inferior cerebellar artery, posterior inferior cerebellar artery, or the vertebral artery), with notable prevalence among Asian populations. Distinguishing features: it persists in sleep, is unilateral, and often shows the "other Babinski" sign (eyebrow elevation with eye closure) — which separates it from blepharospasm (bilateral, disappears in sleep, sensory tricks help).

Every patient with hemifacial spasm needs an MRI with dedicated thin-slice posterior fossa sequences and MR angiography — to identify the offending vessel and to exclude the secondary causes: cerebellopontine angle tumour, vestibular schwannoma, epidermoid, AVM, demyelination, and post-Bell's palsy aberrant regeneration (which produces synkinesis, not true HFS).

Treatment

  • Botulinum toxin type A is the primary intervention, FDA-approved, effective and repeatable. The clinical challenge is balancing neurological benefit against facial aesthetics and symmetry — precise technique, dose and site selection matter, and the treating clinician should think about both domains. For periocular injection, pretarsal placement was more efficacious with fewer side effects than preseptal (overall adverse events 5.2% vs 9.4%; ptosis 2.5% vs 3.3%), with no difference in duration of response. Typical adverse effects: ptosis, facial asymmetry, lagophthalmos, dry eye, bruising. Effects last 3–4 months.
  • Microvascular decompression (MVD) is the definitive, potentially curative surgery. A meta-analysis of six studies (2952 patients with vertebral-artery-associated HFS and 604 non-VA-associated) found the effective rate did not differ between the two groups (OR 1.16, p=0.42), but VA-associated cases had significantly more transient (OR 0.64, p=0.008) and permanent complications (OR 0.28, p=0.0001), including a higher rate of hearing loss (OR 0.35, p=0.0007). Non-VA-associated HFS occurs predominantly in women; VA-associated HFS is commoner in older patients and on the left. Counsel accordingly: MVD offers cure, but the risk profile depends on which vessel is responsible.
  • Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, clonazepam and baclofen give partial relief in some patients and are reasonable while awaiting definitive treatment.

Other peripherally generated movement disorders

  • Hemimasticatory spasm — unilateral involuntary contraction of the masseter/temporalis from trigeminal motor branch compression; associated with facial hemiatrophy and localised scleroderma; responds to botulinum toxin.
  • Painful legs and moving toes — continuous writhing toe movements with deep leg pain, following radiculopathy, neuropathy or trauma.
  • Post-traumatic focal myoclonus and dystonia — see §11; note the demonstrated spinal contribution to apparently peripheral myoclonus.
  • Facial synkinesis after Bell's palsy — aberrant reinnervation producing eye closure with smiling and crocodile tears; treated with botulinum toxin plus neuromuscular retraining, not with MVD.

18 · Drug-induced movement disorders

Drug-induced movement disorders (DIMDs) follow exposure to dopamine-receptor-blocking agents (DRBAs) — antipsychotics, and just as importantly the antiemetics and prokinetics that patients never think of as psychiatric drugs. They are among the few movement disorders you can prevent, and among the few you can cause.

"Extrapyramidal symptoms" is a term to abandon. An expert roundtable makes the point directly: EPS is a non-specific label covering a range of different abnormal movements, and it fosters the misconception that all DIMDs can be treated the same way — which drives the misuse and over-prescription of anticholinergics. Name the specific disorder: drug-induced parkinsonism, acute dystonia, akathisia, or tardive dyskinesia. They are neurobiologically and clinically distinct and their treatments differ.

Anticholinergics — an evidence-based reset

Summarising the roundtable evidence review:

  • Effective: drug-induced parkinsonism, and acute drug-induced dystonia.
  • Not recommended: tardive dyskinesia, akathisia, NMS.
  • Not supported for prevention, except in individuals at high risk of acute dystonia.
  • Harms: peripheral (urinary retention, constipation, dry mouth, blurred vision, glaucoma precipitation, heat intolerance) and central (impaired cognition, delirium, falls) — all especially concerning in older adults, and cumulative anticholinergic burden is associated with cognitive decline.
  • If used: lowest effective dose, shortest possible duration, and taper gradually on discontinuation (abrupt withdrawal precipitates cholinergic rebound and can unmask dystonia).

Tardive dyskinesia — scale of the problem and treatment

A European narrative review estimates that approximately 22% of patients treated with antipsychotics in Europe have TD, regardless of drug subclass, with higher rates for first- versus second-generation agents. There is no formal European consensus on diagnosis and management, so TD is regularly overlooked, misdiagnosed and undertreated. The practical remedies are unglamorous: document a baseline AIMS before starting a DRBA, repeat it at intervals, and ask the patient's family — patients frequently have no awareness of the movements.

Both approved VMAT2 inhibitors work. A comparative review of four double-blind trials (including data from an Asian population) found significant AIMS improvement with deutetrabenazine and with valbenazine, with acceptable tolerability for both. Practical differences: valbenazine is once daily (40 mg, increasing to 80 mg), deutetrabenazine twice daily with food and titrated weekly. Class cautions: depression and suicidality (screen and monitor), parkinsonism, akathisia, somnolence, QT prolongation; dose adjust for CYP2D6 poor metabolisers and for strong CYP2D6 inhibitors.

Drug-induced dystonia — which drugs, and when

A pharmacovigilance study of the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (Q1 2004 – Q3 2024) analysed 28 938 dystonia reports in 27 618 patients using four disproportionality methods:

  • Most frequently reported: metoclopramide (7178 reports) and aripiprazole (1595).
  • Strongest disproportionality signal: metoclopramide, followed by prochlorperazine and haloperidol.
  • Most events occurred within 0–30 days of starting the drug.
  • Dystonia was not listed in the package insert of eight identified drugs, including certain antiepileptics, antidepressants and antiparkinsonian agents — so absence from the label does not exclude the association.
  • The combination of aripiprazole with risperidone showed a notable interaction signal — antipsychotic polypharmacy raises the risk.
The prescribing lesson. Metoclopramide is the single biggest driver of drug-induced dystonia and tardive syndromes in general practice — often prescribed for nausea, reflux or gastroparesis, repeatedly, without review. Limit it to 5 days where possible, avoid it entirely in patients with parkinsonism, and never dispense it as a repeat prescription without a defined end date.

19 · Wilson disease, NBIA and brain calcification

Wilson disease

Autosomal recessive disease of copper transport caused by biallelic variants in ATP7B (chromosome 13q14.3, 21 exons). The gene product transports excess copper into bile; failure leads to copper accumulation in hepatocytes and subsequently in brain, cornea, kidney and other organs. More than 800 variants are reported, of which around 380 are confirmed pathogenic; H1069Q predominates in European and R778L in Asian populations. About 90–98% of patients are compound heterozygotes, and phenotype and penetrance are extremely variable — even two disease-causing variants do not guarantee demonstrable abnormal copper metabolism.

Diagnostic pitfalls

  • Caeruloplasmin is not a reliable single test. It is an acute-phase reactant (falsely normal in inflammation, pregnancy, oestrogen use) and is low in protein-losing states, malabsorption and heterozygous carriers. Interpret alongside serum copper, 24-hour urinary copper, and non-caeruloplasmin-bound copper.
  • Kayser–Fleischer rings are present in >95% of neurological Wilson disease but may be absent in purely hepatic presentation — and they require a slit lamp, not a torch.
  • Late-onset and asymptomatic cases exist. Do not use age >40 to exclude the diagnosis.
  • Use the Leipzig score (validated in adults and children) when the presentation is not classic; use the New Wilson Index for prognosis in acute liver failure, applied serially.

Presentation and imaging

Hepatic (any pattern from asymptomatic transaminitis to fulminant failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis), neurological (tremor — including the classic coarse proximal "wing-beating" tremor, dystonia, parkinsonism, ataxia, dysarthria, risus sardonicus, drooling), and psychiatric (personality change, disinhibition, depression, psychosis, deteriorating school performance — often the first manifestation and often mistaken for adolescence). MRI shows T2 hyperintensity of putamen, caudate, thalamus, midbrain and pons; the "face of the giant panda" sign in the midbrain is characteristic but not universal.

Management (AASLD 2022 practice guidance)

  • Chelation: trientine is generally preferred over penicillamine for neurological presentations, because penicillamine can precipitate an initial paradoxical neurological deterioration. Penicillamine requires pyridoxine supplementation and monitoring for proteinuria, marrow suppression and autoimmune complications.
  • Zinc (blocks intestinal copper absorption by inducing metallothionein) for maintenance, for presymptomatic patients and in pregnancy.
  • Lifelong treatment and lifelong adherence — stopping therapy can precipitate fulminant hepatic failure. Monitor 24-hour urinary copper, non-caeruloplasmin copper, LFTs, FBC and urinalysis.
  • Liver transplantation for acute liver failure and for decompensated cirrhosis unresponsive to medical therapy.
  • Screen all first-degree relatives. If the proband's variants are known, use targeted genetic testing as the primary screen; otherwise history, examination for KF rings, LFTs, caeruloplasmin, serum and urinary copper.

Neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA)

A heterogeneous group of mostly recessive progressive disorders with iron deposition in the basal ganglia and brainstem. The commonest subtypes by registry data and expert opinion are PKAN (pantothenate kinase-associated, PANK2 — "eye-of-the-tiger" sign), BPAN (beta-propeller protein-associated, WDR45, X-linked de novo, with a biphasic course of childhood developmental delay then adolescent/adult parkinsonism-dystonia), PLAN (phospholipase A2-associated, PLA2G6) and MPAN (mitochondrial membrane protein-associated, C19orf12).

Clinically: dystonia (often oromandibular with tongue-biting), parkinsonism, chorea, spasticity, speech and cognitive deficits, and ocular abnormalities (retinopathy, optic atrophy). Seizures are common in specific subtypes — a systematic review of 305 reported cases found seizures in 49.2% overall, with PLAN (50.8%) and BPAN prominently affected, while only around 10% of PKAN patients (the commonest NBIA) have seizures. Treatment remains symptomatic, though several mechanism-directed compounds are in development alongside general iron-directed approaches; recognise the phenotype, image with SWI/GRE, and refer for genetic confirmation and trial access.

Primary familial brain calcification (PFBC)

Bilateral basal-ganglia calcification without a metabolic cause (always exclude hypoparathyroidism, pseudohypoparathyroidism and other calcium disorders first). An MDSGene systematic review of 516 individuals with confirmed genetic PFBC found:

  • Genes: SLC20A2 (61%), XPR1 (16%), MYORG biallelic (13%), PDGFB (12%), PDGFRB (5%), JAM2 biallelic (2%).
  • Nearly one-third of mutation carriers were clinically unaffected; penetrance was lowest for PDGFRB (54% unaffected) and for SLC20A2 and XPR1 (<70% penetrance), and highest (>85%) for the remaining genes.
  • Among 349 affected patients: 27% had only motor features, 31% only non-motor, and 42% both. Parkinsonism and speech disturbance were the commonest motor manifestations; cognitive deficits, headache and depression the commonest non-motor.
  • The basal ganglia were always calcified.
Counselling point: incidental basal-ganglia calcification on a CT is common and usually benign. What makes it PFBC is extensive calcification beyond the globus pallidus, a family history, and clinical features — and even then, a third of carriers never become symptomatic. Say that clearly to families before ordering cascade testing.

21 · When to refer to a neurologist or movement-disorder clinic

  • Diagnostic uncertainty about parkinsonism — PD vs Parkinson-plus vs drug-induced vs vascular vs functional.
  • Any patient under about 50 with a movement disorder — Wilson disease, genetic and autoimmune causes must be excluded properly.
  • PD with motor fluctuations or dyskinesia not controlled by oral therapy → DBS or device-aided therapy assessment. Do not wait for advanced disease (EARLYSTIM).
  • Suspected Huntington disease or any other condition where genetic testing requires formal counselling.
  • Acute or subacute progressive chorea, ataxia or myoclonus of unknown cause — the autoimmune and paraneoplastic causes have a treatment window.
  • Any focal dystonia for botulinum toxin therapy; hemifacial spasm for toxin or MVD assessment.
  • Tardive dyskinesia requiring a VMAT2 inhibitor; drug-induced parkinsonism persisting beyond 12 months.
  • Refractory essential tremor for MRgFUS or DBS assessment.
  • Suspected functional movement disorder — for confident positive diagnosis and access to FND-informed physiotherapy and psychotherapy.
  • Late-onset ataxia — for repeat-expansion testing and for surveillance of conversion to MSA-C.
  • RLS with augmentation, or refractory to α2δ ligands and iron repletion.
  • Suspected stiff-person spectrum disorder — for antibody confirmation, tumour screening and immunotherapy.
  • All the emergencies in §20.
