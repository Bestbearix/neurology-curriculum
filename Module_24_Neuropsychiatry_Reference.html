1. Framework — rule out medical mimics first

Many neurological diseases open with psychiatric symptoms — anti-NMDA-R encephalitis with psychosis, Huntington disease with mood disturbance, frontotemporal dementia with disinhibition, autoimmune lupus with depression, Wilson disease with personality change in a young patient, hyperthyroidism with mania. A psychiatric diagnosis made too quickly in a young or atypical patient can postpone treatable diagnoses by years. The general physician's job is to keep an open differential — particularly in new psychiatric presentations in adults > 40, any psychiatric presentation with neurological signs, and acute-onset psychosis or severe behavioural change without obvious psychosocial precipitant.

The single principle: a new psychiatric syndrome with any of the following demands a neurological / medical workup before settling on a primary psychiatric diagnosis — age > 40 at first presentation; atypical features (movement disorder, autonomic instability, seizures, focal neurology, cognitive decline); rapid onset; refractoriness to standard psychiatric treatment; or family history of neurological disease.

3. Autoimmune encephalitis

(See Module 11 + 12 for full detail; included here as a critical differential of new psychiatric presentation.)

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis

  • Most common autoimmune encephalitis; young women predominantly; teratoma association in 50% aged 18–45.
  • Clinical course: viral-like prodrome → psychiatric symptoms (anxiety, mood, psychosis with bizarre delusions) often within 2 weeks → seizures → movement disorder (orofacial dyskinesias, choreoathetosis) → autonomic instability → reduced consciousness → coma. Children may present with seizures first.
  • Workup: CSF anti-NMDA-R antibody (most sensitive); EEG often shows extreme delta brush in adults; MRI often normal or non-specific T2/FLAIR; pelvic ultrasound / MRI for teratoma in women, testicular ultrasound in young men.
  • Treatment: first-line IV methylprednisolone + IVIG or plasma exchange + tumour removal if present; second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide for non-responders. ICU support for autonomic instability. Recovery is slow (months) but full or near-full in ~80% with adequate treatment.

4. Catatonia

A neuropsychiatric syndrome of disordered movement, vocalisation, and response to environment. Now recognised as a syndrome that occurs in psychiatric disease (mood disorders, schizophrenia) but also in neurological / medical disease (autoimmune encephalitis, post-stroke, encephalitis, hepatic encephalopathy, hyponatraemia, drug effects, anti-NMDA-R encephalitis prominently).

Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale — common features

  • Stupor: extreme hypoactivity, immobile, minimally responsive.
  • Mutism: minimal verbal response.
  • Negativism: opposing or not responding to instructions.
  • Posturing / catalepsy: maintaining unusual postures.
  • Waxy flexibility (cerea flexibilitas): limbs maintained in passively placed posture.
  • Stereotypy: non-goal-directed repetitive movements.
  • Mannerisms: odd purposeful movements.
  • Echolalia / echopraxia: imitation of others' speech / movements.
  • Excitement (excited catatonia): extreme hyperactivity.
  • Autonomic instability: in malignant catatonia.
Catatonia management:
  • Lorazepam challenge — 1–2 mg IV/IM; if dramatic improvement within minutes to an hour → diagnostic + therapeutic. Continue scheduled lorazepam 3–24 mg/day.
  • Address underlying cause: rule out anti-NMDA-R encephalitis (especially young woman with new catatonia), other autoimmune encephalitis, metabolic, infectious, drug-induced, hepatic, hyponatraemia.
  • ECT if refractory to lorazepam or in malignant catatonia (lorazepam-refractory with autonomic instability and hyperthermia — life-threatening; mortality up to 20% untreated).
  • AVOID antipsychotics in catatonia — they can worsen catatonia and precipitate neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
  • Address comorbid neuropsychiatric disorder.

6. Huntington disease and other movement disorder neuropsychiatry

  • Huntington disease (Module 6): psychiatric symptoms can precede motor by years — irritability, depression, apathy, OCD, psychosis, suicide risk substantially increased; treat depression (SSRI), psychosis (tiapride, olanzapine for chorea + psychiatric; avoid haloperidol long-term — TD risk); cognitive impairment progressive; genetic counselling; family support.
  • Wilson disease (Module 13): psychiatric prodrome in young patient; KF rings; trientine + zinc; psychiatric symptoms often resolve with chelation.
  • Tourette syndrome and tics (Module 6): OCD + ADHD commonly comorbid; CBIT first-line; α2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine); dopamine antagonists for severe.
  • FTD (Module 7): behavioural variant disinhibition + apathy + change in eating + loss of empathy → frequently misdiagnosed as primary psychiatric.

7. Neuropsychiatry of dementia — BPSD & sundowning

Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) are present in > 90% of dementia patients at some point. Drive caregiver burden + institutionalisation + mortality.

Common BPSD

  • Depression + anxiety + agitation + aggression + psychosis + sleep disturbance + sundowning + wandering + disinhibition + apathy.

Management — non-pharmacological first

  • Identify and treat causes: pain, infection (UTI common), medication side effects, sensory deficits, dehydration, electrolytes, sleep disturbance, environmental triggers.
  • Structured daily routine; physical activity; music + reminiscence therapy; bright light + dark night for sundowning; calm low-stimulus environment; caregiver education.

Pharmacological — when necessary

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for cognition; may modestly improve BPSD; first-line in DLB.
  • SSRIs for depression / anxiety (sertraline, citalopram); citalopram has QT prolongation caution.
  • Antipsychotics for severe psychosis / agitation: brexpiprazole (FDA-approved 2023 for AD agitation); risperidone widely used outside US but boxed warning increased mortality; AVOID typical antipsychotics in elderly dementia.
  • DLB: neuroleptic sensitivity reaction — even single doses of antipsychotic can cause severe parkinsonism + autonomic dysfunction + NMS; use quetiapine or clozapine cautiously if absolutely needed (Module 7).
  • Avoid: benzodiazepines (falls, paradoxical disinhibition, delirium); anticholinergics; opioids unless necessary.
FDA black box warning for all antipsychotics in elderly with dementia-related psychosis — increased mortality (cardiovascular + infection). Use only when necessary, at lowest dose, for shortest duration; review regularly; discontinue if possible.

8. Post-stroke neuropsychiatry

  • Post-stroke depression: ~30% of stroke survivors; underdiagnosed; impairs functional recovery + increases mortality. Screen routinely. Treat with SSRI (sertraline / citalopram); CBT; address pseudobulbar affect with dextromethorphan-quinidine if disabling.
  • Post-stroke mania: rare; right hemisphere (orbitofrontal, basal temporal) lesions; treat with mood stabiliser (valproate, lithium with renal monitoring).
  • Post-stroke psychosis: rare; right hemisphere lesions especially; treat cautiously with quetiapine.
  • Post-stroke anxiety + PTSD: common after stroke event; CBT + SSRI.
  • Post-stroke fatigue: distinct from depression; very common; modafinil sometimes helpful.
  • Pseudobulbar affect: pathological laughing / crying disproportionate to mood; bilateral corticobulbar tract lesions; dextromethorphan-quinidine (Nuedexta — FDA-approved); SSRIs.
  • Cognitive impairment: post-stroke vascular cognitive impairment (Module 7); manage vascular risk; cognitive rehabilitation.

9. Neuropsychiatry of multiple sclerosis (cross-ref Module 8)

  • Depression: ~50% lifetime; suicide risk increased; underdiagnosed; treat with SSRI / SNRI + CBT.
  • Anxiety: common; often comorbid with depression.
  • Bipolar disorder: increased in MS.
  • Cognitive impairment: in ~40–60%; subcortical pattern with attention, processing speed, memory; not associated with EDSS; cognitive rehabilitation.
  • Fatigue: ~80%; distinct from depression; amantadine, modafinil, methylphenidate (mixed evidence).
  • Pseudobulbar affect: dextromethorphan-quinidine.
  • Sexual dysfunction: common; multifactorial.

10. Neuropsychiatry of epilepsy (cross-ref Module 3)

  • Depression: increased ~2× general population; underdiagnosed; SSRIs (sertraline, citalopram) are generally safe in epilepsy — bupropion + tramadol + maprotiline LOWER seizure threshold and should be avoided.
  • Anxiety: common.
  • Psychiatric AED side effects: levetiracetam (irritability, depression — 10% — "Keppra rage"); topiramate (cognitive slowing); zonisamide; phenobarbital (depression, especially in children); valproate (sedation, weight); brivaracetam may be alternative to LEV for those with mood effects.
  • Ictal psychiatric features: ictal fear, deja vu, depersonalisation, ecstatic seizures.
  • Postictal psychosis: 24–72 hours after a cluster of seizures; lucid interval; treat with brief antipsychotic + benzodiazepine; manage seizures.
  • Interictal psychosis (Schizophrenia-like): temporal lobe epilepsy.
  • SUDEP risk: counsel.
  • PNES (functional seizures) — Module 21.

11. Neuropsychiatry of traumatic brain injury

  • ~50% of TBI survivors have a psychiatric diagnosis in the first year.
  • Depression + anxiety + PTSD + sleep disturbance + irritability + impulsivity + aggression + apathy + executive dysfunction.
  • Post-concussion syndrome: cognitive + physical + emotional symptoms persisting weeks–months; address all components; CBT; gradual return to activity (avoid prolonged rest).
  • SSRI for mood; trazodone for sleep; CBT.
  • AVOID benzodiazepines (impair recovery) and typical antipsychotics where possible.
  • Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE): post-mortem diagnosis; suspected in repeated head injury (boxing, American football); progressive cognitive, behavioural, and psychiatric symptoms.

13. ECT and DBS in psychiatric and neurological disease

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)

  • Indications: severe depression (especially with psychosis, suicide risk, refractory); catatonia (after lorazepam trial); mania; NMS (refractory); status epilepticus (very refractory); PD (selected for severe depression or motor symptoms).
  • Common side effects: transient post-ictal confusion; autobiographical memory loss (usually for events around treatment period); headache; muscle soreness.
  • Contraindications (relative): recent MI (within 3 months); raised ICP; unstable cervical spine; severe COPD; pheochromocytoma untreated.
  • Anaesthesia (methohexital + succinylcholine); EEG monitoring during seizure; effective course usually 6–12 sessions, 2–3/week.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) in psychiatric disease

  • OCD: nucleus accumbens / anterior limb of internal capsule — for refractory severe OCD.
  • Major depression: investigational; subcallosal cingulate, ventral capsule targets.
  • Tourette syndrome: centromedian thalamus / GPi for severe refractory.
  • Established neurological indications: PD (STN, GPi), essential tremor (VIM), dystonia (GPi), epilepsy (centromedian thalamus, anterior nucleus).

14. Suicide risk in neurological illness

Multiple neurological conditions carry increased suicide risk: HD (very high — counsel families); ALS; MS; epilepsy; stroke (early post-stroke); TBI; PD; chronic pain. Screen routinely; safety plan; involve family; treat depression aggressively; consider ECT for severe / refractory; address access to means.

15. When to refer

  • Same day: anti-NMDA-R encephalitis suspected (CSF + treat empirically); malignant catatonia; NMS; serotonin syndrome; severe lithium toxicity; suicide risk; first-episode psychosis in a young patient with atypical features (autonomic instability, seizures, movement disorder).
  • Within 1–2 weeks: any new psychiatric presentation in older adult with atypical features (FTD vs depression vs autoimmune); suspected Wilson disease in young patient with psychiatric prodrome; established neuropsychiatric symptoms not responding to standard treatment.
  • Routine: established neuropsychiatric conditions (PD depression, post-stroke depression, MS cognitive impairment) for multidisciplinary management.
  • Always involve: psychiatry + neurology + (where relevant) immunology + endocrinology + geriatrics + clinical pharmacy + social work / family support.
