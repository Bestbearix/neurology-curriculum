1 · Defining disorders of consciousness

Consciousness has two components. Arousal (wakefulness) depends on the ascending reticular activating system — brainstem tegmentum, locus coeruleus, raphe, pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei — projecting through the thalamic intralaminar nuclei to cortex. Awareness (the content of consciousness) depends on recurrent thalamocortical activity, classically described in the gamma band, with at least fleeting working memory required for a conscious percept to exist. Disorders of consciousness affect one component, the other, or both — and the clinical states below are simply the combinations.

Use descriptive language, not labels. "Opens eyes to firm sternal pressure, localises with the right arm, no verbal output" carries information; "stuporous" does not, and different clinicians mean different things by it. Record what you did and what happened.
Always test for locked-in syndrome before concluding a patient is unresponsive. Ask directly: "Look up at the ceiling" and "Blink twice if you can hear me." Vertical eye movement and blinking may be the only voluntary responses available, and patients have been managed as comatose for days while fully aware. This single question costs nothing and is the most consequential one in this module.

2 · Initial approach to the unresponsive patient

Treat coma like trauma resuscitation: ABCs first, history and examination in parallel, and a targeted workup afterwards. The purpose of the first ten minutes is not to make a diagnosis but to prevent secondary brain injury and to reverse what is immediately reversible.

The first five minutes

  • Airway, breathing, circulation. Intubate for GCS ≤ 8, vomiting risk, hypoxia, or to control PaCO₂ in raised intracranial pressure. Avoid hypoxia and hypotension — both worsen every cause of coma on this list.
  • Vital signs. Hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration is the Cushing response and means raised ICP. Fever suggests infection, heatstroke or a toxidrome; hypothermia suggests exposure, sepsis, hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia or intoxication — and can mimic brain death.
  • Capillary glucose. Hypoglycaemia is reversible coma and irreversible injury if missed. Give thiamine before glucose in anyone at nutritional risk.
  • Naloxone 0.4 mg IV (titrated) if opioid toxicity is possible — pinpoint pupils with hypoventilation. Expect resedation with long-acting opioids.
  • Flumazenil is rarely appropriate: it risks seizures in mixed overdose and in chronic benzodiazepine users. The reflexive push has fallen out of favour.
  • Empirical antimicrobials ± aciclovir if meningoencephalitis is plausible — fever, neck stiffness, rash, immunocompromise. Do not delay them for the CT or the LP.
  • Cervical-spine precautions in any unwitnessed collapse or possible trauma until cleared.
  • Treat seizures, and consider that ongoing non-convulsive seizure activity may itself be the cause of the coma.
The modern "coma cocktail" is selective, not reflexive: oxygen · thiamine, then glucose · naloxone if opioids are plausible · cervical immobilisation if trauma is possible. Flumazenil is not part of it.

The history you must obtain from someone else

The patient cannot give it, so the history comes from family, bystanders, ambulance crew, the medication list and the old notes — and it is frequently more valuable than the scan.

  • Tempo. Sudden (vascular, seizure, trauma, cardiac) versus gradual (metabolic, infective, mass, toxic).
  • The scene. Empty bottles or blister packs, syringes, a running engine or charcoal burner (carbon monoxide), a fall, a seizure witnessed, the last time the patient was seen normal.
  • Drugs. Prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal, recreational, and access to other people's medication. Ask about recent dose changes and about missed doses (anticonvulsants, insulin, steroids, dopaminergics).
  • Background. Diabetes, liver or renal failure, malignancy, HIV, anticoagulation, psychiatric illness, alcohol use, recent surgery, pregnancy.
  • Recent course. Fever, headache, confusion, behavioural change, falls, weight loss, vomiting.
Do not accept "found confused" as a history. Telephone the family, the nursing home or the ambulance crew before they disperse. The single detail that explains the coma — a missed dialysis session, a new tramadol prescription, a charcoal stove in a closed room — is usually obtainable in one phone call and unobtainable an hour later.

3 · Investigation strategy and urgency when the advanced tests are not on site new section

Altered mental status is one of the commonest presentations in acute medicine — up to 4–10% of emergency-department complaints — and it generates more investigation than almost any other symptom. In a hospital without same-day MRI, continuous EEG, a neuroimmunology laboratory or an on-call neurologist, the question is not which test but: does this result change what I do in the next hour, today, or this week — and what do I treat empirically while I wait?

The organising principle in coma is different from elsewhere in neurology. Here the bedside examination localises and the tests confirm — but the treatable causes are mostly identified by cheap, immediate tests and by the history, not by imaging. The scan's main job is to find the lesion that needs a neurosurgeon or that contraindicates a lumbar puncture. Everything else is glucose, electrolytes, gases, drugs, cultures and an EEG.

3.2 · Neuroimaging — how urgent, and how much does it actually yield?

The yield data are sobering, and they should change your practice. In the ACR 2024 Appropriateness Criteria update for altered mental status, coma, delirium and psychosis: a meta-analysis of 25 studies covering 79 201 patients with atraumatic AMS found 94% underwent head CT but relevant abnormal findings in only 11%. Across 708 145 adult ED encounters, head CT ordered for AMS had a critical-result yield of 9.8%. Among 285 febrile elderly patients with AMS, abnormal imaging was found in 16.5% — and the predictors of an abnormal scan were lower GCS, a lateralising sign, higher systolic blood pressure and lower body temperature. The commonest neurological diagnoses were intracranial haemorrhage and ischaemic stroke.

Contrast adds little acutely. Contrast-enhanced CT as a first-line test in acute AMS does not add significant value over non-contrast CT; reserve contrast for suspected intracranial infection, tumour or inflammatory pathology, and prefer MRI with and without contrast for that question when it is available.

Know the limits of your CT. A normal non-contrast CT does not exclude: early ischaemic stroke (including basilar occlusion), encephalitis, non-convulsive status epilepticus, meningitis, hypoxic-ischaemic injury in the first hours, PRES, CVST, diffuse axonal injury, or any metabolic or toxic cause. If the CT is normal and the patient is still comatose, the useful next tests are EEG, lumbar puncture and MRI — in that order of availability in most district hospitals.
Imaging that justifies transfer today — because the finding demands an intervention you cannot deliver: a mass lesion with shift or herniation · cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct with fourth-ventricle compression (this is a neurosurgical emergency and deteriorating consciousness is the trigger) · acute hydrocephalus · large haemorrhage in an anticoagulated patient · suspected basilar occlusion within the thrombectomy window · an epidural or subdural haematoma. Telephone the receiving centre before or during the scan, not after.

3.4 · Lumbar puncture

  • Tier B whenever meningitis, encephalitis or subarachnoid haemorrhage is plausible. Give empirical antibiotics, dexamethasone and aciclovir before the LP if there will be any delay — the CSF profile remains interpretable for hours, and mortality tracks the delay to antibiotics.
  • CT before LP if there are focal signs, papilloedema, new seizures, immunocompromise or a depressed conscious level — but the CT is to exclude a mass effect, not to exclude meningitis.
  • Send it all in one tap: cell count and differential, protein, glucose with a paired plasma glucose, Gram stain and culture, and — where the question includes encephalitis — PCR for HSV and enterovirus, plus a tube saved and frozen. Write the clinical question on the request form.
  • If autoimmune encephalitis is possible, take paired serum and CSF at the same sitting and freeze a pre-treatment aliquot (§15).

3.7 · Making one referral or transfer count

  • Telephone before you transfer. For anything in tier B, a conversation with the receiving intensivist, neurologist or neurosurgeon changes the destination, the timing and the pre-transfer treatment more often than a letter does.
  • Send your results on paper — bloods, gases, ECG, CT images or report, drug chart, and the times of each. A receiving centre that cannot see them will repeat them, costing hours.
  • Document the examination that you did, with times — GCS components, pupils, brainstem reflexes, motor response, respiratory pattern, and temperature. In a patient who later becomes a prognostication or brain-death question, your early examination is irreplaceable data that nobody can reconstruct.
  • Record what you have already given, especially sedatives, paralytics, antibiotics and thiamine, with doses and times. Sedative and paralytic exposure is the commonest confounder in later neurological assessment.
  • Stabilise before moving: secure the airway, treat hypotension and hypoxia, control seizures, correct glucose, and keep the patient normothermic. Transfer is when patients deteriorate.
  • Name the question in the referral. "Unexplained coma, normal CT, needs EEG to exclude NCSE" gets a different response from "confused patient for review".
Summary in one sentence. Tier A is free and must already be done; tier B decides whether the patient travels today; tier C is where most of the remaining treatable disease is found, so send those tests now and treat empirically where it is safe; and no tier-D or tier-E investigation should ever delay a transfer, an antibiotic, an antiviral, thiamine, or a decision.

4 · The focused coma exam — four pillars

Plum and Posner's four-pillar framework separates supratentorial, infratentorial and metabolic/diffuse coma. Examine all four in every comatose patient, and record the findings with the time.

Pillar 3 — Pupils

Pupils are the single most useful sign, because the pathways for pupillary control run near — but are anatomically distinct from — those for consciousness. Metabolic coma almost always spares pupillary reactivity. The important exceptions are anticholinergic toxicity, severe anoxia, profound barbiturate intoxication, hypothermia, and neuromuscular-blocking or topical agents.

Pillar 4 — Eye movements

  • Resting position: conjugate deviation towards a destructive cortical lesion (and away from the hemiparesis); deviation away from the lesion suggests a pontine destructive lesion or a thalamic "wrong-way" haemorrhage. Deviation may also be seizure-related and will then be intermittent.
  • Roving conjugate movements mean an intact brainstem — typical of metabolic or diffuse cortical coma. They disappear with brainstem injury.
  • Ocular bobbing (brisk downward jerk, slow return) — pontine destruction. Ocular dipping (slow down, fast up) — diffuse cortical dysfunction, often anoxic.
  • Skew deviation — brainstem or cerebellar lesion.
  • Oculocephalic reflex (doll's eyes): passive head rotation. Intact = eyes move opposite to the head (brainstem intact). Absent = eyes stay fixed with the head. Do not perform if cervical injury is possible.
  • Cold caloric (oculovestibular) reflex: head at 30°, intact tympanic membrane, irrigate with 50 mL ice water and observe for up to a minute, with at least five minutes between sides. An awake patient shows nystagmus with the fast phase away from the cold ear (COWS). In coma with an intact brainstem there is tonic conjugate deviation towards the cold ear with no fast phase, because the fast phase is cortical. No response at all means brainstem dysfunction — or a drug effect.
COWS describes the awake patient. In coma there is no cortical fast phase, so you see only the tonic deviation towards the cold ear. Expecting nystagmus in a comatose patient and not seeing it is not an abnormal result.

CRS-R (Coma Recovery Scale — Revised)

The gold-standard behavioural tool for prolonged disorders of consciousness, with six subscales (auditory, visual, motor, oromotor/verbal, communication, arousal). It outperforms other behavioural scales and — importantly — remains more sensitive for detecting consciousness in routine practice than fMRI or EEG. Repeated assessments are essential: a single assessment misclassifies patients, because arousal fluctuates. See §17.

6 · Localisation — the three-bucket sort

Supratentorial structural lesion

A hemispheric lesion causes coma only once it has produced a contralateral deficit and then progressed to compress the diencephalon — the rostro-caudal deterioration of Plum and Posner: drowsy → small reactive pupils and Cheyne–Stokes → bilateral spasticity → flexor posturing → extensor posturing → mid-position fixed pupils → loss of pontine reflexes → loss of medullary reflexes and apnoea. The orderly sequence is the clue.

Infratentorial structural lesion

Sudden coma with brainstem signs from the outset — cranial-nerve palsies, abnormal pupils (pinpoint reactive = pons; mid-position fixed = midbrain), ocular bobbing, gaze palsy, crossed findings. There is no rostro-caudal sequence. Causes: brainstem haemorrhage or infarct, basilar occlusion, cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct with brainstem compression.

Diffuse, metabolic or toxic

The single most useful clue is that pupillary reactivity is preserved and the findings are symmetric. Supporting features: asterixis, multifocal myoclonus, tremor, roving eye movements, generalised seizures, and a level of consciousness that fluctuates. Tone is diffusely reduced; reflexes are symmetric.

10 · Delirium — the most common altered mental status you will see

Delirium affects up to half of stroke patients and the large majority of ICU patients. Hypoactive delirium is more common than hyperactive and far more often missed — the quiet, withdrawn patient is assumed to be tired or depressed. Delirium is not a benign accompaniment of illness: each day of delirium carries measurable excess mortality, and survivors have long-term cognitive impairment.

Diagnosis — CAM and CAM-ICU

The Confusion Assessment Method requires features 1 and 2, plus either 3 or 4:

  1. Acute onset and fluctuating course — established by asking someone who knows the patient.
  2. Inattention — cannot sustain or shift attention. Test it: months of the year backwards, days of the week backwards, serial sevens, or a letter-cancellation task. This is the cardinal feature.
  3. Disorganised thinking — rambling, illogical, tangential.
  4. Altered level of consciousness — anything other than alert.

The CAM-ICU adapts this for ventilated patients using non-verbal responses, and is paired with the RASS. A RASS of −4 or −5 means the patient is too sedated to assess — lighten sedation and reassess rather than recording "unable to assess" indefinitely.

Causes — I WATCH DEATH

Infection · Withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepine, opioid, nicotine) · Acute metabolic (electrolytes, acid–base, glucose, renal, hepatic) · Trauma · CNS pathology (stroke, seizure, haemorrhage, tumour, meningitis, encephalitis) · Hypoxia · Deficiencies (thiamine, B12, niacin) · Endocrinopathies (thyroid, adrenal, glucose) · Acute vascular · Toxins and drugs · Heavy metals. Prevention and management — what the evidence supports

A systematic meta-review searching eight databases identified 32 systematic reviews and meta-analyses covering 335 individual studies of 16 non-pharmacological interventions in ICU delirium. The interventions that significantly reduced delirium were the ABCDEF bundle and other multicomponent non-pharmacological packages, and early mobilisation. Multicomponent strategies — particularly those including early mobilisation and family participation — appeared more effective than single interventions.
  1. Treat the cause. Everything else is supportive. Search deliberately for infection, drugs, metabolic derangement, pain, retention and constipation.
  2. Deploy the multicomponent bundle — the ABCDEF bundle: Assess and manage pain · Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials · Choice of analgesia and sedation (avoid benzodiazepines) · Delirium assess, prevent and manage · Early mobility and exercise · Family engagement and empowerment.
  3. Orientation and environment: clock and calendar in view, glasses and hearing aids returned, daylight and a protected night, minimise nocturnal observations and noise, avoid ward moves, avoid restraints.
  4. Early mobilisation — one of the few interventions with consistent benefit across reviews.
  5. Family participation — not a courtesy but an intervention with evidence behind it. Bring them in, and give them a role.
  6. Pain control without opioid excess. Uncontrolled pain drives delirium; so does the opioid load used to treat it.
  7. Deprescribe: anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, sedating antihistamines, and any drug started recently that could be responsible.
  8. Drugs only for severe agitation that threatens safety — low-dose haloperidol, or an atypical antipsychotic. They shorten neither the delirium nor the admission. Avoid antipsychotics in parkinsonism and Lewy body dementia (use quetiapine or clozapine if unavoidable), and be aware of the mortality signal in elderly patients with dementia. Benzodiazepines are first-line only for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, where they are the treatment.
Four things not to miss behind a "delirium" label: non-convulsive status epilepticus · Wernicke encephalopathy · HSV encephalitis (fever, headache, behavioural change, temporal-lobe signs — start aciclovir now) · and a toxidrome (serotonin syndrome, NMS, anticholinergic). Each is treatable and each is routinely missed because the word "delirium" closes thinking down.

11 · Sepsis-associated encephalopathy new section

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is the leading cause of encephalopathy in critically ill patients — diffuse brain dysfunction caused by systemic infection without direct CNS infection. It ranges from inattention and agitation through delirium to deep coma, and it is both under-recognised and prognostically important: encephalopathy in sepsis independently predicts mortality, and survivors carry long-term cognitive impairment affecting functional ability, verbal learning and memory.

Mechanism — why the brain fails in sepsis

Inflammatory brain changes in sepsis have been recognised since the 1920s, initially attributed to microbial toxins entering the brain. Current understanding centres on:

  • Blood–brain barrier breakdown, which permits peripheral-to-central immune signalling — the pivotal step.
  • Neuroinflammation with microglial and astrocytic activation.
  • Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress.
  • Neurotransmitter imbalance, including cholinergic dysfunction.
  • Haemodynamic impairment — impaired cerebral autoregulation and hypoperfusion.

Emerging work describes biologically distinct subphenotypes — ischaemic-hypoxic, inflammatory-dominant and metabolic-degenerative — each with different molecular features and outcomes, pointing towards biomarker-driven stratification in future.

Diagnosis — a diagnosis of exclusion, with no confirmatory test

There is no evidence-based standard for diagnosing SAE and no specific radiological or biological criterion. It is diagnosed when brain dysfunction accompanies systemic infection and other causes have been excluded. This matters practically: the label must never be applied before excluding CNS infection, non-convulsive seizures, drug effect, metabolic derangement and stroke — all of which occur in septic patients and all of which are treatable.

Practical approach: assess with CAM-ICU and RASS · review sedation and renal clearance of sedatives · exclude hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypoxia, hypercapnia, hepatic and renal failure · consider CNS infection and image or tap where indicated · and obtain an EEG when the conscious level is disproportionate to the illness or does not improve as sepsis is treated.

Seizures in sepsis — commoner than recognised

Patients with SAE develop convulsive and non-convulsive seizures; the cytokine storm and systemic inflammation lower seizure threshold. Non-convulsive seizures are prevalent in SAE and are frequently misdiagnosed, because the only manifestation is a conscious level that fails to improve. Continuous EEG is indicated in principle but is not widely available, and its optimal timing and duration are undefined — so in practice, a routine EEG in the septic patient who is not waking up is the realistic and worthwhile test. Management follows the standard stepwise approach: benzodiazepine → antiseizure medication → anaesthetic agents (propofol, thiopental) for refractory status. Survivors carry an increased long-term risk of seizures.

Management

There is no specific therapy. Management is treatment of the sepsis plus prevention of secondary brain injury: early source control and appropriate antimicrobials · maintenance of cerebral perfusion and haemodynamic homeostasis · avoidance of hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia · avoidance of neurotoxic and unnecessarily sedating drugs · the delirium bundle from §10 · and early recognition and treatment of seizures.

The counselling point for families. SAE begins as a reversible complication of sepsis, but it can evolve into long-term cognitive impairment. Tell families early that confusion in sepsis is a manifestation of the illness rather than a psychiatric problem, that it usually improves as the infection is treated, and that some cognitive difficulty may persist for months — so that they are not blindsided later, and so that rehabilitation and follow-up are planned rather than improvised.

Diagnosis — by exclusion, and by finding the precipitant

Hepatic encephalopathy is a diagnosis of exclusion. On admission perform a clinical examination, blood tests and — where indicated — neuroimaging, with the explicit aim of ruling out other causes of acute brain dysfunction in a cirrhotic patient: intracranial haemorrhage (they fall, and they are coagulopathic), sepsis and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal, Wernicke encephalopathy, drug effect, and non-convulsive seizures.

Ammonia is a poor diagnostic test. A normal level does not exclude hepatic encephalopathy and a high level does not confirm it; it correlates loosely with grade and is unreliable unless the sample is free-flowing, on ice and analysed immediately. Use it to support a clinical diagnosis and to follow a trend — not to make or refute the diagnosis. Clinical examination and clinical judgement are what establish overt HE.

Find the precipitant — this is the treatment

Episodic overt HE is generally triggered by one or more precipitants, alone or in combination, and they must be actively sought: gastrointestinal bleeding · infection including SBP · constipation · dehydration and over-diuresis · electrolyte disturbance, especially hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia · renal impairment · sedatives and opioids · dietary protein load · portosystemic shunt (including TIPS) · alcohol · non-adherence to lactulose.

Treatment

  • Correct the precipitant — the single most effective intervention.
  • Non-absorbable disaccharides: lactulose, titrated to 2–3 soft bowel movements daily, orally or by nasogastric tube; enemas if the patient cannot swallow safely. Over-treatment causes dehydration and hypernatraemia and can worsen the encephalopathy.
  • Non-absorbable antibiotic: rifaximin, as an add-on for recurrence prevention and in combination for treatment.
  • Airway protection in grade III–IV; avoid sedatives, and if one is unavoidable use the shortest-acting agent at the lowest dose.
  • Nutrition — do not restrict protein. Malnutrition and sarcopenia worsen HE; provide adequate protein and consider vegetable and dairy sources.
  • Secondary prophylaxis — continue lactulose ± rifaximin after discharge; recurrence is common and preventable.
  • Primary prophylaxis is indicated in the cirrhotic patient with gastrointestinal bleeding, using a non-absorbable disaccharide or antibiotic.
  • Assess for transplantation — recurrent or persistent HE is a marker of decompensation and a transplant discussion, not just a dosing problem.
Continuity is the weak link. Anti-HE treatment must be maintained after discharge to prevent further episodes, yet this is where it most often fails — the lactulose dose is never titrated at home, or it is stopped because of diarrhoea. Explain the bowel-movement target to the patient and the family explicitly, in numbers, and review it at the first follow-up.

14 · Thiamine — a high-value strategy beyond alcohol new section

The core message. Thiamine deficiency is a low-cost, reversible cause of altered mental status that receives inconsistent attention outside alcohol-related contexts. Contemporary evidence links thiamine depletion to a broad range of medical, surgical, oncological, obstetric and critically ill populations. In these settings altered mental status may be the earliest manifestation, while the standard diagnostic tools — MRI and serum thiamine levels — frequently lack sensitivity early in the course. Delayed recognition produces persistent cognitive and neurological deficits, despite the safety, low cost and ready availability of parenteral thiamine.

Who is at risk — beyond alcohol

  • Reduced intake: malnutrition, homelessness, food insecurity, anorexia nervosa, fad or restrictive diets, prolonged fasting.
  • Reduced absorption: bariatric surgery (any type, and often years later), gastrectomy, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic diarrhoea, coeliac disease.
  • Increased losses or demand: hyperemesis gravidarum, persistent vomiting from any cause, dialysis, diuretic therapy, refeeding, sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, malignancy and chemotherapy.
  • Iatrogenic: prolonged IV fluids or parenteral nutrition without vitamin supplementation, and glucose loading before thiamine in a depleted patient.
  • Critical illness generally — a large proportion of ICU patients are thiamine-deficient.

Recognition

The classic triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia is present in only a minority. Suspect Wernicke encephalopathy on the basis of any of: confusion or reduced consciousness, ocular abnormality (nystagmus, gaze palsy, ophthalmoplegia), gait ataxia, or unexplained hypotension and hypothermia, in a patient with any risk factor above. Untreated, it progresses to Korsakoff syndrome — irreversible anterograde amnesia with confabulation.

Do not use tests to decide. Serum thiamine is rarely available, slow, and unreliable once treatment has begun; MRI is insensitive early and a normal scan does not exclude the diagnosis. The decision to treat is clinical and risk-based. Early empiric thiamine is a high-value intervention: safe, cheap, available, and capable of preventing irreversible neurological injury.

Treatment

  • Give thiamine before glucose in any at-risk patient — a glucose load in a depleted patient can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy.
  • Parenteral, not oral, when the diagnosis is suspected: oral absorption is limited and unreliable, particularly in the patients who need it.
  • Use high-dose parenteral thiamine for suspected Wernicke encephalopathy, given three times daily for several days before stepping down — follow your national or local formulary for the exact regimen, but err on the side of the higher dose and the longer course. Under-treatment is the common error.
  • Replace magnesium as well — thiamine-dependent enzymes require it, and hypomagnesaemia causes apparent thiamine resistance.
  • Continue supplementation through the period of risk (ongoing vomiting, refeeding, parenteral nutrition, continued alcohol use).
A practical rule for the ward. Any patient with unexplained altered mental status and a nutritional risk factor gets parenteral thiamine now — before the scan, before the level, and before the glucose. The cost of treating a hundred patients who did not need it is trivial; the cost of missing one is Korsakoff syndrome.

15 · Autoimmune encephalitis in acute care new section

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) is immune-mediated inflammation of the brain parenchyma presenting with seizures, altered consciousness, neuropsychiatric symptoms and movement disorders. A significant proportion develop life-threatening complications requiring ICU admission — refractory status epilepticus, severe dysautonomia, coma and respiratory failure. It belongs in this module because it is one of the few causes of subacute coma that is fully treatable and routinely diagnosed late.

When to suspect it

  • Subacute onset (days to weeks, less than three months) of working-memory deficit, altered mental status or psychiatric symptoms.
  • New focal CNS findings, new seizures not explained by a known disorder, CSF pleocytosis, or MRI features suggesting encephalitis.
  • A young patient with rapid neuropsychiatric decline — psychosis, agitation, catatonia — especially with orofacial dyskinesia, seizures and dysautonomia (the anti-NMDA receptor pattern).
  • Faciobrachial dystonic seizures with hyponatraemia (LGI1).
  • Reasonable exclusion of alternative causes — but see the rule below about not waiting.

Investigation

  • Paired serum and CSF antibodies, taken at the same sitting — some antibodies are detected only in CSF, and a serum-only sample is a common and costly error. Freeze a pre-treatment aliquot if your laboratory can, because samples taken after steroids or IVIg may be uninterpretable.
  • CSF: cells, protein, oligoclonal bands, and viral PCR to exclude infection.
  • MRI brain with contrast; EEG (which may show extreme delta brush in anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, or non-convulsive seizures).
  • Tumour search — ovarian teratoma in anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, small-cell lung cancer with several intracellular antibodies. Search on clinical suspicion, not on the antibody result.
Antibody-negative autoimmune encephalitis poses considerable diagnostic difficulty — and it is real. Diagnosis relies on the combination of clinical criteria, autoantibodies in serum and CSF, neuroimaging and EEG. A negative panel does not close the case. Management centres on prompt initiation of first-line immunotherapy — corticosteroids, plasma exchange and IVIg — with escalation to second-line or emerging targeted therapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, IL-6 inhibitors, proteasome inhibitors) in refractory cases, alongside aggressive supportive care. Prognosis is influenced by the timeliness of treatment. Start on clinical grounds.

Managing the complications

These patients occupy critical care for weeks. Anticipate: refractory status epilepticus requiring anaesthetic agents; severe dysautonomia with blood-pressure and heart-rate swings, and central hypoventilation; profound agitation needing sedation that then obscures the neurological assessment; prolonged ventilation and its complications; and a long recovery with residual cognitive and psychiatric sequelae. Recovery can be slow and late — do not equate a prolonged ICU course with a poor prognosis in this condition.

16 · After cardiac arrest — hypoxic-ischaemic injury, temperature, and prognostication new section

Cardiac arrest is one of the leading causes of death, and only a small minority of those resuscitated survive with good neurological outcome. Crucially, most deaths after cardiac arrest follow active withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy on the basis that neurological recovery is judged impossible — which makes the accuracy of prognostication a matter of life and death in a direct and uncomfortable way.

Preventing secondary brain injury

The measures that matter are unglamorous and available anywhere: maintain normoxia (avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia), normocapnia, adequate mean arterial pressure and cerebral perfusion, normoglycaemia, seizure control, and normothermia with strict fever avoidance. Seizures and myoclonic status after arrest are common, should be treated, and must not by themselves be read as a death sentence (see below).

Imaging patterns of hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy

CT is the usual first study in adults because it rapidly excludes conditions needing urgent intervention — haemorrhage, oedema, infarct — but it is insensitive early; loss of grey–white differentiation is a late and ominous sign. MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging is far more sensitive, and DWI change precedes FLAIR change. In adults the pattern that matters is combined cortical and deep grey-matter involvement, particularly basal ganglia and thalami — characteristic of a severe insult and correlated with poor neurological outcome. Less profound insults produce watershed-territory injury. In neonates the patterns differ by maturation, and loss of the normally T1-hyperintense posterior limb of the internal capsule is an early sign of severe injury strongly associated with adverse motor outcome.

Prognostication — multimodal, delayed, and never on one finding

Delayed post-hypoxic leukoencephalopathy (Grinker's myelinopathy)

A rare but important syndrome that manifests days to weeks after a hypoxic event, following apparent recovery. A systematic review of 73 cases found the primary causes were benzodiazepine overdose, opioid overdose, polysubstance overdose and carbon monoxide poisoning, with common comorbidities of schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, hypertension and substance use disorder. Symptoms: decreased consciousness, psychomotor agitation, cognitive decline, parkinsonism and encephalopathy. Imaging shows diffuse white-matter T2 hyperintensity, sometimes involving basal ganglia and globus pallidus; MR spectroscopy shows reduced NAA and elevated choline. Treatment is largely supportive — amantadine, antioxidants and steroids have been used, hyperbaric oxygen may benefit carbon monoxide cases, and parkinsonism has been treated with levodopa. Most patients recovered substantially over months, though many had residual neurocognitive deficits.

Why this matters at the district hospital: a patient who deteriorates one to three weeks after an overdose or a carbon monoxide exposure has not necessarily relapsed, had a stroke, or developed a psychiatric illness. Recognising the syndrome changes the investigation, prevents inappropriate treatment, and — most importantly — allows you to tell the family that substantial recovery over months is the usual course.

Assessment

  • The CRS-R is the gold standard and outperforms other behavioural scales. Crucially, expert opinion holds that the CRS-R remains a more sensitive test for detecting consciousness than fMRI or EEG, with far more evidence supporting its routine use — advanced techniques supplement the bedside assessment, they do not replace it.
  • Assess repeatedly. Arousal fluctuates; a single assessment misclassifies. Assess at different times of day, after ensuring the patient is not over-sedated, in pain, seizing, or in an unfavourable posture.
  • Optimise the conditions first: remove sedating drugs, treat pain, infection, hydrocephalus, spasticity and seizures, correct sensory deficits, and position the patient upright.

Treatment

  • Amantadine — recommended by the AAN/ACRM/NIDILRR guideline for traumatic disorders of consciousness (typically 100–200 mg twice daily) to accelerate functional recovery. It is cheap and available, and is the single most actionable item in this section.
  • Vagal nerve stimulation — an individual-patient-data meta-analysis of 10 studies and 112 patients found VNS associated with a mean CRS-R increase of 2.78 points (95% CI 1.62–3.94), with 40.2% exceeding the minimal clinically important difference of 3 points. Patients in MCS improved more than those in coma or UWS, and greater improvement was suggested in shorter-duration or less severe DoC. Risk of bias was moderate — this is early evidence, not established practice.
  • The rest is the fundamentals: preventing and treating complications (infection, hydrocephalus, spasticity, contractures, pressure injury, heterotopic ossification, seizures), nutrition, rehabilitation, and structured family communication.
  • Avoid sedating drugs, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics and typical antipsychotics, all of which depress the very responses being assessed.
The ethical weight of the diagnosis. The label attached to these patients drives decisions about life-sustaining therapy. Given that up to 40% of behaviourally unresponsive patients have covert awareness, and that a single assessment misclassifies, the obligations are concrete: assess repeatedly with a structured tool, optimise conditions before concluding anything, involve more than one assessor, document the evidence for the diagnosis rather than the label alone, and be explicit with families about uncertainty and about the time course of recovery — which in traumatic injury can extend over months to years.

18 · Brain death — the 2023 consensus guideline

Brain death, or death by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC), is the permanent loss of function of the brain as a whole, including the brainstem, producing coma, brainstem areflexia and apnoea in the setting of an adequate stimulus. It is a clinical diagnosis, and it is legally equivalent to death.

What changed in 2023. The AAN, with the American Academy of Pediatrics, the Child Neurology Society and the Society of Critical Care Medicine, published a single guideline covering adults and children — 85 recommendations — replacing the 2010 adult and 2011 paediatric guidelines and building on the World Brain Death Project. Its stated intent is to be conservative yet practical, and to emphasise the circumstances in which determination should be delayed or deferred, so as to minimise the risk of a false-positive determination. This matters because studies of US hospital policies found wide variability and incomplete concordance with national guidance, improving over time but still incomplete — and because the credibility of the entire practice depends on there being no false positives.

Examination — all findings must be absent

  • Coma with no eye opening or motor response to noxious stimulation in all four limbs and cranially.
  • Pupils mid-position or dilated and unreactive to bright light.
  • Corneal reflexes absent.
  • Oculocephalic reflex absent — only after the cervical spine is cleared.
  • Oculovestibular (cold caloric) absent — 50 mL ice water per ear with the head at 30°, observing for at least a minute, with an interval between sides.
  • Facial movement absent to supraorbital or temporomandibular pressure.
  • Gag and cough reflexes absent, the cough tested with deep tracheal suction.
  • Number of examinations: a minimum of one in adults — though a second independent examination may reduce the risk of a false positive from diagnostic error — and two in children.
  • Examiners must be appropriately credentialled, adequately trained and competent, in accordance with local law and institutional standards; the guideline also addresses when advanced practice providers may perform the evaluation.
Spinal reflexes do not invalidate brain death. Triple flexion, the Lazarus sign, undulating toe flexion, and complex spontaneous movements of spinal origin can all occur. So can false triggering of the ventilator. Know this before you are at the bedside with the family watching, and explain it to them in advance.

Apnoea test

  • Prerequisites: haemodynamically stable, not hypoxaemic, hypotensive or hypovolaemic.
  • Contraindications / reasons to use an ancillary test instead: high risk of cardiopulmonary decompensation (high oxygen requirement or ventilator settings), prior evidence of CO₂ retention (severe obesity, COPD), high cervical spinal injury.
  • Technique: arterial catheter wherever possible · ensure PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg (or the chronic baseline in known hypercapnia) and arterial pH 7.35–7.45 · preoxygenate at least 10 minutes with 100% oxygen to PaO₂ >200 mmHg · then provide apnoeic oxygenation by one of: an insufflation catheter less than 70% of the tube's diameter placed just above the carina delivering 100% oxygen at 4–6 L/min (adults); CPAP with 100% oxygen on the ventilator; or a flow-inflating bag with a functioning PEEP valve. On ECMO, titrate exogenous CO₂ into the circuit or reduce sweep gas flow to 0.2–1 L/min.
  • Interpretation: no respiratory effort with PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg, or ≥20 mmHg above baseline (targeting the chronic baseline in known CO₂ retainers).
  • Abort for hypotension, significant desaturation or arrhythmia, and proceed to an ancillary test.

Ancillary testing

Ancillary tests are used only when the clinical examination or apnoea test cannot be completed or is confounded — not to shorten the process and not to substitute for clinical assessment. Options: cerebral blood-flow studies (digital subtraction angiography, radionuclide flow, CT angiography where validated locally) and EEG demonstrating electrocerebral silence. The 2023 guideline gives specific guidance in the settings clinicians find hardest — after treatment of raised ICP, in primary infratentorial injury, on TTM, on ECMO, and in pregnancy.

Communication and process

  • There is no obligation to obtain consent to initiate a BD/DNC evaluation, though communication with the family before and during the process is essential and reduces conflict.
  • Hospital policies should include guidance on managing disagreements with families.
  • Time of death is the time the final ABG of a positive apnoea test is drawn, or the time the ancillary test is completed.
  • Notify the organ procurement organisation according to local law before discussions about withdrawal.
  • Check your own hospital's written policy against the current guideline — the published variability is precisely in institutional policies, and the fix is local.
The historical point worth knowing. The apparent transatlantic divide — the UK's brainstem-death formulation versus the US whole-brain formulation — dates to a 1980 BBC programme and the controversy that followed it, and generated years of dispute about whether patients declared dead by UK criteria could recover. In the event, no adult has been reported to recover neurological function after a correctly conducted determination. The lasting lesson is not about the criteria but about the fragility of public trust, and why conservatism, standardisation and transparent communication are built into the modern guideline.

19 · Red flags and when to refer

Emergency referral or transfer, every time:
  • Coma of unknown cause — needs ICU/HDU-level care.
  • Any acute unilateral fixed dilated pupil — uncal herniation until proven otherwise.
  • Cushing response (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration) — impending herniation.
  • Cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct with any deterioration in consciousness — neurosurgery now; outcome tracks the speed of decompression.
  • Acute hydrocephalus, or a mass lesion with shift.
  • Suspected basilar occlusion — sudden coma, crossed signs, locked-in features — within the intervention window.
  • Suspected meningoencephalitis — give antibiotics, dexamethasone and aciclovir before transfer.
  • Suspected non-convulsive status epilepticus — for EEG.
  • Rapid neuropsychiatric decline in a young patient — possible autoimmune encephalitis.
  • Post-cardiac-arrest coma requiring temperature management and multimodal prognostication.
  • Deteriorating consciousness in acute liver failure — transplant assessment, and cerebral oedema risk.

Ten reversible causes never to miss

  1. Hypoglycaemia — capillary glucose in the first minute.
  2. Wernicke encephalopathy — thiamine before glucose, on risk not on history.
  3. Opioid toxicity — pinpoint pupils, hypoventilation; naloxone.
  4. Non-convulsive status epilepticus — EEG, or a monitored benzodiazepine trial.
  5. CNS infection — antibiotics, dexamethasone and aciclovir within the hour.
  6. Raised ICP with herniation — osmotherapy and an urgent surgical conversation.
  7. Hyponatraemia or other severe electrolyte disturbance.
  8. Hypercapnia and hypoxia — a blood gas, not a scan.
  9. Endocrine crisis — myxoedema coma, adrenal crisis; hydrocortisone first.
  10. Carbon monoxide — high-flow oxygen; pulse oximetry reads falsely normal.

Brain death — core checklist (2023 guideline)

Prerequisites: catastrophic permanent injury with an identified mechanism known to cause BD/DNC · adequate observation period (≥24 h after hypoxic-ischaemic injury in patients ≥24 months) · neuroimaging consistent with the mechanism and severity · SBP ≥100 and MAP ≥75 · normothermia · no drug effect (pentobarbital <5 µg/mL) · no neuromuscular blockade · no severe metabolic derangement. Examination: coma, and absent pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, oculovestibular, facial, gag, cough and motor responses — minimum one examination in adults, two in children. Apnoea test: PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg or ≥20 mmHg above baseline with no respiratory effort. Ancillary test only if the clinical examination or apnoea test cannot be completed.
