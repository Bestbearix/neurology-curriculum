1 · Big picture: defining disorders of consciousness

Consciousness has two components — arousal (wakefulness, mediated by the ascending reticular activating system) and awareness (cognitive content, mediated by cerebral cortex with thalamic input). Disorders of consciousness involve one, the other, or both.

NormalYesYes—
Lethargy / drowsyMildly reducedMostly preservedEasily aroused by voice; quickly drifts.
ObtundationReducedReducedArouses to vigorous stimulation; slow responses.
StuporMarkedly reducedMarkedly reducedArousable only by repeated noxious stimuli.
ComaAbsentAbsentUnarousable; no purposeful response. Eyes closed.
Vegetative state / unresponsive wakefulness syndromeYes (eyes open)AbsentSleep-wake cycles; no command following.

Locked-in syndromeYesYes (intact)Quadriplegia + lower CN palsies; vertical eye movement and blinking preserved. Often pontine infarction.

Use precise, descriptive language ("opens eyes to firm sternal pressure" rather than "stupor") — the labels above are loose and clinicians use them inconsistently.

Pearl: If the patient seems unresponsive but can blink to command, look up to command, or move the eyes vertically — suspect locked-in syndrome. Always ask: "blink twice if you can hear me" and "look up." This may be the only voluntary response possible.

2 · Initial approach to the unresponsive patient

Treat coma like trauma resuscitation — ABCs first, history and exam in parallel, then targeted workup.

The first 5 minutes

  1. Airway, breathing, circulation. Intubate if GCS ≤ 8, vomiting risk, hypoxia, or to control PCO₂ for raised ICP.
  2. Vital signs: BP, HR, RR, SpO₂, temperature. Hypotension → resuscitate. Hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration = Cushing's response → raised ICP.
  3. Glucose finger-stick — hypoglycaemia is reversible coma; give D50W 50 mL IV with thiamine 100 mg IV first (glucose alone in thiamine-deficient patient precipitates Wernicke encephalopathy).
  4. Naloxone 0.4 mg IV if opioid overdose possible (pinpoint pupils, hypoventilation).
  5. Flumazenil 0.2 mg IV may reverse benzodiazepine overdose but is rarely used — risk of seizures in mixed overdoses or chronic BZD users.
  6. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics ± aciclovir if meningoencephalitis suspected (fever, neck stiffness, rash, immunocompromised).
  7. Cervical-spine precautions in any unwitnessed collapse or trauma until imaging clears.
The "coma cocktail" (selective, not all four): O₂ · thiamine 100 mg IV → glucose · naloxone 0.4 mg IV if opioid suspected · cervical immobilisation if trauma possible. The reflexive flumazenil push has fallen out of favour.

Quick history from witnesses / EMS

  • Tempo: sudden (vascular, seizure) vs gradual (metabolic, infective, mass).
  • Trauma, last meal, medications, alcohol, illicit drugs.
  • Past medical history: diabetes, liver/renal failure, malignancy, psychiatric, anticoagulation.
  • Recent illness or fever.
  • Suicidal ideation, access to medications.

3 · The focused coma exam — four pillars

Plum & Posner's four-pillar framework distinguishes supratentorial, infratentorial, and metabolic/diffuse coma. Examine these four in every comatose patient:

Pillar 1 — Motor responses

ResponseMeaning
Follows commandsCortex relatively intact — not truly comatose.
Localises to noxious stimulus (reaches above clavicle)Cortical pathways largely preserved.
Withdraws from noxious stimulusCortex partially functional.
Decorticate (flexor) posturing — arms flexed/adducted, legs extendedLesion above red nucleus (cerebral hemispheres / internal capsule).
Decerebrate (extensor) posturing — arms and legs extended, pronatedLesion at midbrain / upper pons (below red nucleus).
No response (flaccid)Lower brainstem dysfunction OR spinal injury OR profound metabolic/sedative effect.
Asymmetric responsesSuggests structural lesion (hemispheric or brainstem).

Spontaneous myoclonus, seizures, fasciculations, and posturing should all be noted. Lack of motor response can also reflect locked-in syndrome, Guillain-Barré, severe neuromuscular blockade, or cervical-cord injury.

Pillar 2 — Respiratory pattern

PatternLocalization
Cheyne–Stokes (crescendo-decrescendo + apnoea)Bilateral cerebral disease or metabolic. Usually not imminently dangerous; short-cycle CSR is more ominous (posterior fossa or raised ICP).
Sustained hyperventilationMetabolic acidosis, hepatic encephalopathy, pulmonary congestion, sepsis. Rarely rostral brainstem (central neurogenic).
Apneustic (long inspiratory pause)Pontine lesion (especially infarction).
Ataxic / Biot's (irregular rate & depth)Medullary damage — may progress to apnoea.
Ondine's curse (loss of automatic breathing, voluntary preserved)Medullary lesion.

Pillar 3 — Pupils

Pupils are the single most useful sign because the pathways for pupillary control travel near, but are anatomically distinct from, those for consciousness. Metabolic coma almost always spares pupillary reactivity (key exceptions: anticholinergics, severe anoxia, severe barbiturate intoxication, hypothermia).

Pupil patternSite / cause
Small (1–2 mm), reactive, bilateralPontine lesion (pontine haemorrhage), opioids. Reactivity preserved despite size.
Mid-position (4–6 mm), fixedMidbrain lesion (loss of both sympathetic and parasympathetic input).
Unilateral fixed and dilated ("blown pupil")Uncal herniation compressing CN III — neurosurgical emergency. Also PCom aneurysm.
Bilateral fixed and dilatedBrainstem catastrophe, severe anoxia, severe barbiturate intoxication, anticholinergic toxicity. Recall: hypothermia and severe barbiturate intoxication can mimic brain death — reversible.
Pinpoint, reactive only on bright light + magnifierOpioid overdose.
Bilateral mid-dilated, equal, reactiveMetabolic encephalopathy (usually).

Pillar 4 — Eye movements

  • Resting eye position: conjugate gaze deviation toward a destructive cortical lesion (away from hemiparesis); away from the lesion suggests a pontine destructive lesion or a thalamic "wrong-way" haemorrhage.
  • Roving conjugate eye movements — intact brainstem, metabolic or diffuse cortical coma. Disappear with brainstem injury.
  • Ocular bobbing (brisk downward jerks, slow return) — pontine destruction.
  • Skew deviation — brainstem or cerebellar lesion.
  • Oculocephalic reflex ("doll's eyes") — passive head rotation. Intact: eyes move opposite to head turn (brainstem intact). Absent: eyes stay fixed with the head (brainstem dysfunction or deep metabolic coma). Do not perform if cervical injury possible.
  • Cold caloric (oculovestibular) reflex — head at 30°, irrigate ear canal with 30–60 mL ice water. Awake patient: nystagmus with fast phase away from cold ear (COWS — cold opposite, warm same). Comatose patient with intact brainstem: tonic conjugate deviation toward the cold ear, no fast phase. No response: brainstem dysfunction.
Mnemonic: "COWS" describes the fast-phase nystagmus of an awake person. In coma there is no cortical fast phase — you see only the tonic deviation toward the cold ear.

5 · Localization — the three-bucket sort

Supratentorial lesion

Hemispheric lesion (stroke, haemorrhage, tumour, abscess) typically causes coma only after it has produced contralateral hemiparesis and then progressed to compress the diencephalon (rostro-caudal deterioration). Signs progress in order: drowsy → small reactive pupils → bilateral spasticity → flexor posturing → extensor posturing → midbrain pupils → loss of pontine reflexes → loss of medullary reflexes (Plum & Posner sequence).

Infratentorial lesion

Pontine or midbrain lesion: sudden coma with focal brainstem signs from the start — cranial-nerve palsies, abnormal pupils (pinpoint reactive = pons; mid-fixed = midbrain), bobbing, gaze palsy, "crossed" findings. Rostro-caudal sequence is absent. Typical causes: brainstem haemorrhage or infarction, basilar artery occlusion, cerebellar haemorrhage with brainstem compression.

Diffuse / metabolic / toxic coma

The single most useful clue: pupillary reactivity is preserved, and findings are symmetric. Other clues: asterixis, myoclonus, tremor, generalised seizures, roving eye movements. Reflexes may be normal or reduced; tone diffusely reduced. Causes: hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, uraemia, hepatic, hypo/hyperthyroidism, hypo/hypernatraemia, hypercapnia, sepsis, drug overdose, alcohol.

Practical pearls (Merritt's): fever (sepsis, heatstroke, infection) · hypothermia (sepsis, exposure, hypothyroid, hypoglycaemia) · cherry-red skin (carbon monoxide) · uraemic frost · jaundice · myxoedema · acetone breath (DKA) · fetor hepaticus · Battle sign (basilar skull fracture) · subhyaloid haemorrhages (SAH) · Roth spots (endocarditis) · papilloedema (raised ICP) · resistance to neck flexion only (meningitis, SAH, foramen magnum herniation) · urinary/faecal incontinence (suggests unwitnessed seizure).

Investigations to order in unexplained coma

  • Stat finger-stick glucose, ABG, electrolytes (Na, K, Ca, Mg, PO₄), urea, creatinine, LFTs, ammonia, TSH, cortisol, CK, troponin, lactate.
  • CBC, coagulation, blood cultures.
  • Urine toxicology + serum alcohol, acetaminophen, salicylate, specific drug levels (lithium, phenytoin, valproate, AEDs).
  • Non-contrast CT head — first imaging. If normal, get contrast or MRI as appropriate.
  • Lumbar puncture if meningitis/encephalitis/SAH suspected (after CT excludes mass effect; do not delay empirical antibiotics).
  • EEG — if coma is unexplained or any subtle twitching is seen, non-convulsive status epilepticus must be excluded. Continuous EEG (cEEG) for at least 24–48 h if comatose.
  • Specific autoimmune panels and MRI for suspected autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA-R etc.).

8 · Delirium — the most common AMS the general physician sees

Up to 50% of stroke patients and 80% of ICU patients develop delirium. Hypoactive delirium is more common than hyperactive and far more often missed. Each day of delirium increases mortality risk ~10%.

DSM-5 / CAM core features

  1. Acute onset & fluctuating course.
  2. Inattention (can't sustain or shift attention; can't count backward from 20, name days of week in reverse).
  3. Disorganised thinking (rambling, illogical, switching subjects) OR altered level of consciousness.

The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) requires features 1 + 2 + (3 OR 4) for diagnosis. The CAM-ICU adapts it for ventilated patients.

Risk factors

PatientAcute illnessIatrogenic
Age > 65, frailty, baseline cognitive impairment, prior stroke, depression, alcohol/drug abuseSeverity of illness, hypoxia, electrolytes, sepsis, poor pain control, atrial fibrillation, anaemiaDeep sedation, sleep disruption, anticholinergics, opioids (meperidine, morphine), mechanical ventilation, steroids, benzodiazepines

Causes (use mnemonic I WATCH DEATH)

Infection · Withdrawal (alcohol, BZD, opioid) · Acute metabolic (electrolytes, acid-base, hypoglycaemia) · Trauma · CNS pathology (stroke, seizure, ICH, tumour, meningitis, encephalitis) · Hypoxia · Deficiencies (thiamine, B12, niacin) · Endocrinopathies (thyroid, adrenal, glucose) · Acute vascular · Toxins / drugs · Heavy metals.

Management

  • Treat the cause — the rest is supportive.
  • Non-pharmacological first: orient (clock, calendar, family at bedside), restore glasses/hearing aids, promote sleep-wake cycle (lights, daytime mobilisation, minimise nighttime disruption), early mobilisation, avoid restraints.
  • Pain control — uncontrolled pain is a delirium driver, but minimise opioid load.
  • Withdraw the offending drug if possible (anticholinergics, BZDs, sedatives).
  • Pharmacological — only for severe agitation that risks harm. Low-dose haloperidol 0.5–1 mg PO/IV or olanzapine / quetiapine. Avoid BZDs unless alcohol or BZD withdrawal (when they are first-line). Avoid in elderly with dementia (mortality signal).
  • Prevention is paramount — the ABCDEF bundle (Awakening trials, Breathing trials, Choice of sedation, Delirium monitoring, Early mobilisation, Family engagement) reduces ICU delirium and improves outcomes.
Don't miss: non-convulsive status epilepticus, Wernicke encephalopathy (give thiamine 200–500 mg IV TID for 3 days then daily before glucose loads in any malnourished/alcohol-use patient), HSV encephalitis (fever + altered mentation + temporal-lobe signs → treat with aciclovir + LP + MRI), serotonin syndrome / NMS / anticholinergic toxidrome.

10 · Brain death determination

Brain death = irreversible loss of all brain function, including brainstem, with apnoea, demonstrated when confounders have been excluded. It is a clinical diagnosis. Once declared, time of death is the time of completion of apnoea test or ancillary test. Brain death is legally equivalent to death.

Prerequisites (all must be met)

  • Irreversible coma with a known cause; CT/MRI explains the coma (e.g. massive haemorrhage, severe diffuse oedema).
  • No sedative drug effect — allow 5–7 × elimination half-life. Long-acting drugs to consider: phenobarbital (100 h), diazepam (40 h), amitriptyline (24 h), primidone (20 h), lorazepam (15 h). Midazolam can persist ~3 h. Therapeutic hypothermia slows clearance.
  • No residual neuromuscular blockade (4 twitches on train-of-four).
  • No severe acid-base, electrolyte, or endocrine derangement.
  • Core temperature ≥ 36 °C.
  • Systolic BP ≥ 100 mmHg.
  • No spontaneous respirations.

Clinical examination (all must be absent)

  1. Pupils: mid-position (4–6 mm), non-reactive to bright light.
  2. Corneal reflexes: absent.
  3. Oculocephalic ("doll's eyes"): absent — only after cervical spine is cleared.
  4. Oculovestibular (cold caloric): absent — 50 mL ice water in each ear, observe 2 min.
  5. Facial movement to supraorbital pressure or TMJ compression: absent.
  6. Gag reflex: absent (gloved finger to posterior pharynx).
  7. Cough reflex: absent (deep tracheal suction × 2 passes).
  8. Motor response to noxious stimuli in all 4 limbs: absent (spinal reflexes such as triple flexion may persist and do not invalidate brain death).

Apnoea test

  1. Patient hemodynamically stable, SBP ≥ 100 mmHg.
  2. Pre-oxygenate with 100% O₂ for 10 min (target PaO₂ ≥ 200 mmHg).
  3. Adjust ventilator to normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg) and draw baseline ABG.
  4. Disconnect ventilator; provide O₂ via insufflation catheter at level of carina (6 L/min) or T-piece with CPAP.
  5. Observe for spontaneous respirations for 8–10 min.
  6. Repeat ABG at 8–10 min, reconnect ventilator.
  7. Positive apnoea test: PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg or rise of ≥ 20 mmHg above baseline, with no observed breathing → confirms apnoea.
  8. If unstable (drop in SBP, severe desaturation, arrhythmia) → abort and proceed to an ancillary test.

Ancillary tests (only if apnoea cannot be performed)

  • EEG — ≥ 30 min of electrocerebral silence with increased gain.
  • Digital subtraction or CT angiography — absence of intracranial flow above proximal ICA.
  • Transcranial Doppler, nuclear cerebral blood flow study.

Ancillary tests are used in < 5% of brain-death determinations and should not replace clinical assessment.

Once brain death is declared — document time of final ABG or ancillary test as time of death. Notify family. Notify the organ procurement organisation before withdrawal discussions (in most jurisdictions). Organ donation occurs in ~70% of declared brain-death cases.

11 · Red flags & when to refer

Always emergency referral:
  • Coma of unknown cause — admit to ICU/HDU.
  • Any acute unilateral fixed dilated pupil (uncal herniation).
  • Cushing's triad (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration) — impending herniation.
  • Cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct with any clinical change — neurosurgery now.
  • Suspected meningoencephalitis — antibiotics + aciclovir empirically before LP.
  • Non-convulsive status epilepticus suspected (subtle twitching, unexplained obtundation) — cEEG.
  • Possible autoimmune encephalitis in a young patient with rapid neuropsychiatric decline.

Brain death core checklist (Merritt's Table 20.1)

Prerequisites met → coma with known cause + imaging consistent + no sedatives + no NMB + normal electrolytes + temp ≥ 36 °C + SBP ≥ 100.
