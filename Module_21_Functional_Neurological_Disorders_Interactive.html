Why this module?

FND is one of the commonest reasons for neurology consultation — ~16% of new outpatient referrals — and one of the most poorly handled in routine practice. The shift from "diagnosis of exclusion" to "rule-in diagnosis based on positive bedside signs" has transformed the field. Done well, the consultation itself is the start of treatment. Done badly, it's the start of years of disability and iatrogenic harm.

After this module you will:

  • Make a positive rule-in diagnosis of FND from bedside signs — not by exclusion.
  • Perform and interpret Hoover's sign, tremor entrainment, and the supine vs sitting leg-lift test.
  • Differentiate PNES from epileptic seizures using semiology and know when video EEG is needed.
  • Communicate the diagnosis using the Stone framework (name it + explain it + show the sign + treatment plan with hope).
  • Route patients into FND-specific physiotherapy and CBT (CODES trial).
  • Recognise when FND coexists with other neurological disease (~10–15% of PNES patients have epilepsy too; functional movements coexist with PD).
  • Avoid the dismissive phrases ("non-organic", "psychogenic", "medically unexplained") and the diagnostic traps (over-investigation, splints, opioid escalation).
The single principle: diagnose FND by what you see on examination, not by what you cannot see on imaging. Look for inconsistency, incongruity, and reversibility with distraction. Then communicate the diagnosis with confidence, demonstrating the positive sign to the patient.

Lessons

1. Paradigm shift — rule-in diagnosis

FND was historically diagnosed by exclusion. The modern approach (DSM-5 2013; ICD-11 2022) makes it a rule-in diagnosis based on positive bedside features that are inconsistent and incongruent with neurological disease.

  • Hoover's sign, tremor entrainment, ictal eye closure — more specific than imaging or EEG for many FND subtypes.
  • FND is not a diagnosis of psychological cause; the requirement of an identified psychological stressor was removed in DSM-5.
  • FND coexists with neurological disease (~10–15% of PNES have coexisting epileptic seizures; functional movements with PD; FND post-stroke).
  • FND is treatable with FND-specific physiotherapy + CBT + multidisciplinary care.
2. DSM-5 / ICD-11 criteria

DSM-5 Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (Conversion Disorder):

  1. One or more symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function.
  2. Clinical findings provide evidence of incompatibility between the symptom and recognised neurological or medical conditions.
  3. Not better explained by another condition.
  4. Clinically significant distress or impairment.

ICD-11: "Dissociative neurological symptom disorder" with sub-types (motor weakness, movement disorder, non-epileptic seizures, sensory, speech, vision, cognitive, mixed). Criteria align with DSM-5.

3. Predictive processing model (Edwards-Stone)
  • The brain generates predictions about expected sensory and motor signals.
  • In FND, a strongly held prior ("my leg will not move") is weighted more than incoming sensory evidence (the actual fact that the limb can move).
  • Attention focused on the symptom amplifies the aberrant prediction; distraction weakens it — explains why distraction reverses functional signs.
  • Predisposing + precipitating + perpetuating factors shape the picture.
  • Imaging shows real abnormalities in functional connectivity. FND has neurobiological correlates — it is NOT imaginary nor feigned.

5. Functional limb weakness — rule-in signs

Hoover's sign (the workhorse):

  1. Patient supine. Place hand under the weak leg's heel; ask them to push down — note effort.
  2. Place hand under the unaffected leg's heel; ask them to lift the unaffected leg.
  3. Feel the involuntary downward pressure of the weak heel into your hand (reflex hip extension during contralateral hip flexion).

True pyramidal weakness: weak heel does not push down. Functional weakness: weak heel pushes down strongly during contralateral flexion. Sensitivity ~63%, specificity ~100% (Stone JNNP 2010).

Other signs: hip abductor (Sonoo); drag (monoplegic) gait without circumduction; give-way weakness; inconsistent power; trunk-thigh sign; Spinal Injuries Center sign (supine leg won't lift; sitting up it lifts effortlessly).

6. Functional movement disorders
  • Functional tremor: variable frequency / amplitude; tremor entrainment with the unaffected hand (tap 1 Hz then 3 Hz — tremor entrains, becomes disorganised, or stops); ballistic movement task pauses tremor; whack-a-mole sign (migrates when suppressed).
  • Functional dystonia: fixed posture from onset (genuine dystonia is mobile and task-specific); fixed flexion fingers or plantar flexion+inversion foot; onset after minor injury / surgery; pain common.
  • Functional jerks / myoclonus: variable; entrainable; distractible; stimulus-precipitated; Bereitschaftspotential pre-jerk on back-averaged EEG (specialist centres).
  • Functional gait: astasia-abasia (dramatic veering, near-falls almost always caught); knee buckling without falls; scissors with crossing legs; skating / sliding without foot lift; slow exaggerated steps with effort signs.

Gold standard diagnosis: video EEG during a typical event. ILAE criteria (LaFrance Epilepsia 2013): possible / probable / clinically established / documented. Ictal eye closure is the most specific single bedside feature (PPV > 95%). Coexisting epilepsy in 10–15% — comorbidity does not exclude either diagnosis.
8. Communicating the diagnosis (Stone style)
  1. Name it: "You have a functional neurological disorder. We call it FND. It's a recognised medical condition."
  2. Explain mechanism: "Your hardware (nerves and muscles) is intact. The software (how the brain runs the movement) is stuck in a faulty pattern. We see this often and can help."
  3. Show the rule-in sign: demonstrate Hoover's sign or tremor entrainment.
  4. Differentiate from feigning: "I'm not saying you're making this up. It's not under your conscious control."
  5. Explain normal investigations: "MRI is normal because the problem is in how the brain uses normal hardware, not damage to the hardware."
  6. Treatment plan with hope: "FND-specific physiotherapy and CBT can substantially improve symptoms in most people."
  7. Resource: "Many people find neurosymptoms.org helpful."
Avoid: "non-organic", "psychogenic", "hysterical", "medically unexplained", "all in your head", "it's stress". Use: "functional", "real", "treatable", "brain software not hardware".
9. Treatment — physio, CBT, multidisciplinary

FND-specific physiotherapy (Nielsen et al. JNNP 2015 consensus):

  • Education using the predictive-processing model.
  • Distract-attention movement strategies (reciprocal limb movements, rhythmic activities).
  • Encourage automatic over effortful movement.
  • Graded return to normal activity; reduce avoidance.
  • Avoid splints, wheelchairs, walking aids beyond minimum needed for safety.

CBT for PNES (CODES trial Lancet Psychiatry 2020): 12-session CBT did not reduce seizure frequency vs standardised medical care but improved quality of life, somatic symptoms, and overall functioning. Many programmes still use CBT for wider benefits.

Multidisciplinary FND clinics (UK, US, Netherlands, Australia) show better outcomes than fragmented care. Coordinate neurology + physiotherapy + OT + SLT + psychology + psychiatry + GP.

Treat coexisting depression / anxiety / PTSD with SSRI/SNRI; sleep with CBT-I (Module 14); pain with standard chronic-pain approaches avoiding opioids and benzos (Module 17). Avoid AEDs for PNES; avoid dopaminergics for functional dystonia.

10. Prognosis, red flags, FND vs malingering
  • Outcomes: third improve / third stable / third worsen — depends on duration + comorbidity + treatment quality.
  • Better prognosis: short duration, early correct diagnosis, fewer comorbidities, engagement with treatment, agreement with diagnosis.
  • Worse prognosis: long duration, multiple conflicting opinions, ongoing litigation / benefits, severe psychiatric comorbidity, lack of buy-in.
Red flags to re-evaluate FND diagnosis: new focal signs; findings following recognisable neurological patterns; coexistence with progressive symptoms; weight loss / fever / autonomic features inconsistent with picture. FND can coexist with neurological disease — re-evaluate if clinical picture evolves.

11. When to refer
  • Same day: presentations that overlap with stroke / status epilepticus / spinal cord emergency — never diagnose FND until those are excluded; the emergency workup proceeds.
  • Within 1–2 weeks: new FND diagnosis for FND-specific physiotherapy and / or CBT; PNES for video EEG monitoring confirmation if uncertain; complex cases for multidisciplinary FND clinic.
  • Routine: established FND with stable symptoms; comorbidity management.
  • Always involve: neurology + FND-specific physiotherapy + psychology / CBT + GP + (where relevant) psychiatry, OT, speech therapy.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 34-year-old woman has 3 months of left leg weakness after a minor twisting injury, with give-way weakness and a "dragging" gait. MRI brain and spine normal. NCS normal

Bedside: Hoover's sign positive — the "weak" left heel pushes down strongly when she lifts her right leg against resistance. Drag gait without circumduction. Power varies with attention.

Diagnosis: Functional limb weakness (left leg).

Plan:

  1. Communicate the diagnosis using the Stone framework: name it ("This is FND, a recognised medical condition"); explain the mechanism ("Your hardware is intact — your brain's software is stuck"); show the positive Hoover's sign to her; differentiate from feigning ("This is not under your conscious control"); explain why investigations were normal ("MRI is normal because the problem is in how the brain uses normal hardware"); offer treatment with hope.
  2. Refer to FND-specific physiotherapy (not standard rehab) — distract-attention strategies, encouraging automatic over effortful movement, graded return to normal activity, avoiding splints / aids beyond minimum safety needs.
  3. Address perpetuating factors: avoidance, deconditioning, learned non-use, fear of movement, sleep, mood.
  4. Patient resource: neurosymptoms.org.
  5. Follow-up at 3 months to reinforce the diagnosis and assess progress.
Case 2 — A 28-year-old presents with frequent "seizures" lasting 5–10 minutes with side-to-side head shaking, asynchronous limb thrashing, eyes tightly closed with resistance to opening, mid-event vocalisation, and no postictal confusion. Multiple AEDs have been tried with no benefit

Video EEG during a captured event: normal background, no epileptiform activity during the event.

Diagnosis: Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES / functional seizures). ILAE criteria: documented level (video EEG captures the typical event without ictal EEG abnormality).

Plan:

  1. Communicate the diagnosis sensitively — name it, explain mechanism, show the video to the patient with the EEG demonstrating no electrical seizure (this is therapeutic). Differentiate from epilepsy and from feigning.
  2. Gradual taper of anticonvulsants over weeks to months (after confirming PNES does not coexist with epilepsy — 10–15% of PNES patients have both).
  3. Refer to CBT for PNES (12 sessions per CODES trial — may not reduce seizure frequency directly but improves quality of life, somatic symptoms, and overall functioning).
  4. Address comorbid anxiety, depression, PTSD, trauma (very common in PNES).
  5. Educate family / carer about the diagnosis to reduce reinforcement of the sick role; avoid emergency calls and ED attendance for typical events; safety planning for genuine emergencies.
  6. Treat coexisting epilepsy if confirmed (some patients have both).
  7. Multidisciplinary FND clinic if available.
Case 3 — A 42-year-old man has had a 2-year right hand tremor that is "always there" — at rest, posture, and action, with variable amplitude and frequency. Worse when he focuses on it

Bedside: tremor frequency 4–6 Hz with marked variability. When asked to tap a 3 Hz rhythm with the left hand, the right-hand tremor entrains to 3 Hz. When asked to perform a ballistic movement with the left hand, the right tremor pauses. When the right hand is held still, the tremor migrates to the right shoulder.

Diagnosis: Functional tremor.

Plan:

  1. Communicate the diagnosis using the Stone framework and demonstrate tremor entrainment and the whack-a-mole sign to the patient.
  2. Reassure about normal investigations and explain why brain imaging will be normal.
  3. Refer to FND-specific physiotherapy — distract-attention strategies (rhythmic activities with the unaffected hand, dual tasking).
  4. Avoid anti-tremor medication trials (propranolol, primidone, gabapentin) — they will not help and add side effects + reinforce the wrong illness model.
  5. Discuss perpetuating factors: somatic attention, deconditioning, anxiety about the tremor.
  6. Patient resource: neurosymptoms.org.
Case 4 — A 55-year-old woman with Parkinson disease (Hoehn-Yahr 2, on levodopa) has developed a 6-month history of fixed left arm flexion at the wrist and fingers, with pain, that started after a minor wrist sprain. The dystonia is not seen on action and does not respond to levodopa adjustment

Bedside: fixed dystonic posture from onset (not action-induced; no geste antagoniste); painful; precipitated by minor injury; inconsistent with the slowly progressive PD-related dystonia she might have developed.

Diagnosis: Functional dystonia coexisting with Parkinson disease.

Plan:

  1. Acknowledge BOTH conditions — PD does not exclude FND; FND coexists with neurological disease commonly.
  2. Communicate the functional dystonia diagnosis sensitively (this is often harder when the patient has a known neurological diagnosis); explain that the wrist dystonia has features that are inconsistent with typical PD dystonia.
  3. Refer to FND-specific physiotherapy + occupational therapy; avoid splints which reinforce the disorder.
  4. Continue PD-specific care (Module 6); do not adjust dopaminergic dose for the dystonia — it will not respond.
  5. Consider botulinum toxin trial only after FND physiotherapy + multidisciplinary input have been adequate.
  6. Address pain (often co-existing) with standard chronic-pain approaches (Module 17); avoid opioids.
  7. Multidisciplinary FND clinic ideally.
Case 5 — A 38-year-old has 6 months of "complete blindness" in the right eye and tubular fields in the left. Pupils react normally to light; full optokinetic response when a striped drum is rotated in front of him

Examination: normal pupillary light reflexes bilaterally including the right eye despite reported "no light perception". OKN drum response present. Visual fields show tubular fields that do not expand with distance — biologically implausible (true tunnel vision expands with viewing distance because of the cone of the visual axis). Fundoscopy normal. OCT normal.

Diagnosis: Functional visual loss.

Plan:

  1. Sensitive communication of the diagnosis using the FND framework; emphasise the rule-in features (preserved pupil reactions despite reported "no light perception" + intact OKN response + tubular fields).
  2. Refer to ophthalmology + FND-specific physiotherapy / OT / vision rehabilitation.
  3. Address comorbid depression, anxiety, trauma where present.
  4. Avoid further imaging unless new features emerge.
  5. Reassure that recovery is possible with appropriate treatment.
Case 6 — A 45-year-old man presents with 3 weeks of profound subjective memory complaint ("I can't remember anything") + concentration loss + "brain fog" but he gives a meticulously detailed history of the complaint over 45 minutes. MoCA 28/30. He works full-time as a project manager

No objective cognitive impairment on bedside testing; rich history-giving; high functioning. Co-existing severe anxiety (GAD-7 = 16), insomnia, and post-acute COVID-19 syndrome.

Diagnosis: Functional cognitive symptoms (with coexisting anxiety, insomnia, post-acute COVID).

Plan:

  1. Reassure that no objective dementia or progressive neurodegeneration is present (and explain why — preserved MoCA, intact history-giving, rich detail).
  2. Communicate the diagnosis sensitively — functional cognitive symptoms are real and treatable.
  3. Treat coexisting anxiety (SSRI; CBT) and insomnia (CBT-I — Module 14).
  4. Address post-acute COVID-19 — overlap with nociplastic syndromes (Module 17).
  5. Sleep hygiene; reduce caffeine; exercise as tolerated.
  6. Educate about how anxiety + sleep loss + somatic attention create "brain fog" without underlying brain damage.
  7. Surveillance for progressive cognitive decline if the picture evolves (Module 7 dementia workup).
  8. Patient resource: neurosymptoms.org.
