Why this module?

Paediatric neurology happens against active development. Disease is often expressed not as a focal sign but as delay, regression, or unexplained behaviour. The general physician's job is to recognise emergencies (neonatal HIE inside the 6-hour cooling window, infantile spasms, status epilepticus, paediatric stroke, raised ICP, suspected non-accidental injury), identify red flags on milestones, distinguish a floppy infant's central from peripheral pattern, and route children into specialist multidisciplinary care quickly enough to make a difference.

After this module you will:

  • Use milestones as a vital sign and recognise red flags by age.
  • Recognise and respond to neonatal HIE within the 6-hour therapeutic-hypothermia window.
  • Identify infantile spasms / hypsarrhythmia urgently — outcome depends on early treatment.
  • Use the Sarnat staging, GMFCS, and the floppy-infant central-vs-peripheral split fluently.
  • Apply the modern SMA gene-therapy framework (nusinersen, onasemnogene, risdiplam).
  • Recognise and treat Dravet syndrome (avoid sodium-channel blockers; reach for stiripentol/CBD/fenfluramine).
  • Triage paediatric headaches, distinguishing migraine from raised ICP / posterior-fossa tumour.
  • Recognise non-accidental head injury patterns and act on safeguarding concerns.
The single principle that prevents disasters: when a young child presents with unexplained encephalopathy, seizure, or rapid neurological decline, run a fast checklist — glucose, electrolytes, ammonia, lactate, infection, structural cause, non-accidental injury, metabolic emergency. The "treatable" diagnoses (hypoglycaemia, electrolytes, meningitis, urea-cycle defects, NAI, raised ICP) come before the rarer ones.

Lessons

1. Milestones — use them as a vital sign

Four streams: gross motor, fine motor / vision, speech / language / hearing, social / behaviour. The "rule of one" memory aid: 1 mo lifts head, 6 mo sits with support, 12 mo walks (one foot), 18 mo climbs, 2 y runs; for language: 1 word at 12 mo, 10–25 at 18 mo, phrases at 2 y, sentences at 3 y.

Primitive reflexes appear and disappear on a predictable timetable. Persistence past 6 months and absence of postural reflexes (parachute at 8–10 mo) point to CNS pathology — core features of cerebral palsy.

Always refer urgently for: regression, abnormal HC trajectory, focal neurology, parental concern. Parental concern is one of the most sensitive screening tools.
2. Neonatal HIE and therapeutic hypothermia
Sarnat stageFeaturesOutcome
I (mild)Hyperalert, normal tone, mydriasis, no seizureNormal in > 48 h
II (moderate)Lethargic, hypotonic, miosis, seizures common 24–48 h~25–50% disability
III (severe)Coma, flaccid, suppressed brainstem reflexes, multi-organ failure~75% mortality/major disability
Therapeutic hypothermia:
  • Eligibility: ≥ 36 weeks; < 6 hours of age; moderate–severe encephalopathy (Sarnat II/III); evidence of asphyxia (10-min Apgar ≤ 5, cord pH ≤ 7.0 or BD ≥ 16, resuscitation at 10 min).
  • Core temp 33.5 °C × 72 h, slow rewarming over 6–12 h.
  • NNT ~7 to prevent death or disability (CoolCap, NICHD, TOBY).
  • HELIX (2021): limited benefit in low/middle-income settings — context matters.
  • Continuous video / aEEG; MRI day 4–7 (DWI) + day 10–14; injury patterns: BGT (acute profound) worst prognosis; parasagittal (partial prolonged); PVL (preterm → spastic diplegia).
3. Neonatal seizures
  • Often subtle: lip-smacking, cycling, sustained gaze deviation, apnoea, autonomic. Many electrographic-only — continuous video EEG essential; electroclinical dissociation common.
  • Causes: HIE (50–60%), ICH, stroke, meningitis, electrolytes (glucose, Ca, Mg, Na), inborn errors (pyridoxine-dependent — PNPO, ALDH7A1; NKH; urea cycle), channelopathies (KCNQ2/3 benign familial; SCN2A).
  1. Correct electrolytes (glucose 2 mL/kg D10W; Ca gluconate; Mg; Na).
  2. Phenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV, repeat to 40 mg/kg.
  3. Second-line: levetiracetam 40–60 mg/kg IV (NEOLEV2: PB more effective acutely; LEV often preferred for safety).
  4. Third-line: phenytoin, midazolam infusion, lidocaine infusion.
  5. Refractory: pyridoxine 100 mg IV under EEG trial; pyridoxal-5-phosphate (PNPO); biotin; folinic acid.
  6. Targeted: carbamazepine / oxcarbazepine for KCNQ2 / SCN2A gain-of-function.
4. Cerebral palsy

Permanent, non-progressive disorder of movement and posture from a developmental brain insult, with associated cognitive/behavioural/seizure comorbidities. ~2/1000 live births.

Causes

  • Central: HIE, ICH, congenital malformation, congenital infection, chromosomal (T21, Prader-Willi), inborn errors (Zellweger, urea cycle, mitochondrial).
  • Peripheral: anterior horn (SMA — type 1 Werdnig-Hoffmann); nerve (CMT in infancy); NMJ (congenital myasthenic, transient neonatal MG, infantile botulism); muscle (congenital MD/LAMA2/FKRP, congenital myopathies — nemaline/central core/myotubular, myotonic dystrophy type 1 congenital).

Workup: CK, lactate, ammonia, acylcarnitines, urine organic acids, plasma amino acids, chromosomal microarray, SMN1 deletion testing immediately in hypotonia + areflexia + tongue fasciculations, EMG/NCS, muscle biopsy, MRI, trio whole-exome / genome sequencing.

SMA newborn screening is now standard in many countries. Pre-symptomatic treatment with nusinersen (intrathecal), onasemnogene abeparvovec (IV gene therapy 2019), or risdiplam (oral 2020) dramatically improves outcomes — milestones not previously possible.
7. Paediatric stroke
  • ~2–13 per 100,000 children/year; neonates highest rate (perinatal arterial ischaemic stroke, often presents with focal seizures day 1–3).
  • Causes: cardioembolic (CHD, endocarditis), arteriopathy (focal cerebral arteriopathy post-VZV, moyamoya, sickle cell, dissection, NF1), prothrombotic (sickle cell, leukaemia, factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid), infection/inflammation, trauma (dissection), metabolic (MELAS).
  • Sickle cell: transcranial Doppler screening (STOP trial); regular transfusion or hydroxyurea; bone marrow transplant for selected.
  • Acute: urgent MRI; tPA and EVT in selected paediatric centres (AHA 2019); aspirin for most AIS; anticoagulation for cardiac embolism, dissection, CVST.
8. ADEM & paediatric demyelinating disease
  • ADEM: monophasic, encephalopathy + multifocal CNS deficits + multifocal T2 lesions; 5–10 days post viral / vaccine. Treat: high-dose IV methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/d × 3–5 days, then oral taper; IVIG or PLEX if refractory.
  • Paediatric ADEM is strongly MOGAD-associated (2023 international criteria — cross-ref Module 8); recurrence common.
  • Paediatric MS: 3–5% of all MS; defer diagnosis if first event < 12 y (may be MOGAD); McDonald 2017 with caveats. DMTs from interferons/glatiramer to fingolimod (paediatric phase III), increasingly natalizumab and ocrelizumab in adolescents.
  • NMOSD with AQP4 antibody — rarer; eculizumab / inebilizumab / satralizumab in adolescents.
9. Paediatric movement disorders
  • Sydenham chorea: post-strep autoimmune; major Jones criterion. Treat infection; symptomatic valproate/CBZ/risperidone; steroids/IVIG severe; long-term penicillin prophylaxis.
  • PANDAS / PANS: abrupt OCD/tics/anxiety after infection; treat infection; CBT/SSRI; immunotherapy selected severe (controversial).
  • Tics / Tourette: onset 5–7 y; wax/wane; comorbid OCD/ADHD. First-line CBIT behavioural therapy; α2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine); dopamine antagonists for severe (Module 6).
  • Dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa): childhood dystonia with diurnal worsening; GCH1; dramatic L-dopa response.
  • Stereotypies: hand-flapping etc — common in ASD/ID and in typical children; no urge, no suppressibility (vs tics).
10. Paediatric headache
  • Migraine: most common recurrent headache; shorter (1–72 h) and bilateral OK in < 15. Childhood periodic syndromes (cyclical vomiting, abdominal migraine, BPV of childhood).
  • Tension-type: common; less disabling.
  • Secondary: posterior-fossa tumour (worst on waking + vomiting + gait + papilloedema + CN VI), IIH (obese teen girls; cross-ref Module 4), CVST, hydrocephalus / shunt malfunction, meningitis, post-traumatic, medication overuse.
  • Red flags for imaging: occipital, awakening from sleep, increasing pattern, focal neurology, papilloedema, < 6 y, abnormal HC, vomiting without other migraine features, new pattern.
  • Treatment: paracetamol / ibuprofen + triptan (sumatriptan nasal, almotriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan); preventive (topiramate, propranolol, amitriptyline, riboflavin, magnesium, behavioural). CGRP mAbs / gepants — limited adolescent data.
11. Autism spectrum & intellectual disability

DSM-5: persistent social communication deficits + restricted/repetitive behaviours + sensory abnormalities, onset in developmental period. Prevalence ~2%, male predominant.

Screen: M-CHAT-R at 18 and 24 months; refer if positive. Always test hearing in language-delayed children.

Workup: hearing, chromosomal microarray + Fragile X (~5–7% yield), whole-exome sequencing if syndromic. Identify treatable mimics — Rett (girls, regression + stereotypies, MECP2), PKU (newborn screen), congenital hypothyroidism, lead.

Management: early behavioural intervention (ABA / NDBI), speech/OT, education planning, family support. Pharmacotherapy targets comorbidities (risperidone/aripiprazole for irritability; methylphenidate for ADHD; SSRI for anxiety/OCD).

12. Non-accidental head injury & safeguarding
Suspect in any infant/young child with:
  • Subdural haemorrhages (multiple ages, bilateral, posterior).
  • Retinal haemorrhages (multilayer, peripheral).
  • Inconsistent or evolving history; injury inconsistent with developmental ability.
  • Delay seeking medical care.
  • Long bone / rib fractures of different ages; metaphyseal corner fractures.
  • Coexisting neglect / psychosocial concerns.
Investigate without delay: skeletal survey, ophthalmology retinal exam, CT brain acutely + MRI, bloods + bleeding screen; involve safeguarding teams.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 38-week newborn born after a cord-prolapse emergency, 10-min Apgar 4, cord pH 6.9. At 30 minutes: hypotonic, lethargic, miosis, seizures begin at 12 hours

Sarnat II (moderate). Continuous aEEG shows discontinuous background. No congenital anomalies.

Diagnosis: Neonatal hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, moderate (Sarnat II).

Plan within 6 hours:

  1. Therapeutic hypothermia: core temperature 33.5 °C for 72 hours, then slow rewarming over 6–12 hours.
  2. Continuous video EEG / aEEG.
  3. Treat seizures: phenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV (repeat to 40 mg/kg); second-line levetiracetam 40–60 mg/kg IV.
  4. Correct electrolytes (glucose, Ca, Mg, Na).
  5. Multiorgan support (renal, hepatic, coagulation, cardiac).
  6. Brain MRI day 4–7 (DWI) and day 10–14 (conventional) — assess injury pattern (BGT = acute profound; PVL; parasagittal).
  7. Family communication and multidisciplinary follow-up (developmental paediatrics, neurology, audiology, ophthalmology, physiotherapy).
The 6-hour window matters. HELIX trial (2021) shows the benefit of cooling may be limited in low/middle-income settings — context-specific decisions.
Case 2 — A 7-month-old has clusters of brief flexor spasms 20–30 times per cluster, particularly on waking. Now has lost the social smile she had at 4 months. EEG: chaotic high-voltage slow waves with multifocal spikes. Skin: 4 hypopigmented macules on the trunk

Wood's lamp accentuates the macules. MRI: cortical tubers and subependymal nodules.

Diagnosis: Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS, West syndrome) with hypsarrhythmia, in the context of tuberous sclerosis complex.

Plan:

  1. Urgent treatment — outcome depends on speed of control.
  2. Vigabatrin first-line because of tuberous sclerosis: 50 mg/kg/day titrated to 150 mg/kg/day; monitor for visual field constriction (ERG, baseline ophthalmology).
  3. If non-TS or non-responsive: ACTH or oral prednisolone (typically 40–60 mg/m²/day prednisolone or high-dose ACTH).
  4. EEG to confirm hypsarrhythmia resolution (the treatment target).
  5. Tuberous sclerosis workup (cross-ref Module 12): TSC1/TSC2 genetic testing, echocardiogram (cardiac rhabdomyomas), renal ultrasound (angiomyolipomas), retinal exam, dermatology.
  6. Consider everolimus (mTOR inhibitor) for SEGA / refractory seizures / renal AML.
  7. Family support; multidisciplinary follow-up; cognitive surveillance.
Case 3 — A 14-month-old has had prolonged febrile hemiclonic seizures since 6 months. New short tonic-clonic seizures and myoclonic jerks. Carbamazepine started — seizures dramatically worse

Developmental progress now plateaued. Genetic testing: SCN1A loss-of-function variant confirmed.

Diagnosis: Dravet syndrome.

Plan:

  1. Stop carbamazepine immediately — sodium-channel blockers (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine) worsen seizures in Dravet because SCN1A is on inhibitory interneurons.
  2. First-line: valproate + clobazam.
  3. Add stiripentol (EMA/FDA approved with valproate + clobazam), cannabidiol (FDA-approved 2018 for Dravet and LGS), or fenfluramine (FDA 2020 — striking efficacy in trials).
  4. Ketogenic diet for refractory cases.
  5. Aggressive fever management (paracetamol/ibuprofen with any illness; cooling measures); avoid known triggers.
  6. Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) counselling; rescue medication (buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam) at home.
  7. Multidisciplinary care: paediatric neurology, developmental paediatrics, speech/OT, school planning.
Case 4 — A 2-month-old has profound hypotonia, never controls his head, alert and engaged, no movement against gravity, areflexia, and tongue fasciculations

SMN1 deletion test positive. SMN2 copy number = 2.

Diagnosis: Spinal muscular atrophy type 1 (Werdnig-Hoffmann).

Plan:

  1. Disease-modifying treatment as soon as possible (outcome depends on timing):
    • Onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma): single IV gene therapy if < 2 years and weight ≤ 21 kg; AAV9-SMN1; transformative if given early; very high cost.
    • Nusinersen (Spinraza): intrathecal antisense oligonucleotide; 4 loading doses then every 4 months.
    • Risdiplam (Evrysdi): oral, daily, SMN2 splicing modifier; convenient for outpatient.
  2. Multidisciplinary care: respiratory (NIV, cough assist), nutrition (gastrostomy if needed), physiotherapy, orthotics, palliative care discussions.
  3. Family counselling and genetic counselling (autosomal recessive; carrier screening for siblings and future pregnancies).
  4. SMA newborn screening for at-risk siblings (pre-symptomatic treatment dramatically improves outcomes).
Case 5 — A 4-year-old presents with 3 weeks of progressively worsening morning headaches and vomiting on rising. New ataxia and abducens palsy

Fundoscopy: bilateral papilloedema. MRI: 4 cm cystic-solid posterior-fossa mass with hydrocephalus.

Diagnosis: Posterior-fossa tumour (likely medulloblastoma or pilocytic astrocytoma) with obstructive hydrocephalus.

Plan:

  1. Urgent neurosurgery consultation — likely need for hydrocephalus management (external ventricular drain or endoscopic third ventriculostomy) followed by tumour resection.
  2. Dexamethasone for peritumoural oedema.
  3. MRI total spine (for CSF dissemination — medulloblastoma).
  4. Tissue diagnosis at resection; molecular subgrouping for medulloblastoma (WNT — best prognosis; SHH; group 3; group 4).
  5. Combined paediatric neuro-oncology team — resection + craniospinal RT (in older children) + chemotherapy per protocol; pilocytic astrocytoma may be curable with resection alone (BRAF fusion).
  6. Cross-ref Module 12 for paediatric brain tumour orientation.
  7. Long-term: neurocognitive, endocrine, hearing surveillance; secondary tumour risk after RT.
Case 6 — A 4-month-old, primary caregiver brings him in for "vomiting and seizures". He has retinal haemorrhages, bilateral subdural haemorrhages of different signal intensities, and a poorly healing humeral fracture from a "rolling off the sofa" history given 2 days ago

Examination: bulging fontanelle; encephalopathic; bruising in unusual locations. Coagulation screen normal.

Diagnosis: Highly suspicious abusive head trauma (non-accidental injury) — multiple ages of bleeding, retinal haemorrhages, inconsistent history (4-month-old cannot roll), unexplained fracture.

Plan:

  1. Stabilise — airway, breathing, circulation; treat raised ICP / seizures.
  2. CT brain acutely; MRI brain and spine within 24–72 hours.
  3. Skeletal survey within 24 hours; repeat at 10–14 days (occult fractures).
  4. Ophthalmology for dilated fundus examination (retinal haemorrhages — multilayer, peripheral, dome-shaped retinoschisis support shaking mechanism).
  5. Full bloods, coagulation, urine and serum tox, urine organic acids (exclude metabolic), Ca/PO₄ (exclude osteogenesis imperfecta or rickets).
  6. Involve safeguarding / child-protection team without delay; document carefully (timing, history, photos).
  7. Notify police as required by local mandatory reporting laws.
  8. Multidisciplinary discussion; social-work assessment; protection of siblings.
Do not delay safeguarding referral pending further investigations. Document accurately and use professional language; pre-judgement of guilt is not your role, but child safety is.
