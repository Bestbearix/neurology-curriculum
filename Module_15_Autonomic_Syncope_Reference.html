1. Framework — separate "cardiac killers" from benign reflex syncope

Syncope is one of the most common reasons for ED attendance — and one of the most over-investigated. The job at the front door is binary: is this likely reflex (vasovagal) syncope (benign, requiring reassurance and counselling) or is there a cardiac cause (potentially lethal, requiring monitoring and workup)? Orthostatic causes sit in between and are common in elderly and in patients with autonomic disease.

Autonomic disorders are easy to miss because their features cross many systems — orthostatic dizziness, postprandial fatigue, urinary symptoms, erectile dysfunction, constipation, dry eyes/mouth, abnormal sweating — and are often dismissed as non-specific. A high index of suspicion in any patient with diabetes, Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy, amyloidosis, Sjögren syndrome, or chronic GBS recovery is essential.

The single principle: in any transient loss of consciousness (TLoC), ask three questions before anything else — (1) was it true syncope (rapid onset, brief, complete recovery)? (2) any prodrome (vasovagal vs sudden = cardiac)? (3) any high-risk features (exertion, supine, structural heart disease, palpitation, family history of sudden death)? Investigate accordingly; do not pan-scan low-risk vasovagal patients.

Central autonomic network

  • Hypothalamus (paraventricular, dorsomedial) — autonomic integration with endocrine, behavioural, thermoregulatory output.
  • Insular cortex — interoception; right-sided in most → sympathetic; left-sided → parasympathetic (right insular stroke can cause arrhythmia, BP changes).
  • Amygdala, anterior cingulate — autonomic responses to emotion.
  • Brainstem nuclei: nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS — afferent baroreflex), nucleus ambiguus (cardiac vagal), rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM — sympathetic outflow), dorsal motor nucleus of vagus.

Baroreflex — the key circuit for postural BP

  1. Carotid sinus & aortic arch baroreceptors → CN IX & X afferents → NTS.
  2. NTS → ↑ vagal output (nucleus ambiguus) ↓ HR; NTS → caudal VLM → inhibits RVLM ↓ sympathetic → vasodilation, ↓ HR, ↓ contractility.
  3. Reverse with hypotension: ↑ sympathetic via RVLM → vasoconstriction, ↑ HR.
  4. Failure anywhere in this loop → orthostatic hypotension.

3. Syncope — definitions, classification, triage

Syncope is a transient, self-limited loss of consciousness due to global cerebral hypoperfusion, with rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery. Distinguish from non-syncopal TLoC (seizure, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, psychogenic).

4. Syncope workup & risk scores

Core workup for any TLoC

  1. Detailed history (patient + witness) — prodrome, trigger, posture, exertion, duration, recovery, prior episodes, family history of sudden death.
  2. Examination — orthostatic vitals at 1 & 3 minutes (and 5 if delayed OH suspected); cardiac (murmurs, aortic stenosis, HCM); neurological screen.
  3. 12-lead ECG in every patient — look for long QT, Brugada, HCM, WPW, AV block, ARVC pattern.
  4. Bedside glucose; pregnancy test in reproductive-age women.
  5. Echocardiogram if abnormal exam, ECG, or clinical concern for structural heart disease.
  6. Holter / event recorder / implantable loop recorder for recurrent syncope without diagnosis.
  7. Tilt-table only after cardiac causes excluded (see below).
  8. Reserve brain CT/MRI/EEG for clear neurological features (focal deficit, prolonged confusion, witnessed seizure).

Risk stratification

San Francisco Syncope Rule (CHESS)

Any one of the following predicts 7-day serious outcome (sensitivity ~96%):

  • CHF (history)
  • Hematocrit < 30%
  • ECG abnormal (new changes or non-sinus rhythm)
  • Shortness of breath (history)
  • Systolic BP < 90 mmHg at triage

Canadian Syncope Risk Score (CSRS, Thiruganasambandamoorthy 2016)

PredictorPoints
Predisposition to vasovagal (warm/crowded place, prolonged standing, fear/emotion/pain)−1
History of heart disease (CAD, valve, CHF, NS-VT/VT, congenital)+1
Systolic BP < 90 or > 180 mmHg in ED+2
Troponin elevated > 99th percentile+2
Abnormal QRS axis+1
QRS duration > 130 ms+1
QTc > 480 ms+2
ED diagnosis of vasovagal syncope−2
ED diagnosis of cardiac syncope+2

Total: −3 to +11. 30-day serious adverse event risk: −3 to −2 → very low (~0.4%); −1 to 0 → low (~0.7%); 1–3 → medium (~3–8%); 4–5 → high (~12–20%); ≥ 6 → very high (~28%).

Red flags that mandate admission / urgent cardiology referral:
  • Syncope during exertion or in the supine position.
  • Preceding palpitation.
  • Family history of sudden cardiac death < 40 years.
  • Structural heart disease (severe AS, HCM, severe MR, prior MI, CHF).
  • Abnormal ECG (long QT, Brugada, AV block, NSVT, pre-excitation, RBBB + LAFB).
  • Syncope with injury or no warning ("drop attacks").
  • New murmur or sign of CHF.

5. Tilt-table testing and autonomic function tests

  • Tilt-table test (head-up tilt at 60–70° for 20–45 min, sometimes with provocation by isoproterenol or nitroglycerin): provokes reflex syncope or demonstrates OH/POTS. Best for recurrent syncope of unclear cause after cardiac workup negative.
  • Active stand test (lying then standing, BP/HR at 1 and 3 minutes): cheaper and effective for orthostatic hypotension and POTS. Five-minute reading may capture delayed OH (PD).
  • Heart-rate variability tests: deep breathing (vagal — parasympathetic), Valsalva ratio (combined), 30:15 ratio on standing (vagal).
  • Sympathetic skin response and QSART (quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test) — small-fibre / sudomotor assessment.
  • Plasma catecholamines (lying and standing): low standing NE → postganglionic failure (PAF, PD); normal/high lying NE with poor rise → central failure (MSA).
  • 123I-MIBG cardiac scintigraphy — reduced uptake in PD and PAF (postganglionic loss); normal in MSA (preganglionic). Useful when diagnosis between PD and MSA is unclear.

6. Orthostatic hypotension (OH)

Definition

  • Classic OH: sustained fall in systolic BP ≥ 20 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing or head-up tilt.
  • In supine hypertensive patients, drop in SBP ≥ 30 mmHg may be used.
  • Initial OH: transient drop within 15 s of standing — younger people; resolves spontaneously by ~30 s.
  • Delayed OH: drop after 3 minutes — common in PD; prolonged tilt required to detect.

Neurogenic vs non-neurogenic

FeatureNon-neurogenic (volume / drugs)Neurogenic
Heart-rate response↑ HR by ≥ 15 bpm (compensatory)Inadequate rise (< 15 bpm increase) — sympathetic failure
CausesVolume depletion, anaemia, antihypertensives, diuretics, alcohol, vasodilators, antidepressants (TCAs, MAOIs, trazodone), antipsychotics, α-blockers, dopamine agonistsPAF, MSA, PD, DLB, diabetic / amyloid / paraneoplastic / Sjögren autonomic neuropathy, AAG, GBS, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury
Standing plasma NENormal riseLow NE rise; absolute low NE in PAF/PD; normal supine but low standing
The heart-rate-response trick: a HR increase < 15 bpm (or HR-to-systolic-drop ratio < 0.5) on a 20-mmHg systolic drop strongly suggests neurogenic OH. A compensatory tachycardia > 15 bpm makes volume depletion or drug effect more likely.

7. Management of OH & supine hypertension

Non-pharmacological (always first)

  • Review medications: discontinue or reduce diuretics, antihypertensives, α-blockers, TCAs, MAOIs, dopamine agonists where possible. Time antihypertensives differently if OH.
  • Volume expansion: increase fluid intake to 2–2.5 L/day; salt intake ~ 6–10 g/day (unless contraindicated by HF/CKD). Bolus 0.5 L water in < 5 min raises BP within 5–10 min (pressor effect of water).
  • Physical counter-pressure manoeuvres at warning of symptoms: leg crossing with tensing, hand grip, squat, abdominal contraction. Sleep with head of bed up 10–20° (reduces nocturnal supine HTN and nocturnal natriuresis).
  • Compression garments: thigh-high stockings or abdominal binders (binder more effective than stockings; both better than nothing).
  • Avoid hot environments, hot showers, large carbohydrate-rich meals (postprandial hypotension), Valsalva.
  • Exercise: recumbent (rowing, swimming, recumbent cycling) initially; gradually progress to upright.

Pharmacotherapy

DrugDoseComments
Midodrine (α1 agonist)2.5–10 mg po TID (last dose by 6 pm to limit supine HTN)First-line; effective in PD, MSA, PAF; piloerection, urinary retention, supine HTN are limitations; warn against lying down 4 hours after dose

Managing coexistent supine hypertension

  • Common in MSA, PAF, and PD — present in 50% of patients with neurogenic OH.
  • Avoid lying flat; head of bed up 10–20° at night.
  • Carbohydrate snack at bedtime (lowers BP by inducing postprandial response).
  • Short-acting evening antihypertensive if supine SBP > 160–180 (losartan 50 mg, nifedipine immediate release, transdermal nitroglycerin removed in morning) — must NOT carry over to morning when patient stands.
  • Do NOT use long-acting antihypertensives — risk of morning OH.

8. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS)

Diagnostic criteria (consensus)

  • Sustained HR rise ≥ 30 bpm within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt (≥ 40 bpm in adolescents aged 12–19).
  • Absence of orthostatic hypotension (no > 20 mmHg systolic drop).
  • Chronic symptoms ≥ 3 months (lightheadedness, palpitations, fatigue, cognitive fog, headache).
  • Not explained by another cause (anaemia, dehydration, deconditioning, drugs, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, mast cell disease).

Clinical

  • Predominantly young women (15–50); often after a trigger — viral illness, COVID-19, pregnancy, surgery, trauma.
  • Subtypes proposed: neuropathic (denervation in lower limbs), hyperadrenergic (high standing NE > 600 pg/mL, hypertension), hypovolaemic.
  • Overlap conditions: Ehlers-Danlos (hypermobility), mast cell activation syndrome, chronic fatigue syndrome / ME, post-acute COVID-19 sequelae.

Treatment

  1. Non-pharmacological first:
    • Increase salt 6–10 g/day + fluid 2–3 L/day.
    • Compression — abdominal binder + waist-high stockings.
    • Structured graduated exercise programme — recumbent or seated exercise (rowing, recumbent bike, swimming) progressing to upright over months; THE most effective intervention long-term.
    • Avoid prolonged standing in heat; cool clothing.
  2. Pharmacotherapy: low-dose propranolol 10–20 mg po TID/QID (small doses; large doses can worsen symptoms) or ivabradine 2.5–7.5 mg BID (selective HR reduction, no BP effect); fludrocortisone; midodrine (especially neuropathic POTS); pyridostigmine; methyldopa or clonidine for hyperadrenergic POTS; IV saline boluses can rescue acute flares.
  3. Address comorbid mast cell symptoms (H1 + H2 antihistamines) and anxiety/depression as appropriate.

10. Autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy & acute pandysautonomia

  • Acute autoimmune autonomic neuropathy (AAN) / acute pandysautonomia: subacute onset (days–weeks) of severe OH + anhidrosis + parasympathetic failure (dry eyes/mouth, dilated unreactive pupils, fixed heart rate, urinary retention, sexual dysfunction) + GI dysmotility, often after viral prodrome (HSV, EBV, rubella, post-vaccination). Monophasic. Slow incomplete recovery.
  • Autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy (AAG): anti-α3-ganglionic acetylcholine receptor (gAChR) antibodies in ~50%; chronic or subacute course; selective autonomic failure; tonic pupils, sicca, severe OH, severe gastrointestinal dysmotility.
  • Other antibodies: amphiphysin, CRMP5 (paraneoplastic with small-cell lung cancer); occasionally limbic encephalitis overlap.
  • Treatment: IVIG, plasma exchange, corticosteroids, rituximab, mycophenolate. Address any underlying tumour (paraneoplastic) — cross-ref Module 12.

11. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy

  • The most common cause of autonomic neuropathy worldwide.
  • Long-standing T1DM or T2DM; concurrent length-dependent sensorimotor neuropathy in most.
  • Manifestations:
    • Cardiovascular: resting tachycardia, exercise intolerance, OH, silent MI, blunted HR variability, increased mortality.
    • GI: gastroparesis (early satiety, bloating, vomiting, erratic glycaemia), diabetic diarrhoea, constipation, faecal incontinence.
    • Urogenital: erectile dysfunction (often the earliest sign), retrograde ejaculation, bladder dysfunction (atonic bladder, recurrent UTI), incomplete emptying.
    • Sudomotor: distal anhidrosis + truncal hyperhidrosis; gustatory sweating after meals.
    • Pupillary: small poorly reactive pupils.
    • Hypoglycaemia unawareness from impaired counterregulatory sympathetic response.
  • Treatment: tight glycaemic control (more effective in T1DM than T2DM for autonomic neuropathy); symptom-directed care (midodrine, fludrocortisone for OH; prokinetics — metoclopramide short courses (Module 6 caution), erythromycin, prucalopride; PDE5 inhibitors for ED; intermittent self-catheterisation for bladder; α-blockers for incomplete emptying); cardiology screening if cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy.

13. Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (PSH)

  • "Sympathetic storms" after severe acquired brain injury — most commonly traumatic brain injury (5–30%), anoxia, stroke, encephalitis.
  • Episodic, simultaneous, transient increases in HR, BP, respiratory rate, temperature, sweating, plus motor features (dystonic posturing).
  • Misdiagnosed as sepsis, seizures, PE, withdrawal.

Baguley diagnostic criteria (2014)

Two components: Clinical Feature Scale (severity of HR, BP, RR, temperature, sweating, posturing) + Diagnosis Likelihood Tool (clinical features and antecedent brain injury). PSH probability is combined diagnostic + clinical: total 8–16 = unlikely; 17–26 = possible; ≥ 27 = probable.

Triggers and management

  • Triggers: pain, position changes, suctioning, environmental stimuli, urinary retention, constipation.
  • Reduce noxious stimuli; treat underlying triggers (catheterise full bladder, give analgesia, treat constipation).
  • Abortive: morphine 1–2 mg IV (gold standard), benzodiazepine, propofol, dexmedetomidine.
  • Prevention: scheduled propranolol (non-selective β-blocker, blood-brain-barrier-penetrating), gabapentin, clonidine, baclofen (especially with posturing), bromocriptine; combinations often needed.
  • Avoid haloperidol (can worsen dystonic features).
  • Improves over weeks to months as brain recovers.

14. GBS dysautonomia & other acute settings

  • GBS (Module 9): autonomic instability in > 50% — labile BP, sinus tachy/brady, arrhythmias, ileus, urinary retention. Major mortality driver in severe GBS. Monitor on telemetry; avoid pushed boluses of cardioactive drugs.
  • Spinal cord injury: autonomic dysreflexia in lesions above T6 — surge of HTN, headache, bradycardia, sweating above the lesion, triggered by noxious stimulus below (distended bladder, faecal impaction, pressure ulcer). Treatment: sit patient up, loosen restrictive clothing, identify and remove trigger; nifedipine immediate-release or topical/sublingual nitrate if BP persists. Cross-ref Module 10.
  • Tetanus: severe autonomic instability is the major killer in modern tetanus — magnesium infusion + benzodiazepines + α-blockade.
  • NMS, serotonin syndrome, MH, sympathomimetic toxidromes: cross-ref Module 13.
  • Pheochromocytoma: episodic catecholamine surge — sustained or paroxysmal HTN + headache + sweating + palpitations. α-blockade (phenoxybenzamine, doxazosin) BEFORE β-blockade to avoid unopposed α-vasoconstriction. Surgical removal definitive.

15. When to refer

  • Same day: high-risk syncope (CSRS ≥ 4, abnormal ECG, exertional, structural heart disease, family history of sudden death); paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity in acute brain injury; autonomic dysreflexia; suspected pheochromocytoma crisis; severe GBS dysautonomia.
  • Within 1–2 weeks: recurrent unexplained syncope after low-risk workup; suspected autonomic neuropathy; suspected MSA / PAF / DLB; new POTS; severe diabetic autonomic neuropathy.
  • Routine: chronic OH stable on therapy; established PAF / PD with autonomic features on regular review.
  • Always involve: cardiology for cardiac causes of syncope; movement disorders for MSA/PD; autonomic clinic for tilt-table, MIBG, complex polypharmacy.
