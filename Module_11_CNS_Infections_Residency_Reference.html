1 Approach: meningitis vs encephalitis

▎Key points
  • Meningitis = meningeal inflammation (fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia). Encephalitis = brain-parenchyma inflammation (altered mentation, seizures, focal deficits). They overlap (meningoencephalitis).
  • Do not delay treatment for diagnostics. In suspected bacterial meningitis, give empiric antibiotics + dexamethasone immediately; in suspected encephalitis, start aciclovir empirically.
  • Sequence when CT is needed first: blood cultures → dexamethasone + antibiotics → CT → LP (don't let imaging delay drugs).

The first fork is meningitis versus encephalitis, because it changes the empiric drug: predominant meningismus → bacterial meningitis pathway (antibiotics + steroid); predominant encephalopathy/seizures/focal signs → encephalitis pathway (aciclovir, and consider autoimmune encephalitis in parallel — cross-links to Modules 7 & 8). Take a careful exposure/host history: immune status (HIV, transplant, biologics), travel and residence (TB, endemic mycoses, malaria, cysticercosis), animal/insect and food exposures, and vaccination.

2 CSF interpretation

▎Key points
  • Bacterial: ↑↑ neutrophils, very high protein, low glucose (CSF:serum <0.4). Viral: lymphocytes, mildly high protein, normal glucose.
  • TB / fungal: lymphocytes, high protein, low glucose — the "chronic lymphocytic with low glucose" pattern.
  • Always send opening pressure, cell count/differential, glucose (with paired serum), protein, Gram stain/culture, and targeted studies (PCR panel, cryptococcal antigen, AFB/TB-PCR, VDRL).

Reflex add-ons by host: in HIV/immunocompromised send cryptococcal antigen (serum + CSF) and toxoplasma serology; in the right epidemiology send TB-PCR (Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra) and endemic-fungal testing; in encephalitis send the HSV/VZV/enterovirus PCR panel plus autoimmune-encephalitis antibodies. Multiplex PCR panels speed diagnosis but have false negatives/positives — interpret with the CSF profile.
A normal CSF does not exclude early HSV encephalitis — HSV PCR can be negative in the first 72 h. If suspicion is high, continue aciclovir and repeat the LP in 3–7 days.

3 Bacterial meningitis

▎Key points
  • Commonest adult pathogens: Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis; add Listeria cover in neonates, age >50, pregnancy, and immunocompromise.
  • Empiric adult therapy: vancomycin + ceftriaxone (± ampicillin for Listeria) + dexamethasone.
  • Give dexamethasone with or just before the first antibiotic dose; continue only if pneumococcal, stop if Listeria is confirmed.
  • It is a true emergency — mortality and disability rise with every hour of delay.

Adjunctive dexamethasone (provided reviews/cohorts). Dexamethasone before/with the first antibiotic dose reduces mortality and hearing loss chiefly in pneumococcal meningitis; contemporary cohorts (e.g., the Danish nationwide 2015–2023 study) show falling mortality alongside protocolized empiric antibiotics + dexamethasone. Stop the steroid if Listeria is identified (no benefit).
Don't forget the public-health & prophylaxis steps: droplet precautions and post-exposure chemoprophylaxis for close contacts of meningococcal disease; notify public health.

4 ICP, LP timing & herniation

▎Key points
  • CT before LP if: focal deficit, new seizure, papilledema, reduced/altered consciousness, or immunocompromise — but never delay antibiotics for the CT.
  • A normal CT does not guarantee LP is safe in fulminant bacterial meningitis — clinical herniation risk still applies.
  • Raised ICP is a major driver of death; recognize and treat it (head-up, osmotherapy, treat seizures, consider ICP monitoring / lumbar drainage in selected refractory cases).
LP and herniation (provided narrative review). The review of herniation shortly after LP in acute bacterial meningitis emphasizes that in a patient clinically at high risk of herniation, CT may not reliably exclude the risk — prioritize ICP-directed measures and antibiotics, and defer LP if the patient is deteriorating. Respiratory arrest or a tonic "seizure" can actually be early herniation.

Severe community-acquired bacterial meningitis complicated by raised ICP has been managed with ICP monitoring and, in refractory intracranial hypertension with patent basal cisterns, controlled lumbar drainage (provided series) — specialized, ICU-level decisions.

5 Tuberculous meningitis (TBM)

▎Key points
  • Subacute basal meningitis with cranial neuropathies, hydrocephalus, and basal enhancement ± infarcts (vasculitis) ± tuberculomas. CSF: lymphocytic, high protein, low glucose.
  • Diagnose with TB-PCR (Xpert Ultra), AFB smear/culture (large-volume CSF), and imaging; treatment is empiric when suspected — don't wait for culture.
  • Standard therapy = 4-drug anti-TB + adjunctive corticosteroids; high mortality/morbidity worldwide.
Corticosteroids — genotype-stratified dexamethasone (provided phase-3 RCT). Adjunctive dexamethasone is recommended in TBM. This trial randomized LTA4H CC/CT-genotype HIV-negative adults and did not demonstrate the anticipated survival benefit in that genotype stratum — challenging the idea that steroid benefit is uniform and reopening how to target steroids. (Dexamethasone still has an established mortality benefit in HIV-positive TBM per prior trials.)
High-dose rifampin (provided RCT). Standard rifampin penetrates the CNS poorly; a multicountry trial tested intensified rifampin dosing. Optimizing rifampin exposure is an active strategy, but the field still lacks a clearly superior regimen — "new approaches needed" (provided review).
Watch for hydrocephalus and paradoxical worsening. TBM frequently causes obstructive hydrocephalus (may need CSF diversion) and paradoxical reactions/IRIS after starting therapy. Spinal TB (Pott disease) can cause cord compression.

6 Viral encephalitis — HSV

▎Key points
  • HSV-1 encephalitis = the treatable can't-miss: fever, altered mentation, temporal/limbic involvement, seizures. Start IV aciclovir empirically in any suspected encephalitis.
  • Dose aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h; confirm with CSF HSV PCR (repeat if early/negative but suspicious) and MRI (temporal/insular T2/FLAIR).
  • Delay in aciclovir worsens outcome — treat first, investigate in parallel.
Adjunctive dexamethasone — DexEnceph (provided RCT). A UK multicentre trial randomized adults with PCR-confirmed HSV encephalitis to IV dexamethasone plus aciclovir versus aciclovir alone, testing whether steroids improve outcome. The role of adjunctive steroids in HSV encephalitis remains investigational — aciclovir is the established therapy.
Post-HSV autoimmune (anti-NMDA-R) encephalitis: new movement disorder/psychiatric decline weeks after HSV encephalitis, often with negative repeat HSV PCR → test NMDA-R antibodies and give immunotherapy, not more aciclovir (cross-links to Module 8). Recognized childhood HSV encephalitis has an inborn-immunity (TLR3 pathway) basis.

Other viral encephalitides to weigh by epidemiology: VZV (vasculopathy), enteroviruses, arboviruses (e.g., Japanese encephalitis, West Nile), and — in the CSF/host context — CMV and HIV itself. Enterovirus D68 is linked to acute flaccid myelitis (below).

7 Brain abscess

▎Key points
  • Presents with headache, focal deficit, ± fever and seizures; ring-enhancing lesion with restricted diffusion on DWI (distinguishes from most tumours).
  • Source-directed: contiguous (otogenic/sinus/dental), haematogenous (endocarditis, right-to-left shunt), or post-neurosurgical.
  • Management usually combines neurosurgical aspiration/excision + prolonged targeted antibiotics; steroids only for significant mass effect/edema.
Surgery vs medical therapy (provided emulated-trial cohort). In a nationwide population-based emulated trial, a nonoperative strategy was associated with higher mortality and rupture risk than neurosurgery, and about a third of nonoperatively managed patients ultimately required surgery — supporting neurosurgical drainage in most abscesses, with medical-only therapy reserved for selected small/deep/multiple lesions or poor surgical candidates.
Corticosteroids in brain abscess are not benign — a target-trial-emulation study links steroid use to higher in-hospital mortality; reserve them for life-threatening edema/mass effect, not routine use.

8 Fungal CNS infection

▎Key points
  • Cryptococcal meningitis (HIV/immunocompromised) — very high opening pressure; diagnose by cryptococcal antigen; treatment = amphotericin B + flucytosine induction then fluconazole, with aggressive ICP management by serial LP.
  • Mould disease — Aspergillus and mucormycosis (rhino-orbital-cerebral, in diabetes/immunocompromise) are angioinvasive emergencies needing surgery + antifungals.
  • Endemic/regional fungi: histoplasmosis, and talaromycosis (Southeast Asia; linked to anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies), candida (healthcare-associated), nocardia (bacterial actinomycete but behaves like a mould-abscess).

Cryptococcus: the dominant preventable cause of death is raised ICP — manage with repeated therapeutic LPs (or drainage), not just antifungals; the provided risk-prediction and qPCR papers refine diagnosis and mortality stratification in sub-Saharan Africa. Aspergillus CNS disease is treated with voriconazole/isavuconazole (the provided ESCAI cohort supports isavuconazole). Mucormycosis demands early surgical debridement + liposomal amphotericin B and reversal of immunosuppression/acidosis. Candida, histoplasmosis, and nocardiosis are managed per the provided IDSA/ECMM/WHO guidance.

Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (mucor/aspergillus) in a neutropenic or diabetic-ketoacidotic patient is a surgical emergency — think of it with orbital/facial pain, black eschar, or cranial neuropathies.

9 Parasitic CNS infection

▎Key points
  • Neurocysticercosis (Taenia solium) — the leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic areas; cystic lesions at different stages; treat with albendazole ± praziquantel + corticosteroids, and antiseizure medication.
  • Cerebral toxoplasmosis — ring-enhancing lesions in advanced HIV; empiric pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine, response confirms diagnosis.
  • Cerebral malaria (P. falciparum) — coma + parasitaemia; a medical emergency treated with IV artesunate; long-term cognitive sequelae, especially in children.

Neurocysticercosis management is staged by cyst location (parenchymal vs extraparenchymal/racemose), viability, and burden — anti-parasitic therapy is paired with steroids to blunt the inflammatory reaction and with seizure control; point-of-care antigen testing (provided Tanzania study) aids diagnosis where imaging is limited. Cerebral toxoplasmosis is distinguished from primary CNS lymphoma in HIV by response to therapy, toxoplasma serology, and (if needed) thallium-SPECT/CSF EBV-PCR. Cerebral malaria carries characteristic imaging/EEG features (provided studies) and both cerebral malaria and severe malarial anaemia are associated with long-term cognitive impairment.

10 HIV, PML & opportunistic disease

▎Key points
  • In advanced HIV, use the CD4 count + imaging pattern to frame the differential (toxoplasmosis, primary CNS lymphoma, cryptococcus, TB, PML, CMV).
  • PML (JC virus) — subacute multifocal white-matter disease in immunosuppression (HIV, natalizumab); no specific antiviral — restore immunity (ART, stop the offending immunosuppressant), and anticipate IRIS.
  • HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder persists in the ART era; optimize ART and manage comorbidities.
PML / JCV (provided review). PML is caused by JC virus reactivation; ART has cut HIV-related PML, but iatrogenic PML has surged with natalizumab and other immunomodulators. There is no specific antiviral — management is immune restoration, tempered by the risk of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), which can paradoxically worsen deficits.

Note the emerging concept of CSF viral escape (detectable/active CNS virus despite peripheral control) relevant to HIV reservoirs and persistent symptoms. Healthcare-associated ventriculitis/meningitis (shunts, drains, post-op) is a distinct entity with its own microbiology and diagnostic markers (provided papers on CSF heparin-binding protein and PCR panels).

Acute flaccid myelitis (AFM)

A polio-like anterior-horn syndrome of children, temporally and mechanistically linked to enterovirus D68 (the provided causality review and the recent identification of MFSD6 as an EV-D68 entry receptor). Presents with acute flaccid limb weakness ± cranial nerve involvement after a viral prodrome; MRI shows longitudinal grey-matter (anterior horn) cord signal. Management is supportive; recognize it as an infectious cord syndrome (cross-links to Module 10).
