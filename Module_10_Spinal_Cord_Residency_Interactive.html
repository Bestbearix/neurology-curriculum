Treat the cord like the emergency it is

The general-physician Module 10 teaches recognition and referral. This enriched version assumes you localize the cord syndrome, image the whole spine emergently, and act on the decompression-timing and MESCC evidence — while never missing the treatable mimics.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Localize by sensory level + cross-sectional tract pattern; recognize spinal shock.
  2. Triage the compressive cord emergency (MESCC, epidural abscess/hematoma, cauda equina) to emergent MRI + treatment.
  3. Classify traumatic SCI (ISNCSCI/AIS) and apply early (<24 h) decompression + MAP support; state the steroid controversy.
  4. Recognize incomplete cord syndromes and the vascular myelopathies (infarction, dural AV fistula).
  5. Work up inflammatory/infectious myelitis and the inherited/toxic myelopathies; manage autonomic dysreflexia.

Syndrome / cause

A discrete sensory level on the trunk (nipple T4, umbilicus T10) is the cord's fingerprint — it mandates emergent whole-spine MRI.

Cord anatomy & localization

Key points
  • Localize in two dimensions: vertical level (sensory level) + cross-sectional pattern.
  • Dorsal columns = vibration/proprioception (spared in anterior infarcts); corticospinal + spinothalamic run laterally.
  • Cord ends at conus ~L1–L2; landmarks nipple T4, umbilicus T10, inguinal L1.
Spinal shock: acute cord lesions are initially flaccid/areflexic below the level — not spastic. Absent tone doesn't exclude a severe myelopathy.

Acute compressive cord emergencies

Key points
  • Acute/subacute myelopathy → emergent whole-spine MRI with contrast + treat the cause; image the entire spine.
  • MESCC: dexamethasone now; Patchell — surgery + RT beats RT alone; ambulatory status predicts walking.
  • Epidural abscess: fever + back pain + deficit; S. aureus; drainage + antibiotics.
  • Cauda equina: saddle anaesthesia + retention → emergent MRI + urgent decompression.
Time is spine. Lesions are often multiple or at a different level than the clinical guess — image the whole spine. If MRI unavailable → CT myelography.

Metastatic epidural cord compression (MESCC)

Patchell (Lancet 2005). Decompressive surgery + radiotherapy > radiotherapy alone for retaining/regaining walking. Give dexamethasone at diagnosis. Modern option: separation surgery + SABR for radioresistant tumours.

Traumatic spinal cord injury

Key points
  • Classify with ISNCSCI + ASIA Impairment Scale (A–E) — determine level, scores, completeness (sacral sparing).
  • Early decompression <24 h → better recovery (pooled IPD).
  • MAP ~85–90 mm Hg × 5–7 days; watch for neurogenic shock (hypotension + bradycardia ≥ above T6).
  • Riluzole (RISCIS): negative. Methylprednisolone: controversial, not recommended routinely.

Decompression timing (pooled IPD). Early (<24 h) decompression → greater AIS-grade improvement. Central cord syndrome analysis compared early vs late similarly.
Hemodynamics (AO Spine CPG). MAP augmentation ~85–90 mm Hg for ~5–7 days; avoid hypotension/hypoxia; ICU care.
Neurogenic shock (hypotension + bradycardia, injuries above ~T6) differs from hypovolemic and spinal shock — fluids, vasopressors, atropine for bradycardia. Cervical injuries (C3–C5) threaten the diaphragm/airway.
Steroid controversy: high-dose methylprednisolone is not universally recommended — modest possible benefit vs respiratory/infectious harm; at most an option in a narrow window per local protocol.

Vascular myelopathy

Key points
  • Cord infarction: hyperacute painful anterior-cord syndrome; aortic disease, atherosclerosis, fibrocartilaginous embolism; supportive care; poor prognosis.
  • Spinal dural AV fistula = the great mimic: older men, progressive congestive myelopathy, LETM + perimedullary flow voids, ~⅓ misdiagnosed as TM — but treatable.
  • Cavernoma: stepwise deficits from recurrent microhemorrhage.
SDAVF — recognize it. Progressive stepwise myelopathy with longitudinally extensive T2 signal + dilated perimedullary flow voids; confirm with spinal angiography; treat by embolization/ligation. Missing it → avoidable paraplegia.
Cord infarction shows owl's-eye anterior grey-matter T2 signal ± vertebral-body infarct; DWI can be positive early. Note the SARS-CoV-2 association; thrombolysis is anecdotal only.

Inflammatory & infectious myelopathy

Key points
  • Transverse myelitis is a syndrome, not a diagnosis — always exclude compression first, then find the cause.
  • Longitudinally extensive TM (≥3 segments) → NMOSD (AQP4-IgG) & MOGAD; short-segment/eccentric → MS-like.
  • Infectious: viral (HSV/VZV, enterovirus, HIV), tuberculous, schistosomal; HTLV-1 tropical spastic paraparesis.

When the cord shows T2 signal without compression, broaden to inflammatory, infectious, vascular (SDAVF), and neoplastic causes. Work-up: contrast MRI brain + whole spine, CSF (cells, protein, oligoclonal bands), serum AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG, and infectious/serologic screens by exposure. Acute inflammatory myelitis usually gets high-dose corticosteroids ± plasma exchange for severe/refractory attacks; infective myelopathy needs directed therapy.

Exclude compression (and treatable SDAVF) before labelling "transverse myelitis" — the most dangerous error is steroids alone for a compressive or fistulous lesion.

Degenerative, structural & inherited/toxic

Key points
  • Degenerative cervical myelopathy — commonest non-traumatic adult cord dysfunction; surgery is the mainstay (CSM-PROTECT riluzole negative).
  • Syrinx/Chiari — suspended "cape" dissociated loss; posterior-fossa decompression when symptomatic.
  • Inherited: HSP; Friedreich ataxia (+omaveloxolone). Toxic/nutritional: B12/nitrous-oxide subacute combined degeneration.
DCM — CSM-PROTECT. Riluzole did not improve the primary mJOA outcome; surgical decompression halts progression and often improves function.

Nitrous-oxide myelopathy is rising — N₂O inactivates B12, so serum B12 may be normal; diagnose on MMA/homocysteine + exposure history.
Autonomic dysreflexia (injury ≥T6): stimulus below the level (usually bladder distension or bowel impaction) → reflex hypertension + bradycardia + pounding headache. Sit up, remove the trigger, then antihypertensive. Untreated → hemorrhage/seizure/death.

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A 68-year-old with prostate cancer has 2 weeks of worsening mid-back pain, now leg weakness, a T8 sensory level, and difficulty voiding. Still walking with assistance.
Diagnosis and immediate steps?

MESCC. Give dexamethasone now + emergent whole-spine MRI. Still ambulatory → act fast: per Patchell, surgery + RT preserves walking better than RT alone (or separation surgery + SABR for radioresistant tumour). Multidisciplinary decision.

Case 2. A 55-year-old man has 18 months of slowly progressive leg weakness worse after walking; MRI shows longitudinally extensive T2 cord signal + prominent dorsal perimedullary flow voids. Treated for "transverse myelitis" without benefit.
What was missed?

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula — the classic mimic (older man, congestive myelopathy, LETM + flow voids), often misdiagnosed as TM. Confirm with spinal angiography; treatable by embolization or ligation; earlier treatment → better recovery.

Case 3. A 24-year-old with a C6 complete SCI develops a pounding headache, flushing above the lesion, and BP 210/110 on the ward.
Diagnosis and management?

Autonomic dysreflexia (≥T6). Sit upright, loosen constrictions, find/remove the trigger — check for blocked catheter/bladder distension and bowel impaction. If BP persists, give a rapid short-acting antihypertensive. Untreated → intracranial hemorrhage/seizures.

Case 4. A 30-year-old presents with subacute leg numbness and unsteadiness; exam shows loss of vibration/proprioception with a positive Romberg. He uses recreational nitrous oxide; serum B12 is normal.
Diagnosis and confirmatory test?

Subacute combined degeneration from nitrous-oxide misuse. N₂O inactivates B12, so serum B12 can be normal — confirm with elevated methylmalonic acid/homocysteine. Stop the exposure and treat with B12; posterior-column signs are the clue.
