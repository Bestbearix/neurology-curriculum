Time is brain. A typical untreated large-vessel ischemic stroke destroys roughly 1.9 million neurons per minute. Every workflow target — door-to-needle <60 min, door-to-puncture <90 min — exists to compress that loss. Treat the clock as a vital sign.

1. Vascular anatomy & localizing syndromes

Stroke localization is pattern recognition applied to arterial territories. The deficit names the territory; the territory narrows the mechanism; the mechanism dictates the workup and prevention. Anterior (carotid) circulation supplies most of the cerebral hemispheres; posterior (vertebrobasilar) circulation supplies the brainstem, cerebellum, thalami, and occipital lobes.

Anterior circulation

The internal carotid feeds the anterior and middle cerebral arteries. The middle cerebral artery (MCA) is the most commonly affected vessel. Its superior division supplies the frontal operculum and motor cortex, producing contralateral face-and-arm-predominant weakness with sensory loss, and, in the dominant hemisphere, a Broca (expressive, non-fluent) aphasia with preserved comprehension. The inferior division supplies temporoparietal cortex; occlusion produces a Wernicke (fluent, comprehension-impaired) aphasia in the dominant hemisphere or hemineglect in the non-dominant hemisphere, often with little or no weakness — a presentation easily mistaken for delirium or a psychiatric event. Deep lenticulostriate perforators supply the internal capsule; their occlusion gives a pure motor hemiparesis affecting face, arm and leg equally.

The anterior cerebral artery (ACA) supplies the medial frontal lobe and the leg area of the motor homunculus, so ACA infarcts cause contralateral leg-greater-than-arm weakness, sometimes with abulia and grasp reflexes. Border-zone (watershed) infarction between major territories arises from global hypoperfusion or proximal stenosis and can produce the "man-in-a-barrel" pattern of bilateral proximal arm weakness; a history of amaurosis fugax (transient monocular visual loss) points to an ipsilateral carotid source.

Posterior circulation

The posterior cerebral artery (PCA) supplies the occipital lobe and medial temporal structures; occlusion causes a contralateral homonymous hemianopia, classically with macular sparing, and in the dominant hemisphere can produce alexia without agraphia. Brainstem strokes produce the diagnostically powerful crossed syndromes — ipsilateral cranial-nerve signs with contralateral long-tract signs:

Cerebellar stroke is a structural trap. A "dizzy, vomiting" patient may harbor a cerebellar infarct that swells over 24–72 h, compresses the brainstem, and causes obstructive hydrocephalus. Low threshold for posterior-circulation imaging, admission, and neurosurgical awareness (suboccipital decompression can be life-saving).

Lacunar syndromes

Small deep infarcts (<15 mm) from lipohyalinosis of single penetrating arteries produce five classic syndromes, all defined by the absence of cortical signs (no aphasia, neglect, or visual-field cut): pure motor hemiparesis (posterior limb of internal capsule or basis pontis), pure sensory stroke (VPL thalamus), sensorimotor stroke (thalamocapsular), ataxic hemiparesis, and dysarthria–clumsy-hand syndrome.

Rule of thumb: if any cortical sign is present, it is not a lacune — pursue a large-vessel or embolic source.

3. Core, penumbra & the tissue clock

Normal cerebral blood flow is roughly 50 mL/100 g/min. Below about 20 mL/100 g/min neurons become electrically silent but remain structurally viable — the ischemic penumbra, the target of reperfusion. Below about 10 mL/100 g/min tissue infarcts irreversibly — the core. The rate at which penumbra converts to core depends on collateral circulation, which is why a favorable imaging profile (small core, large mismatch) can extend the treatment window well beyond the rigid "time clock." This is the conceptual basis for perfusion-based and clinical-mismatch selection in the late window.

4. Hyperacute ischemic management

  1. Establish last known well, compute the NIHSS, and check a fingerstick glucose to exclude a hypoglycemic mimic.
  2. Obtain a non-contrast CT to exclude hemorrhage, CT angiography of head and neck to identify a large-vessel occlusion, and CT/MR perfusion when operating in the late window.
  3. Deliver IV thrombolysis if eligible (≤4.5 h, or MRI mismatch for unknown-onset) — target door-to-needle <60 min.
  4. Pursue mechanical thrombectomy for a large-vessel occlusion within the window — target door-to-puncture <90 min.
  5. Admit to a dedicated stroke unit (an intervention with its own mortality benefit) and begin the TOAST workup.
Blood pressure depends on what you are doing. If giving thrombolysis, lower BP to <185/110 before treatment and maintain <180/105 for 24 h afterward. If not reperfusing, allow permissive hypertension and treat only when BP exceeds 220/120 or there is end-organ injury — abrupt lowering can extend the infarct by reducing penumbral perfusion.

Also attend to glucose (treat hyperglycemia, avoid hypoglycemia), swallowing (NPO until a swallow screen passes to prevent aspiration), and early secondary-prevention initiation once hemorrhage is excluded.

Fever — INTREPID (RCT, provided). Prophylactic fever prevention (targeting 37.0 °C for 14 days with an automated device) reduced fever burden but did not improve functional outcomes versus standard tiered treatment of temperature ≥38 °C. Practical message: treat fever and seek its cause, but routine aggressive prophylactic normothermia is not outcome-improving.

5. IV thrombolysis — evidence & eligibility

NINDS (NEJM 1995) — "Tissue Plasminogen Activator for Acute Ischemic Stroke." IV alteplase 0.9 mg/kg within 3 h gave a global odds ratio for a favorable outcome of 1.7 (95% CI 1.2–2.6) — significantly more patients with minimal or no disability at 3 months. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage within 36 h occurred in 6.4% vs 0.6% (alteplase vs placebo), yet overall outcome favored treatment. This established thrombolysis.
ECASS III (NEJM 2008) — "Thrombolysis with Alteplase 3 to 4.5 Hours after Acute Ischemic Stroke." Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg given 3–4.5 h after onset improved 90-day disability (favorable mRS 0–1) versus placebo, extending the window in selected patients (original protocol excluded age >80, NIHSS >25, anticoagulant use, and prior stroke + diabetes).
Tenecteplase. Higher fibrin specificity and a longer half-life than alteplase permit a single bolus. At 0.25 mg/kg it achieves at least comparable reperfusion and is increasingly preferred, especially before thrombectomy; the provided review summarizes the randomized alteplase comparisons and dose-finding data.
Wake-up / unknown-onset. Per the ESO thrombolysis guideline, MRI DWI–FLAIR mismatch identifies patients last-seen-well >4.5 h who still benefit from thrombolysis.

Practical eligibility

Include: a disabling deficit, onset ≤4.5 h (or qualifying MRI mismatch), age ≥18. Key exclusions: hemorrhage on CT; recent intracranial surgery or significant head trauma within three months; active internal bleeding; BP that cannot be brought below 185/110; platelets <100,000; INR >1.7; therapeutic DOAC use within ~48 h (with normal renal function); and a glucose-correctable mimic. Many "relative" contraindications are individualized — the disabling nature of the deficit weighs against them.

6. Mechanical thrombectomy — evidence & windows

HERMES (Lancet 2016) — individual-patient meta-analysis of MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, SWIFT PRIME, EXTEND-IA, REVASCAT. Thrombectomy for anterior-circulation LVO within ~6 h reduced 90-day disability with an adjusted common odds ratio of 2.49 (95% CI 1.76–3.53; p<0.0001) and a number-needed-to-treat of 2.6 to reduce disability by ≥1 mRS level. Benefit was consistent across age, stroke severity, and occlusion site — one of the largest effects in vascular neurology.
Extended window — DAWN & DEFUSE-3 (per the provided "Extended Window for Stroke Thrombectomy" review). Using perfusion- or clinical-core mismatch to select patients beyond 6 h, pooled 90-day functional-independence rates favored thrombectomy. DEFUSE-3: 6–16 h (perfusion–core mismatch); DAWN: 6–24 h (clinical–core mismatch). Together they created the modern late window.

Guideline anchor (ESO/ESMINT): for anterior-circulation LVO within 6 h, the recommendation is mechanical thrombectomy plus best medical care; selection extends to 6–24 h with perfusion/clinical mismatch imaging. Candidacy requires a proximal occlusion (intracranial ICA, M1, selected M2), a salvageable imaging profile, and reasonable pre-stroke function. Thrombolysis, when indicated, is given alongside and must not delay transfer.

7. Intracerebral hemorrhage

Spontaneous ICH is managed as a distinct emergency. The priorities are to stop the bleed, control pressure, and find the cause. Reverse anticoagulation immediately and agent-specifically: vitamin K plus 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate for warfarin; idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa (or 4-factor PCC where unavailable) for factor Xa inhibitors. Lower systolic blood pressure toward approximately 140 mmHg.

INTERACT2 / ATACH-2 — intensive BP lowering to a target around SBP 140 is safe and reasonable in acute ICH; the more aggressive target below ~120 (ATACH-2) did not improve outcomes and increased renal adverse events.
BP trajectory matters (pooled INTERACT/ATACH-II analysis, provided). Moderate, smooth, sustained SBP within ~120–140 mm Hg in the first hours is associated with better outcomes, whereas large rapid drops within 1 h (e.g., >200 to <140) diminish or even reverse the benefit. Lower deliberately and steadily — not abruptly.
Against hematoma expansion (provided review). The proven levers are early BP control and emergent anticoagulant reversal (idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa or 4-factor PCC for factor Xa inhibitors; vitamin K + PCC for warfarin). Tranexamic acid may modestly reduce expansion; rFVIIa has been studied within ~4 h. Identify expansion-risk markers (e.g., CT "spot sign," early presentation, large baseline volume).

Manage raised intracranial pressure, and involve neurosurgery for cerebellar hemorrhage greater than ~3 cm or with brainstem compression, for obstructive hydrocephalus (external ventricular drain), and for selected accessible lobar clots. Characterize the etiology by location and patient profile: deep (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons) hemorrhage suggests hypertensive arteriopathy; lobar hemorrhage in an older patient suggests cerebral amyloid angiopathy; a younger patient warrants vascular imaging for an underlying malformation, aneurysm, or venous sinus thrombosis.

8. Subarachnoid hemorrhage

A thunderclap headache ("worst of my life," maximal within seconds to a minute) mandates a non-contrast CT; if negative but suspicion remains high, perform a lumbar puncture looking for xanthochromia and a non-clearing red-cell count, because CT sensitivity declines after about six hours. Once aneurysmal SAH is confirmed by CTA or catheter angiography, secure the aneurysm early by endovascular coiling or surgical clipping. Give nimodipine, which improves neurological outcomes (a benefit mediated by neuroprotection rather than a measurable reduction in angiographic vasospasm). Monitor for delayed cerebral ischemia from vasospasm (typically days 4–14), hydrocephalus, seizures, and hyponatremia from cerebral salt wasting.

Minor stroke / high-risk TIA — short-course dual antiplatelet

CHANCE + POINT pooled analysis (provided). Clopidogrel–aspirin started early after minor ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA reduces recurrent stroke. The benefit is concentrated in the first ~21 days while bleeding risk accrues with longer exposure, supporting a short course (≈21 days, up to 90) then single antiplatelet. The provided "Acute Treatment of Disabling and Nondisabling Minor Ischemic Stroke" guidance and the Canadian 2020 secondary-prevention update operationalize this.

AF with concurrent atherosclerosis / stable CAD — anticoagulant monotherapy

OAC ± antiplatelet RCT (provided). In ischemic stroke with AF and atherosclerosis, adding an antiplatelet to anticoagulation did not reduce the composite of ischemic events and major bleeding (17.8% combination vs 19.6% monotherapy) while increasing bleeding — favoring anticoagulant monotherapy. The Edoxaban-in-AF-with-stable-CAD trial reached the same conclusion: edoxaban monotherapy gave a better net outcome than dual antithrombotic therapy.

PFO & cryptogenic stroke

PFO closure (provided review). Roughly half of cryptogenic strokes have a PFO. With good patient selection — younger patients and high-risk anatomy (large shunt, associated atrial septal aneurysm) — transcatheter closure significantly reduces recurrent stroke versus medical therapy alone. Decisions are individualized after specialist (neurology + cardiology) evaluation.
Universal measures, every ischemic stroke: a high-intensity statin to a low LDL target, blood-pressure control, diabetes and smoking management, and a documented TOAST mechanism that justifies the chosen antithrombotic agent.
Anticoagulation timing in AF-related stroke. Larger infarcts carry greater hemorrhagic-transformation risk, so anticoagulation is generally delayed in large strokes and started earlier after a TIA or small infarct. A common heuristic is the "1–3–6–12-day" rule, individualized to infarct size and bleeding risk.

10. Special & young-stroke considerations

In patients under roughly 50, broaden the differential. Cervical artery dissection presents with neck or face pain, a partial Horner syndrome, and sometimes recent trauma or neck manipulation; diagnose with vessel-wall imaging. A patent foramen ovale with paradoxical embolism is supported by a positive bubble study and a high RoPE score, and closure benefits selected patients under 60 after specialist evaluation. Consider antiphospholipid syndrome and malignancy-associated hypercoagulability, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (recurrent thunderclap headache, "string-of-beads" angiography), CNS vasculitis, and genetic causes such as CADASIL, Fabry disease, MELAS, and sickle cell disease. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis deserves special mention: it causes headache, seizures, and infarcts that cross arterial territories (often with hemorrhage), and is treated with anticoagulation even when hemorrhage is present.
