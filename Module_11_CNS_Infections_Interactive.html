Why this module?

CNS infections are the most time-sensitive group of neurological diagnoses. Bacterial meningitis kills within hours; HSV encephalitis destroys temporal lobes within days. Both have effective treatments. The general physician must suspect them early and act before microbiology confirms.

After this module you will:

  • Recognise the two major syndromes — meningitis vs encephalitis — at the bedside.
  • Read a CSF profile and assign a likely category.
  • Choose empirical antibiotics + dexamethasone + aciclovir within the first hour.
  • Know when to image before LP and what counts as a contraindication.
  • Distinguish HSV encephalitis on MRI and start aciclovir empirically.
  • Manage the high-stakes differential of ring-enhancing lesions in HIV.
  • Diagnose TB and cryptococcal meningitis and start appropriate therapy.
  • Recognise autoimmune encephalitis as a treatable cause of cognitive / psychiatric decline.
The single principle that prevents disasters: when you can't exclude bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis with confidence, give empirical antimicrobials + dexamethasone + aciclovir within 60 minutes — before the LP if necessary. Outcome is dictated by time to first dose, not by waiting for confirmation.

Lessons

1. Meningitis vs encephalitis — the bedside split
  • Meningitis: fever + headache + meningismus ± altered mentation. The brain parenchyma is relatively spared (no focal deficits usually).
  • Encephalitis: altered mental status + fever ± seizures ± focal deficits. The brain parenchyma is involved.
  • Brain abscess: behaves like a mass lesion with infection on top — headache (~70%) + focal deficits ± fever (only 50%).

Classic bacterial meningitis triad (fever + headache + neck stiffness) is present in only 41% of adults. Two or more of fever (≥ 38.5 °C), headache, nuchal rigidity, and altered mental status are present in > 95%.

3. Approach in the first hour
  1. Blood cultures × 2.
  2. Empirical antibiotics + dexamethasone + aciclovir within 60 min.
  3. CT head first if IDSA criteria present: immunocompromised · CNS disease history · new-onset seizure · papilloedema · altered LOC · focal deficit. Otherwise LP first.
  4. LP with opening pressure + cell count + glucose (+ serum glucose) + protein + Gram stain + culture + PCR panel + HSV PCR + cryptococcal antigen + AFB if at-risk.
  5. Add doxycycline during tick season.
Antibiotic-induced CSF sterilisation takes longer than people think; cultures can be negative within 2–4 h of antibiotics but cell count / protein / glucose remain abnormal for days. PCR panels remain useful regardless. Never delay treatment for the LP.

5. HSV encephalitis

The treatable encephalitis you must never miss. Most common encephalitis with a specific therapy.

  • HSV-1 reactivation; bilateral asymmetric temporal-lobe involvement.
  • Fever + altered mentation + behavioural change + aphasia + complex partial seizures.
  • MRI: T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in medial temporal lobes, insula, cingulate; haemorrhage; gyriform enhancement.
  • CSF: lymphocytic + often RBCs + ↑ protein + normal glucose. HSV PCR 95–99% sensitive.
  • Aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h × 14–21 days. Start empirically — delay is the strongest predictor of poor outcome.
  • If CSF HSV PCR negative within first 72 h, repeat at 72–96 h.
  • Post-HSE autoimmune encephalitis (often anti-NMDA-R) develops in 5–27% of patients 1–6 weeks after acute illness, especially children.
Triad: fever + altered mentation + temporal-lobe MRI changes = HSE until proven otherwise. Aciclovir is cheap and safe (renal hydration is the main concern); delay costs brain.
6. Brain abscess

Triad (headache + fever + focal deficit) in only ~20%. Fever in only 50%. Most common feature: persistent progressive headache.

Sources: contiguous (sinusitis → frontal; otitis → temporal/cerebellar; dental), haematogenous (endocarditis, cyanotic heart disease, pulmonary AVM), direct (trauma, neurosurgery).

MRI: ring-enhancing lesion with central restricted diffusion (vs tumour which doesn't), thin smooth wall, surrounding oedema.

Treatment: aspiration / drainage by neurosurgery PLUS empirical IV antibiotics — vancomycin + ceftriaxone (or cefepime) + metronidazole for 6–8 weeks; steroids only if significant mass effect.

7. TB meningitis

Subacute (weeks) presentation: fever, headache, weight loss → cranial-nerve palsies (esp. CN VI), confusion, seizures, hydrocephalus. Basal meningitis with hydrocephalus and basal-ganglia vasculitic strokes on imaging.

CSF: lymphocytic (sometimes early neutrophilic), ↓ glucose, ↑↑ protein (very high). Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra ~70–80% sensitive; AFB smear low yield; culture takes weeks.

Treatment: RIPE (rifampicin + isoniazid + pyrazinamide + ethambutol) for 2 months → continuation phase RH for 7–10 months (total ≥ 9–12 months). Adjunctive dexamethasone for 6–8 weeks reduces mortality.

HIV-coinfected: delay ART 2–4 weeks after TB therapy starts (longer than for pulmonary TB) to reduce IRIS.

8. Cryptococcal meningitis (2022 update)

HIV (CD4 < 100 usually), transplant, or immunocompetent (C. gattii). Subacute headache, confusion, raised ICP (a major killer).

CSF: lymphocytic (often surprisingly low in AIDS), ↓ glucose, ↑ protein. India ink ~80%. Cryptococcal antigen (lateral flow) > 95% sensitive.

AMBITION-cm regimen (2022, WHO/IDSA):

  • Induction (14 days): single-dose liposomal amphotericin B 10 mg/kg + flucytosine 100 mg/kg/day + fluconazole 1200 mg/day. (Or amphotericin B deoxycholate + flucytosine × 2 weeks if liposomal not available.)
  • Consolidation: fluconazole 800 mg/day × 8 weeks.
  • Maintenance: fluconazole 200 mg/day until CD4 > 100 for 6 months on ART.

Manage raised ICP with serial therapeutic LPs — drain to OP < 20 cm H₂O. This is the single most important manoeuvre for survival. Lumbar drain or VP shunt if needed.

HIV: delay ART by 4–6 weeks to reduce IRIS.

10. Neurocysticercosis

Larval Taenia solium. Most common parasitic CNS infection worldwide. Endemic in Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa, South/Southeast Asia.

Most common cause of new-onset adult seizures in endemic countries. Also chronic headache, raised ICP, hydrocephalus.

MRI: parenchymal cysts at different stages (vesicular → colloidal → granular → calcified); "starry sky" pattern; scolex sign.

Treatment depends on cyst stage and location:

  • Single viable cyst + seizures: albendazole + steroids + antiseizure medication.
  • Multiple cysts: albendazole + praziquantel + steroids + longer course.
  • Intraventricular: endoscopic surgical removal.
  • Calcified inactive: antiseizure medication only.
  • Subarachnoid (racemose): aggressive long-course antiparasitic + steroids + CSF diversion.

Counsel that antiparasitic drugs may transiently worsen symptoms (treatment-induced inflammation).

11. Autoimmune encephalitis (cross-reference Module 7)
  • Anti-NMDA-R: young women; teratoma in 50% of women 18–45; psychiatric prodrome → seizures → orofacial dyskinesias and choreoathetosis → autonomic instability. First-line steroids + IVIG/PLEX + tumour removal; second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide if needed.
  • LGI1: older men; faciobrachial dystonic seizures (very brief, < 3 s); hyponatraemia; limbic encephalitis MRI.
  • GABA-B, AMPA: limbic, often paraneoplastic (SCLC).
  • Anti-Hu, Ma2, CRMP5: paraneoplastic, intracellular targets, less treatment-responsive.

Always think: subacute (weeks) cognitive/psychiatric/seizure picture + lymphocytic CSF ± OCBs + ± temporal-lobe T2 hyperintensity. Test specific antibodies in CSF and serum. Search for tumour.

Post-HSE autoimmune encephalitis: monitor HSE survivors for new behavioural change, movement disorder, or seizures 1–6 weeks after acute illness.

12. When to refer
  • Emergency: suspected bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis — antibiotics + aciclovir + dexamethasone within 1 hour.
  • Brain abscess with mass effect or new neurological deterioration.
  • Raised ICP in cryptococcal meningitis requiring serial therapeutic LPs.
  • Status epilepticus in any encephalitis.
  • Suspected autoimmune encephalitis with severe presentation.
Public health: notify suspected bacterial meningitis (esp. meningococcal); chemoprophylaxis for close contacts; contact tracing for syphilis, HIV, TB.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 22-year-old college student with 12 hours of fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, and a petechial rash

Temp 39.5, BP 100/60, HR 110. GCS 14 (mild confusion). Photophobia, nuchal rigidity. Petechiae on trunk and extremities. WBC 22,000. CRP 320.

Diagnosis: Meningococcal meningitis (with meningococcaemia) until proven otherwise.

Plan within 60 minutes:

  1. Blood cultures × 2.
  2. Ceftriaxone 2 g IV + vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV + dexamethasone 10 mg IV (give dexamethasone with first dose).
  3. Aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h (until encephalitis features excluded).
  4. No CT needed before LP (no IDSA criteria — GCS 14, no focal deficit, no papilloedema, no immunocompromise) — proceed to LP.
  5. Isolation (droplet precautions × 24 h after first effective antibiotic dose).
  6. Public health notification; chemoprophylaxis for close contacts: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO once for household + kissing contacts + HCW with mouth-to-mouth exposure.
  7. ICU monitoring for septic shock, raised ICP.
Case 2 — A 45-year-old with 3 days of fever, behavioural change, and a complex partial seizure

Found wandering. Confused, dysphasic. Temp 38.6. No focal weakness. MRI: bilateral asymmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the medial temporal lobes and right insula with some haemorrhage. EEG: temporal lobe slowing with intermittent epileptiform discharges. CSF: 120 WBC/µL (90% lymphocytes), RBCs 40, glucose 60 (normal), protein 95.

Diagnosis: HSV-1 encephalitis.

Plan:

  1. Start aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV q8h × 14–21 days empirically (do not wait for HSV PCR).
  2. Continue empirical bacterial meningitis cover until PCR confirms and CSF Gram stain / culture negative.
  3. HSV PCR in CSF — if negative within first 72 h, repeat at 72–96 h.
  4. Antiseizure medication (levetiracetam loading dose).
  5. Monitor renal function (aciclovir nephrotoxicity); maintain hydration.
  6. Counsel family about post-HSE autoimmune encephalitis risk — monitor for relapse 1–6 weeks after acute recovery (new behaviour change, movement disorder, seizures).
Delay in starting aciclovir is the single strongest predictor of poor outcome in HSE. The drug is cheap, safe, and the diagnosis is missed at the cost of brain — start empirically when temporal-lobe MRI + fever + altered mentation come together.
Case 3 — A 30-year-old HIV-positive man (CD4 65) with 2 weeks of headache, fever, and now confusion

Initially mild headache; now worsening, with low-grade fever and reduced consciousness. CT: hydrocephalus. LP after CT: OP 35 cm H₂O, 60 WBC/µL (lymphocytes), glucose 30, protein 80. India ink positive. CSF cryptococcal antigen positive at high titre.

Diagnosis: Cryptococcal meningitis (HIV-associated).

Plan (AMBITION-cm 2022 regimen):

  1. Induction: single-dose liposomal amphotericin B 10 mg/kg + flucytosine 100 mg/kg/day + fluconazole 1200 mg/day × 14 days. (If liposomal not available: amphotericin B deoxycholate + flucytosine.)
  2. Consolidation: fluconazole 800 mg/day × 8 weeks.
  3. Maintenance: fluconazole 200 mg/day until CD4 > 100 for 6 months on ART.
  4. Serial therapeutic LPs to keep OP < 20 cm H₂O (single most important survival manoeuvre). VP shunt or lumbar drain if needed.
  5. Delay ART by 4–6 weeks after starting antifungal therapy (to reduce IRIS).
  6. Avoid corticosteroids unless IRIS or symptomatic raised ICP refractory to LP.
Case 4 — A 60-year-old with cardiac vegetations, fever, and a new focal deficit

Mitral valve endocarditis confirmed (S. aureus). Has new right-sided weakness and persistent headache for 4 days. Afebrile now on antibiotics. MRI brain: 2.5 cm ring-enhancing lesion in left frontal lobe with central restricted diffusion on DWI, surrounding oedema.

Diagnosis: Brain abscess (septic embolic from endocarditis).

Plan:

  1. Continue endocarditis antibiotic regimen with adequate CNS penetration; add metronidazole if not already on it (anaerobic cover).
  2. Neurosurgery referral for stereotactic aspiration / drainage (also gives microbiology).
  3. Empirical: vancomycin + ceftriaxone (or cefepime) + metronidazole; tailor to culture.
  4. 6–8 weeks total IV antibiotic duration; serial MRI to monitor response.
  5. Antiseizure prophylaxis for supratentorial abscess (high seizure risk).
  6. Steroids only if significant mass effect / midline shift (otherwise blunt host response).
Case 5 — A 35-year-old woman with 3 weeks of bizarre behaviour, seizures, and now orofacial dyskinesias

Initially anxiety and insomnia; then psychotic features; admitted to psychiatry; had a seizure; now has prominent orofacial dyskinesias and autonomic instability. CSF: 60 WBC/µL (lymphocytes), normal glucose, normal-to-mildly elevated protein. MRI mostly normal. EEG: extreme delta brush. CSF anti-NMDA-R antibody positive. Pelvic US: 4 cm ovarian mass.

Diagnosis: Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis (paraneoplastic from ovarian teratoma).

Plan:

  1. First-line immunotherapy: high-dose IV methylprednisolone + IVIG or plasma exchange.
  2. Surgical removal of the ovarian teratoma — early tumour removal dramatically improves outcome.
  3. ICU monitoring for autonomic instability (BP swings, arrhythmias, hypoventilation).
  4. Antiseizure medication for seizures.
  5. If inadequate response by 2 weeks: second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide.
  6. Recovery is typically slow (months) but full or near-full in ~80% with adequate treatment.
Case 6 — A 50-year-old man from rural Mexico with new-onset focal seizure

Otherwise healthy. CT shows a single 1 cm cystic lesion with a small bright spot inside ("scolex sign") and surrounding mild oedema in the parietal lobe, plus a couple of small calcifications elsewhere.

Diagnosis: Neurocysticercosis with a viable parenchymal cyst (responsible for seizures) plus inactive calcified cysts.

Plan:

  1. Antiseizure medication (levetiracetam, valproate, or carbamazepine).
  2. For the viable parenchymal cyst with surrounding oedema: albendazole 15 mg/kg/day × 7–14 days + corticosteroids (dexamethasone or prednisone) starting before/with the antiparasitic to control treatment-induced inflammation.
  3. Counsel about transient symptom worsening at start of treatment.
  4. Family screening for taeniasis (stool examination for proglottids / eggs); public health education about handwashing and pork hygiene.
  5. Follow-up imaging at 3–6 months to assess cyst resolution.
