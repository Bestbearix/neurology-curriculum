1 · Big picture: localising weakness from cord to muscle

A patient says "I'm weak." Your first task is to decide where in the motor pathway the lesion lies. Each level produces a distinct pattern (Module 1 covered the basics — this module dives into the peripheral side).

The two domains that distinguish the levels best at the bedside: reflex pattern (UMN ↑ vs LMN ↓) and sensory involvement (peripheral nerve/root vs muscle/NMJ which spare sensation). Once you have those, the differential narrows fast.

Red flags for any patient presenting with weakness: rapidly progressive (< days), dyspnoea, inability to raise head against gravity, bulbar symptoms (chewing, swallowing, talking), inability to ambulate, autonomic instability, new bowel/bladder dysfunction. Any one of these mandates admission and respiratory monitoring.

3 · Approach to suspected neuromuscular disease

History

  • Tempo: seconds-minutes (stroke, complicated migraine, periodic paralysis, conversion); hours-days (GBS, demyelinating MS, mononeuritis multiplex, NMJ crisis, trauma); weeks-months (CIDP, inflammatory myopathy, NMJ disease); years (CMT, slowly progressive myopathy, MND).
  • Distribution: ocular/bulbar (NMJ, myopathy), proximal (myopathy, NMJ), distal (neuropathy, distal myopathies like myotonic), focal (mononeuropathy, plexopathy, radiculopathy, focal MND), generalised symmetric (NMJ, polyneuropathy, GBS, myopathy).
  • Fluctuation: fatigable weakness over the day = NMJ; flares with infection = MS or autoimmune; with cold/heat = channelopathy or MS; with K+ changes = periodic paralysis.
  • Sensory symptoms: tingling, numbness, neuropathic pain → peripheral nerve. Absent sensory symptoms with weakness → NMJ, motor neuron, or muscle.
  • Autonomic: orthostasis, GI dysmotility, sweating, bladder, erectile → small-fiber and autonomic neuropathies (diabetes, amyloid, GBS, Sjögren, paraneoplastic).
  • Bulbar & respiratory: dysphagia, dysarthria, dyspnoea, weak cough → MND, MG, GBS, myopathies.
  • Drug history: statins, fibrates, steroids, colchicine, amiodarone, chemotherapy (vincristine, platinum, taxanes, bortezomib), antibiotics (metronidazole, isoniazid, fluoroquinolones, dapsone, nitrofurantoin), antiretrovirals (didanosine, stavudine), checkpoint inhibitors (immune-related MG/myositis/myasthenia), alcohol.
  • Systemic: diabetes, thyroid, B12, malignancy, paraproteinaemia, HIV, hepatitis, rheumatic disease, weight loss, fever.
  • Family history: CMT (recall pedigree pattern), muscular dystrophy, congenital myasthenia.

Examination — six elements

  1. Bulk & fasciculations.
  2. Tone (flaccid LMN, spastic UMN, myotonia in some myopathies).
  3. Power graded (MRC 0–5) by muscle group; pattern matters more than absolute numbers. Proximal: deltoids, hip flexors. Distal: finger extensors, foot dorsiflexors. Bulbar: jaw, palate, tongue, neck flexion.
  4. Reflexes (graded 0–4+ with plantar response).
  5. Sensation (light touch, pinprick, vibration, proprioception).
  6. Functional tests (Gowers sign for proximal myopathy; ice-pack test for ocular MG; tap percussion for myotonia; pull test; gait).

The first-tier workup for distal symmetric polyneuropathy

Per AAN guidelines, every patient should have: fasting glucose or OGTT, B12 (with MMA if borderline), SPEP/IFE. These three identify the cause in > 80% of treatable polyneuropathies. Add TSH, CBC, renal/liver function, HbA1c. Diabetes + B12 deficiency + paraproteinaemia together account for most workable diagnoses.

Diabetic neuropathy

  • Distal symmetric polyneuropathy is most common — sensory predominant, length-dependent, painful in 25%.
  • Diabetic autonomic neuropathy — orthostatic hypotension, gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction, sweating abnormalities, silent ischaemia.
  • Diabetic amyotrophy (lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy) — severe asymmetric thigh pain followed by weakness and weight loss, often in older patients with relatively well-controlled T2DM; usually monophasic with slow recovery; immune-mediated. IVIG and steroids sometimes used but evidence limited.
  • Treatment of painful diabetic neuropathy: SNRIs (duloxetine), gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin), tricyclics (amitriptyline — cautious in elderly for anticholinergic burden), topical capsaicin or lidocaine. Combination often necessary.

B12-deficiency neuropathy

Sensory ataxia + paresthesias + sometimes pyramidal signs (subacute combined degeneration of dorsal & lateral columns). Macrocytosis often absent. Confirm with low B12 + elevated MMA. Replace IM or oral high-dose. Improvement may be slow and incomplete if treatment delayed.

Other notable acquired neuropathies

  • Vasculitic neuropathy — mononeuritis multiplex pattern, painful, often subacute. ANCA, ANA, ENA, complement, RF, cryoglobulins, hepatitis C; nerve biopsy may be diagnostic. Treat with steroids + cyclophosphamide or rituximab.
  • Paraneoplastic sensory neuronopathy (anti-Hu, often SCLC) — subacute sensory ataxia + pseudoathetosis; treat the underlying tumour; immunotherapy of variable benefit.
  • Amyloid neuropathy — AL (light chain) or hereditary ATTR (transthyretin). Painful small-fibre + autonomic + carpal tunnel; weight loss; cardiac and renal involvement. Hereditary ATTR-PN has approved treatments: tafamidis, diflunisal (TTR stabilisers); patisiran, vutrisiran, inotersen, eplontersen (RNA silencers).
  • HIV-associated — distal symmetric polyneuropathy from HIV itself or older NRTI drugs; also DILS, CIDP-like, mononeuritis multiplex.
  • Leprosy — still globally common; sensory loss with skin patches, thickened nerves (especially ulnar, common peroneal, posterior tibial, greater auricular). Mainstay of treatment is multidrug therapy.
  • Chemotherapy-induced — platinum (cisplatin, oxaliplatin) cause sensory; taxanes both sensory and motor; vincristine pure motor and autonomic; bortezomib painful sensory. Often dose-limiting.

Inherited neuropathies

Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease is the most common. Phenotypes vary; CMT1A (PMP22 duplication) is the prototype — distal weakness, pes cavus, hammer toes, "inverted champagne bottle" calves, distal sensory loss, areflexia, slow nerve conduction. Diagnose by genetic testing; supportive management (PT/OT, orthoses, avoid neurotoxic drugs).

5 · Guillain-Barré syndrome & CIDP

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)

Acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy. Often preceded by infection 1–4 weeks earlier (Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, Mycoplasma, HIV, Zika, SARS-CoV-2, others). Incidence 1–2/100,000/year.

Classical clinical picture (AIDP, the most common form in Western countries)

  • Ascending symmetric weakness from legs upward, evolving over hours to 4 weeks (nadir by 4 weeks by definition).
  • Areflexia early (or hyporeflexia in mild cases).
  • Sensory symptoms (paresthesias, pain) usually mild; objective sensory findings less than motor.
  • Autonomic instability in ~70%: BP swings, arrhythmias, ileus, urinary retention.
  • Respiratory failure in ~25–30%; bulbar involvement.
  • Facial diplegia in ~50%.

Diagnosis

  • CSF: classic albuminocytologic dissociation (elevated protein with normal cell count, usually < 10/µL). Often normal in the first few days; sensitivity rises by week 2. Pleocytosis > 50/µL suggests HIV, Lyme, sarcoid, lymphoma, or another diagnosis.
  • EMG/NCS: demyelinating features (prolonged distal latencies, conduction block, slowed velocities, F-wave prolongation) for AIDP; axonal patterns for AMAN/AMSAN.
  • Anti-ganglioside antibodies (GM1 for AMAN, GQ1b for Miller-Fisher) supportive.
  • Stool culture or serology for recent Campylobacter exposure when relevant.

Treatment

  • IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days or plasma exchange (PLEX) 5 sessions over 1–2 weeks. Both have equal efficacy; not additive. Choice depends on availability, comorbidities, and contraindications.
  • Start within 2–4 weeks of onset (best outcomes within the first 2 weeks).
  • Steroids alone are ineffective and not recommended for GBS.
  • Supportive care: serial respiratory monitoring (FVC, NIF, peak flow); ICU if FVC < 20 mL/kg, NIF less negative than −30 cmH₂O, MIP/MEP failing, bulbar weakness, or autonomic instability. DVT prophylaxis. Pain control. Nutritional support. Physiotherapy from early on.
  • Mortality 3–10%; up to 20% have residual disability at 1 year.
  • Treatment-related fluctuations may occur within 8 weeks of initial nadir; redose IVIG or PLEX as needed.
EGRIS score (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score) and mEGOS (modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score) help predict respiratory failure and 6-month outcome. Key bedside signs of impending respiratory failure: 20/30/40 rule (FVC < 20 mL/kg, MIP less negative than −30, MEP < 40). Don't wait for hypoxia or hypercapnia — intubate semi-electively.

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP)

Chronic counterpart of GBS — symmetric proximal AND distal weakness with sensory involvement, areflexia, course > 8 weeks (relapsing-remitting or progressive). NCS demyelinating. CSF albuminocytologic dissociation. Diagnosis criteria 2021 EAN/PNS.

Treatment: IVIG, PLEX, or corticosteroids are all first-line (unlike GBS where steroids fail). Maintenance often required for years. Subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIG) as maintenance increasingly used. Rituximab, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate for refractory cases.

Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN)

Slowly progressive, asymmetric, purely motor weakness usually starting in distal upper limbs (wrist drop, hand atrophy). Conduction block on NCS at non-entrapment sites; anti-GM1 antibodies positive in ~50%. No upper motor neuron signs — the key feature distinguishing it from ALS. Treat with IVIG; steroids and PLEX can worsen MMN (avoid).

7 · Myasthenia gravis & other NMJ disorders

Myasthenia gravis (MG)

Autoimmune attack on postsynaptic NMJ. Antibodies in 80% against the nicotinic AChR; in another 5–10% against MuSK; LRP4 (1–3%); 5–10% remain "seronegative." Prevalence ~150–250 per million. Female predominance under 40; equal sex incidence after 50.

Clinical features

  • Fluctuating, fatigable weakness. Worse with sustained activity; improves with rest.
  • Ocular onset in 50%: ptosis (often asymmetric), diplopia. Half progress to generalised disease within 2 years.
  • Bulbar: dysphagia, dysarthria, jaw fatigue when chewing, "nasal" voice.
  • Limb: proximal > distal, symmetric.
  • Respiratory: dyspnoea, weak cough; full crisis means ventilatory support.
  • No sensory loss; reflexes preserved.

  • β-blockers
  • Calcium-channel blockers (high dose)
  • Magnesium (especially IV magnesium for eclampsia — known crisis precipitant)
  • Quinine, quinidine, procainamide
  • Muscle relaxants (especially non-depolarising NMBAs; succinylcholine has prolonged effect in MG patients on AChE inhibitors)

  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab) can induce de novo MG, often severe

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)

Pre-synaptic NMJ disorder. Antibodies against voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCC). 60% are paraneoplastic, most commonly with small-cell lung cancer.

  • Proximal weakness, often legs first; strength improves transiently with brief exercise (post-exercise facilitation); the opposite of MG fatigability.
  • Autonomic dysfunction common: dry mouth, erectile dysfunction, constipation.
  • Reflexes diminished but augment after exercise.
  • RNS shows incrementing response at high-frequency (20–50 Hz) stimulation.
  • Treat the cancer if present. Symptomatic: amifampridine (3,4-DAP) 10–80 mg/day in divided doses (FDA-approved); pyridostigmine; immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, azathioprine, rituximab).

Botulism

Pre-synaptic. Botulinum toxin blocks ACh release. Acute descending paralysis starting with bulbar symptoms (diplopia, ptosis, dysarthria, dysphagia) and prominent autonomic features (mydriasis, dry mouth, ileus, urinary retention). Sources: home-canned foods, infant honey, wound botulism (often IV drug users), iatrogenic (over-injection).

Treatment: equine heptavalent antitoxin in adults; BabyBIG (human-derived) in infants. Supportive ICU care; ventilator support may be prolonged. Report to public health authorities (foodborne disease).

8 · Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis & motor neuron diseases

A clinical syndrome of progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons. ALS is the prototype. Median survival 3–5 years from symptom onset; ~10% live > 10 years.

ALS clinical features

  • Combination of UMN and LMN signs in the same body region (Awaji criteria, El Escorial revised). UMN: hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski. LMN: weakness, atrophy, fasciculations.
  • Asymmetric onset, focal: limb (75%), bulbar (25%), respiratory (rare).
  • Bulbar onset: dysarthria, dysphagia, tongue atrophy and fasciculations.
  • Sensory exam normal; ocular movements spared; sphincters preserved until late.
  • ~10% have associated frontotemporal dementia (FTD-ALS overlap; C9orf72 expansion).
  • Family history in 10% (C9orf72 most common; SOD1, FUS, TARDBP).

9 · Myopathies

Clinical features of myopathy

  • Proximal > distal symmetric weakness (exceptions: IBM, distal myopathies, myotonic dystrophy).
  • Difficulty arising from chair, climbing stairs, raising arms above head, walking distances.
  • Gowers sign (using hands to climb up legs) in proximal myopathy of any cause.
  • Sensory exam normal; reflexes preserved until late.
  • CK often (not always) elevated.
  • Myotonia (sustained involuntary contraction with delayed relaxation): myotonic dystrophy types 1 & 2, paramyotonia congenita, hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis.

Muscular dystrophies (selected)

  • Duchenne (X-linked dystrophinopathy) — boys, calf pseudohypertrophy, Gowers sign, loss of ambulation by ~12 years, cardiomyopathy, restrictive lung disease. Modern treatment includes steroids (deflazacort, prednisone), eteplirsen and other exon-skipping antisense oligonucleotides for amenable mutations, gene therapy (delandistrogene moxeparvovec, 2023); cardiac and respiratory support extend life into 30s.
  • Becker MD — milder dystrophinopathy.
  • Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) — CTG expansion in DMPK; distal weakness, grip myotonia, frontal balding, cataracts, conduction blocks, insulin resistance, daytime sleepiness. Type 2 (PROMM): proximal weakness, milder.
  • Facioscapulohumeral (FSHD) — facial & scapular weakness, asymmetric.
  • Limb-girdle muscular dystrophies — heterogeneous group of proximal LGMDs.
  • Oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy — ptosis + dysphagia in older adults.

Metabolic, mitochondrial, congenital myopathies

  • Pompe disease (acid maltase) — treatable with enzyme replacement (alglucosidase alfa, avalglucosidase alfa); proximal weakness + diaphragmatic involvement.
  • McArdle (phosphorylase) — exercise-induced cramps and myoglobinuria.
  • Mitochondrial myopathies (MELAS, MERRF, CPEO, KSS, MIDD) — multisystem; ragged-red fibres on biopsy.
  • Channelopathies (periodic paralyses) — episodic flaccid weakness with K+ shifts; treat the channel disorder.

Statin myalgiaDiffuse aching, often proximal; CK normal or mildly elevated. Very common (5–10%).Continue if mild; trial of different statin or lower dose; check for hypothyroidism, vitamin D, drug interactions

Other drug-induced myopathies

  • Steroid myopathy — chronic high-dose corticosteroid use; proximal weakness; CK normal; muscle atrophy. Improves with steroid taper and exercise.
  • Colchicine — neuromyopathy in renal impairment.
  • Amiodarone — neuropathy + myopathy.
  • Hydroxychloroquine — vacuolar myopathy with curvilinear bodies on biopsy.
  • Antiretrovirals (especially older NRTIs) — mitochondrial myopathy.
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors — myositis (often with overlap MG/myocarditis triad — "3M syndrome"); very high mortality if missed.
  • Alcohol — acute (rhabdomyolysis after binge) and chronic (proximal myopathy).

Critical illness myopathy / neuropathy (ICU-acquired weakness)

Develops after ~1 week of ICU care, especially in sepsis. Diffuse symmetric flaccid weakness; failure to wean from ventilator. Risk factors: prolonged ventilation, sepsis, high-dose steroids, paralytics, hyperglycaemia. EMG: motor predominant axonopathy + myopathic features. Management is supportive: minimise sedation, early mobilisation, treat sepsis, manage glycaemia. Recovery is often slow but largely complete in survivors.

11 · When to refer

Emergency:
  • Acute progressive weakness with dyspnoea, bulbar symptoms, or autonomic instability (GBS, MG crisis, botulism).
  • Suspected myasthenic crisis or impending crisis.
  • Rhabdomyolysis with AKI risk.
  • Acute foot drop in young patient (think peroneal nerve, common peroneal, L5 root, demyelinating).
Routine neurology / neuromuscular clinic: suspected ALS or other motor neuron disease · suspected MG · suspected inflammatory myopathy · CIDP suspected · progressive distal symmetric polyneuropathy with no identified cause after first-tier workup · suspected hereditary neuropathy or myopathy · refractory chronic neuropathy or myopathy on treatment · refractory carpal tunnel after conservative measures · plexopathy.

First-tier workup for distal symmetric polyneuropathy (AAN)

Fasting glucose / OGTT · vitamin B12 ± methylmalonic acid · SPEP / IFE / serum free light chains. Add TSH, CBC, renal/liver, HbA1c. EMG/NCS to confirm pattern (demyelinating vs axonal; multifocal vs symmetric).
