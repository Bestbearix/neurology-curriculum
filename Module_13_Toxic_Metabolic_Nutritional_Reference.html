1. Framework — recognise reversibility

Toxic, metabolic, and nutritional disorders are the great mimics of neurology. They produce delirium, coma, focal deficits, seizures, ataxia, movement disorders, and dementia — and many are fully reversible if recognised and treated promptly. The cost of missing them is high: Wernicke encephalopathy treated late causes Korsakoff psychosis; central pontine myelinolysis follows aggressive sodium correction; permanent extrapyramidal deficits follow chronic manganese, methanol, or carbon-monoxide exposure.

The single principle: in any acute encephalopathy, exclude reversible toxic/metabolic causes before pursuing rarer diagnoses. Glucose, electrolytes, ammonia, ABG, drug history (including OTC, herbal, recreational), trauma, infection, and structural causes — in that order. Empirical thiamine 500 mg IV TID for 2–3 days in any patient with possible alcohol use, malnutrition, hyperemesis, or chronic illness when Wernicke can't be excluded.

2. Bedside approach to acute encephalopathy

Most toxic/metabolic encephalopathies share clinical features that distinguish them from structural disease:

  • Fluctuating attention & cognition (defining feature of delirium).
  • Symmetrical motor signs (paratonia, multifocal myoclonus, asterixis, tremor) — focal deficits suggest a structural lesion until proven otherwise.
  • Symmetric pupil/oculomotor abnormalities (small reactive pupils in metabolic; large or asymmetric in structural/herniation).
  • Preserved brainstem reflexes until very deep coma.
  • Diffuse EEG slowing ± triphasic waves (hepatic, uraemic, hyperammonemic).

3. Hepatic encephalopathy

  • Multifactorial — ammonia (with glutamine accumulation in astrocytes), inflammation, false neurotransmitters, GABAergic tone.
  • West Haven grading: 0 — minimal; I — trivial lack of awareness, sleep–wake reversal; II — lethargy, asterixis, slurred speech; III — somnolent, confused; IV — coma.
  • Precipitants (mnemonic HEPATICS): Hypovolaemia, Electrolytes (hypoK, hypoNa), Protein load (GI bleed, dietary excess), Alkalosis, TIPS / thrombosis, Infection (SBP), Constipation, Sedatives.

Management

  1. Identify and treat precipitant — SBP, GI bleed, hypokalaemia, dehydration, sedatives, constipation.
  2. Lactulose 30–45 mL po q1–2h until soft stools, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools daily. Acidifies the colon, reduces ammonia absorption.
  3. Rifaximin 550 mg po BID after lactulose response — reduces recurrence; usually added long-term after the second episode.
  4. Avoid sedatives. If essential (e.g., agitation in ICU), use the lowest dose of short-acting agent.
  5. Nutrition: do not protein-restrict long-term (worsens sarcopenia); 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein typically tolerated.
  6. Consider liver transplant evaluation for refractory disease.
Ammonia caveat: ammonia helps confirm the diagnosis but doesn't grade severity (poor correlation with West Haven grade); a normal arterial ammonia largely rules out hepatic encephalopathy, but venous ammonia is unreliable (tourniquet, transport, processing artefacts). Do not titrate lactulose to a target ammonia; titrate to mental status and bowel movements.

4. Uraemic encephalopathy and dialysis disequilibrium

  • Uraemic encephalopathy: usually develops when GFR < 10 mL/min, often subacute over weeks. Features: anorexia → fatigue → cognitive slowing → asterixis, multifocal myoclonus, sometimes seizures and coma. EEG: diffuse slowing ± triphasic waves.
  • BUN/urea correlates more closely than creatinine. Symptoms reverse with dialysis or transplant within days.
  • Dialysis disequilibrium: headache, nausea, seizures, encephalopathy during or just after dialysis. Aggressive urea clearance produces an osmotic gradient drawing water into the brain — cerebral oedema. Prevent by short, gentle initial dialysis sessions; treat with mannitol if severe.
  • Dialysis dementia (now rare): subacute progressive dementia, dysarthria, myoclonus, seizures from aluminium accumulation — disappeared after removal of aluminium from dialysate.

5. Electrolyte and osmolar disturbances

Hyponatraemia

  • Acute (< 48 h) and severe (< 125 mmol/L): cerebral oedema, herniation, seizures, coma.
  • Chronic: insidious — falls, confusion, focal deficits in the elderly.
Avoid central pontine myelinolysis (CPM, now called osmotic demyelination syndrome):
  • Correct sodium ≤ 8 mmol/L in 24 h (more cautious in alcoholics, malnourished, K-depleted: ≤ 6 mmol/L).
  • Acute symptomatic severe hyponatraemia (seizures, coma): give 100 mL of 3% saline IV bolus over 10 minutes, may repeat × 2 if symptoms persist (target: rise of 4–6 mmol/L to abolish symptoms).
  • If overcorrection occurs (> 10 mmol/L/24 h), re-lower with D5W ± desmopressin.
  • CPM presents 2–6 days after correction: pseudobulbar palsy, quadriparesis, "locked-in", encephalopathy. MRI: hyperintense pons (later other brainstem and cerebrum sites = EPM = extrapontine myelinolysis).

Hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia

  • Hypernatraemia: lethargy → coma; brain dehydration; subdural/intracerebral haemorrhage in severe cases. Correct slowly (≤ 10 mmol/L/24 h).
  • Hypercalcaemia: drowsiness, headache, depression, psychosis ("stones, bones, abdominal moans, psychic groans"); coma at > 3.5 mmol/L.
  • Hypocalcaemia: tetany, Chvostek, Trousseau, perioral and acral paraesthesiae, seizures, prolonged QT. Always check Mg.
  • Hypomagnesaemia: refractory hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, weakness, tetany, seizures, ventricular arrhythmias (torsades). Common in alcoholism, malnutrition, PPI use.

Glucose and ammonia disorders

  • Hypoglycaemia (< 3 mmol/L) is one of the most common reversible causes of acute neuro deterioration: focal deficits, seizures, coma. Always check glucose. Treat 25 g IV dextrose. Recurrent hypoglycaemia (insulin overdose, sulfonylurea, insulinoma) needs prolonged glucose infusion ± octreotide.
  • Hyperglycaemia: nonketotic hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar coma; focal motor seizures are characteristic and resolve with glucose normalisation, not antiseizure medication.
  • Hyperammonaemia without cirrhosis: valproate (with or without urea-cycle enzyme deficiency unmasked), post-bariatric surgery (urea cycle), inherited urea-cycle defects (OTC, CPS1) in adults. Treat by removing trigger; consider lactulose, rifaximin, dialysis if severe; sodium phenylbutyrate / sodium phenylacetate for urea-cycle defects.

CO₂ narcosis and pulmonary encephalopathy

Chronic CO₂ retention → headache, asterixis, papilloedema (raised ICP from cerebral vasodilation), drowsiness, coma. Treat the underlying lung disease cautiously; sudden correction of hypercapnia can cause CO₂-narcosis-related rebound respiratory failure or post-hypercapnic alkalosis with seizures.

6. Hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (post-cardiac-arrest brain injury)

  • Major determinant of post-cardiac-arrest survival and quality of life. Selective vulnerability of CA1 hippocampus, cortical layers 3 & 5, cerebellar Purkinje, basal ganglia.
  • Targeted temperature management (TTM): 32–36 °C for 24 h after ROSC from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (TTM trial: 33 vs 36 °C equivalent; recent TTM2 trial favoured normothermia/active fever avoidance). Local protocols vary.
  • Multimodal neuroprognostication after rewarming and sedation off, ≥ 72 h post-arrest: bilateral absent N20 SSEPs, bilateral absent pupil + corneal reflexes, status myoclonus on day 1, NSE > 60 μg/L, malignant EEG (burst-suppression with identical bursts, generalised periodic discharges > 50% time, status epilepticus on flat background), diffuse cortical/basal-ganglia cytotoxic oedema on MRI DWI.
  • No single feature alone — combine ≥ 2 robust features and integrate with imaging and EEG.
Common syndromes after hypoxic injury: Lance-Adams syndrome (post-anoxic action myoclonus — survivors), delayed post-hypoxic leukoencephalopathy (apparent recovery → days–weeks later → cognitive decline, akinetic mutism — extensive white matter changes), persistent vegetative state, minimally conscious state, brain death (cross-ref Module 5).

7. Sepsis-associated encephalopathy & PRES

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy

Most common cause of altered mental status in ICU patients. Fluctuating delirium ± multifocal myoclonus ± seizures in patients with sepsis and no localising features. Treat the sepsis; minimise sedatives; reorient frequently; manage pain and sleep (ABCDEF bundle, cross-ref Module 5).

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES)

  • Headache, seizures, visual disturbance (cortical blindness), encephalopathy.
  • Triggers: hypertensive emergency, eclampsia, calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, ciclosporin), chemotherapy (bevacizumab, gemcitabine), TTP/HUS, autoimmune.
  • MRI: bilateral posterior (parieto-occipital) symmetric T2/FLAIR cortical/subcortical hyperintensity, no enhancement, no restricted diffusion typically.
  • Treatment: control BP (target 25% reduction in 1–2 h, then 160/110 mmHg over 24 h), correct triggers, antiseizure medication for seizures. Most reverse within days–weeks.
  • Beware RCVS (reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome) — overlapping precipitants (postpartum, vasoactive drugs); thunderclap headache prominent; segmental vasoconstriction on angiography; nimodipine in some cases.

Management of withdrawal

  • CIWA-Ar–guided benzodiazepines — symptom-triggered diazepam 10–20 mg po (or lorazepam 1–2 mg po/IV) for CIWA-Ar ≥ 8–10.
  • Diazepam (long half-life, smooth taper) preferred in stable hepatic function; lorazepam in severe hepatic disease (no active metabolites).
  • Thiamine 500 mg IV TID for 2–3 days before glucose in any at-risk patient, then 250 mg IM/IV daily for 3–5 days, then oral.
  • Multivitamins (folate), magnesium replacement, glucose if low.
  • Severe withdrawal / DTs: phenobarbital boluses, dexmedetomidine, propofol in ICU.
  • For long-term abstinence: naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram (the latter requires motivation and no liver dysfunction).

Wernicke encephalopathy (WE) and Korsakoff syndrome (KS)

Wernicke encephalopathy is a clinical diagnosis and a medical emergency.
  • Classic triad (only ~16% have all three): encephalopathy + oculomotor signs (nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, esp. lateral rectus / CN VI) + ataxia (gait).
  • Caine criteria (≥ 2 of 4): nutritional deficiency, oculomotor abnormalities, cerebellar signs, altered mental state.
  • Causes other than alcohol: hyperemesis gravidarum, bariatric surgery, prolonged TPN without thiamine, anorexia, malabsorption, dialysis, persistent vomiting, refeeding syndrome, GI malignancy.
  • Treatment: thiamine 500 mg IV three times daily for 2–3 days (Royal College of Physicians regimen), then 250 mg IM/IV daily for 3–5 days, then high-dose oral. Give thiamine BEFORE glucose; glucose without thiamine precipitates Wernicke.
  • Korsakoff syndrome: residual anterograde amnesia + confabulation after the acute Wernicke phase; affects 80% of untreated survivors. Largely irreversible.
  • MRI: symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in mammillary bodies, periaqueductal grey, dorsomedial thalami, tectal plate.

9. Other chronic alcohol-related neurology

  • Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration: gait and lower-limb ataxia > truncal; relative sparing of upper limbs and speech. MRI: superior vermian atrophy. Treat by abstinence + thiamine + nutrition.
  • Alcoholic polyneuropathy: length-dependent painful sensorimotor, distal leg predominant. Mixed nutritional (thiamine, folate) and direct ethanol toxicity. Module 9 cross-ref.
  • Hepatocerebral degeneration (acquired non-Wilsonian): dementia, dysarthria, parkinsonism, choreoathetosis after chronic liver disease.
  • Marchiafava-Bignami disease: corpus callosum demyelination/necrosis in malnourished alcoholics. Encephalopathy, interhemispheric disconnection, seizures. MRI: corpus callosum T2/FLAIR hyperintensity. Some respond to thiamine + B-complex + nutrition.
  • Central pontine myelinolysis (CPM/ODS): after rapid sodium correction in alcoholics, severely malnourished, or hypokalaemic patients. Pseudobulbar palsy, quadriparesis, locked-in. Prevent by slow Na correction (≤ 8 mmol/L/24 h).
  • Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder: microcephaly, growth restriction, characteristic facies (thin vermilion border, smooth philtrum, short palpebral fissures), neurodevelopmental disability. No safe threshold during pregnancy.
  • Tobacco-alcohol amblyopia / nutritional optic neuropathy: bilateral central scotoma; thiamine, B12, folate replacement; abstinence.

10. Opioid intoxication, withdrawal, and overdose

  • Toxidrome: miosis + respiratory depression + decreased mental status (classic triad).
  • Overdose: naloxone 0.4 mg IV/IM/intranasal; repeat q2–3 min titrated to respiration (not consciousness); higher doses needed for fentanyl and analogues (often 2–4 mg or more); reversal lasts 30–60 min — opioid half-life is longer, so observe and consider naloxone infusion.
  • Withdrawal (uncomfortable but not life-threatening unless cardiovascular disease): rhinorrhoea, lacrimation, yawning, piloerection, GI cramping, dilated pupils, anxiety, restless legs.
  • Treatment of opioid use disorder: methadone (clinic-based), buprenorphine (or buprenorphine-naloxone) office-based, extended-release naltrexone. Combine with psychosocial support and overdose prevention (take-home naloxone for patients and family).

12. Other drug-related neurological syndromes

  • Chemotherapy: vincristine (length-dependent painful neuropathy), platinum (cisplatin, oxaliplatin — sensory neuropathy, cold-induced; oxaliplatin acute neurotoxicity), taxanes (paclitaxel — neuropathy), bortezomib (painful neuropathy), ifosfamide (acute encephalopathy reversed by methylene blue), methotrexate (intrathecal — myelopathy; intravenous — leukoencephalopathy), 5-FU (cerebellar syndrome). Immune checkpoint inhibitors: hypophysitis, autoimmune encephalitis, myositis-myocarditis-myasthenia overlap.
  • Lithium toxicity: tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, seizures, NDI; level > 1.5 mmol/L acute or chronic with renal impairment. Treat: stop, IV fluids, haemodialysis if severe (> 4 mmol/L, severe symptoms, or renal failure).
  • Valproate-induced hyperammonaemic encephalopathy: confusion, lethargy, asterixis; carnitine deficiency and urea-cycle interference. Treat: stop valproate; L-carnitine 100 mg/kg IV bolus + 50 mg/kg q6h.
  • Phenytoin toxicity: nystagmus → ataxia → dysarthria → drowsiness with rising levels (use the classic clinical ladder rather than levels alone).
  • Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome: cyclical vomiting + abdominal pain + compulsive hot-water bathing in chronic heavy cannabis use; treat with capsaicin cream + haloperidol + cessation.
  • Nitrous oxide ("whippets") abuse: subacute combined degeneration of the cord from functional B12 deficiency (oxidises cobalamin) — paraesthesiae, ataxia, posterior column signs; treat with parenteral B12 and counselling (cross-ref Module 10).
  • Toluene/inhalant abuse: leukoencephalopathy, cerebellar ataxia, optic neuropathy, dementia, hypokalaemia with renal tubular acidosis.
  • Methanol: anion-gap acidosis + osmolar gap, optic neuropathy ("snowfield blindness"), basal ganglia (putaminal) necrosis. Treat with fomepizole (or ethanol), folate, dialysis.
  • Ethylene glycol: anion-gap acidosis + osmolar gap, oxalate crystals in urine, renal failure, cranial neuropathies after 4–18 days. Treat with fomepizole (or ethanol), pyridoxine, thiamine, dialysis.
  • Carbon monoxide: headache, confusion, syncope, coma; cherry-red skin uncommon. Treat with 100% O₂; hyperbaric O₂ for severe poisoning, pregnancy, persistent symptoms. Delayed neurological sequelae (parkinsonism, cognitive decline, neuropsychiatric) develop weeks later; bilateral globus pallidus T2 hyperintensity classic.

15. Wilson disease

  • Autosomal recessive ATP7B mutation; impaired biliary copper excretion → copper accumulates in liver, brain, cornea, kidney.
  • Presents 5–35 years (rarely later). Three broad presentations:
    • Hepatic: hepatitis, cirrhosis, acute liver failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis (a key feature).
    • Neurological: dysarthria, tremor (wing-beating), dystonia, parkinsonism, ataxia, drooling, "risus sardonicus".
    • Psychiatric: personality change, depression, psychosis.
  • Kayser-Fleischer rings: golden-brown corneal rings on slit-lamp; present in > 95% of patients with neurological Wilson, fewer with pure hepatic presentation.
  • Diagnosis: low ceruloplasmin (< 0.2 g/L), elevated 24-h urinary copper (> 100 µg/24 h), liver biopsy with elevated tissue copper (> 250 µg/g dry weight), ATP7B mutation analysis. Free serum copper elevated.
  • MRI: "face of the giant panda" sign in midbrain; basal ganglia and thalamic T2 changes.
  • Treatment: chelators (penicillamine — but may worsen neurological symptoms transiently; trientine — better tolerated); zinc (induces metallothionein, reduces gut absorption — useful maintenance and presymptomatic relatives); low-copper diet; liver transplant for fulminant or advanced cirrhosis.
  • Screen all first-degree relatives.

16. Mitochondrial and selected inherited metabolic disorders (adult presentations)

  • MELAS (Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy with Lactic Acidosis and Stroke-like episodes): stroke-like episodes not respecting vascular territories (typically posterior); seizures; sensorineural hearing loss; migraine; short stature; ragged-red fibres on muscle biopsy; mtDNA m.3243A>G. Treatment: avoid sodium valproate (worsens), L-arginine for acute stroke-like episodes (Japan-led trials), coenzyme Q10, antiseizure medications.
  • MERRF (Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged-Red Fibres): myoclonus + epilepsy + ataxia + myopathy; mtDNA m.8344A>G.
  • Kearns-Sayre / CPEO: progressive external ophthalmoplegia, ptosis, retinopathy, heart block (KSS); large mtDNA deletions.
  • LHON (Leber hereditary optic neuropathy): bilateral subacute optic neuropathy in young men (m.11778, m.14484, m.3460); idebenone may help m.14484 carriers.
  • Adult-onset leukodystrophies: adrenoleukodystrophy/AMN (X-linked, VLCFA elevated), metachromatic leukodystrophy (ARSA), Krabbe (GALC), CADASIL (Module 2), Alexander, vanishing white matter — usually progressive cognitive/behavioural decline + spasticity + ataxia + leukoencephalopathy on MRI.
  • Porphyria (acute intermittent): severe abdominal pain + autonomic features + neuropathy + psychiatric features + tachycardia + hyponatraemia. Triggers: many drugs (CYP inducers — sulfonamides, barbiturates, oestrogens), fasting, alcohol. Diagnose with urinary porphobilinogen during attack. Treatment: stop precipitants, IV glucose, IV haemin, pain control (avoid barbiturates).

17. When to refer

  • Same day: any unexplained acute encephalopathy after exclusion of reversible causes; suspected NMS, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia; pituitary apoplexy (Module 12); methanol/ethylene glycol/CO poisoning; sodium < 120 mmol/L with seizures or coma; severe alcohol withdrawal/DTs.
  • Within 1 week: suspected Wilson disease (any combination of neuropsychiatric and hepatic features in a young patient); mitochondrial disease workup; toxic neuropathy investigation; suspected porphyria.
  • Routine: chronic alcohol-related neurology after stabilisation; vitamin replacement follow-up; chelation therapy management; addiction medicine referral.
  • Always: poisons centre for any non-routine toxic exposure; hepatology for Wilson disease; clinical genetics for inherited metabolic disorders.
