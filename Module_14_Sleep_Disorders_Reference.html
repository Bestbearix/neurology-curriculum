1. Framework — why sleep matters in a general clinic

Sleep complaints reach the general physician faster than the sleep specialist. They are massively under-recognised: untreated obstructive sleep apnea drives hypertension, stroke, atrial fibrillation, and motor-vehicle accidents; untreated insomnia worsens depression and chronic pain; REM sleep behaviour disorder is a window into pre-clinical Parkinson disease. The cost of missing them is rarely acute but always significant.

  • Sleep complaints split into three big buckets: insomnia (cannot sleep), hypersomnia (sleeps too much / falls asleep in the day), and parasomnia / abnormal behaviour during sleep.
  • OSA can present as any of these — and frequently mimics narcolepsy, RBD, and insomnia. Exclude OSA before pursuing rarer diagnoses.
  • Adults need ~7–9 h sleep on average; chronic short sleep (< 6 h) raises all-cause mortality, cardiovascular events, dementia risk.
The single principle: a thoughtful sleep history — bed partner observation, sleep–wake schedule, daytime sleepiness (Epworth), snoring, pauses, choking, leg movements, dream enactment, nocturnal eating — answers most of the diagnostic question before any test. Two-week sleep diary or actigraphy then refines the picture.

2. Sleep physiology and architecture

Normal sleep architecture (adult)

Stage% nightEEGFeatures
Wake (W)—Low-voltage, fast (alpha when eyes closed)High muscle tone, rapid eye movements
N1~5%Low-voltage, mixed-frequency; vertex sharpsSlow rolling eye movements; hypnic jerks; easily roused
N2~45%Sleep spindles (12–16 Hz) + K-complexesDefining stage of "light sleep"; most of the night
N3 (slow-wave sleep)~25%Delta (0–2 Hz, ≥ 20% of epoch)Most restorative; deepest sleep; growth hormone surge; NREM parasomnias here
REM (R)~25%Low-voltage, sawtooth wavesBursts of rapid eye movements; muscle atonia (except eye and respiratory); dreaming; autonomic instability

Cycles repeat every 90–120 minutes. N3 dominates the first third of the night; REM dominates the last third. Across life: REM declines from ~50% in newborns to ~25% in adults; N3 declines progressively from adolescence to old age.

Sleep–wake regulation (two-process model)

  • Process C (circadian): ~24-hour rhythm from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), entrained by light via the retinohypothalamic tract. Melatonin secretion (pineal) rises in the evening and peaks at ~3 am.
  • Process S (homeostatic sleep drive): builds across wakefulness (adenosine accumulates), dissipates during sleep.

Key neurotransmitter systems

  • Wake-promoting: orexin/hypocretin (lateral hypothalamus — loss in narcolepsy type 1), histamine (TMN), noradrenaline (LC), acetylcholine (PPT/LDT), serotonin (raphe), dopamine.
  • Sleep-promoting: GABA + galanin (ventrolateral preoptic nucleus, VLPO); adenosine; melatonin.
  • REM control: cholinergic "REM-on" neurons (PPT/LDT) and monoaminergic "REM-off" neurons (LC, raphe) — flip-flop switch.
  • REM atonia: brainstem subcoeruleus → ventromedial medullary glycinergic/GABAergic inhibition of α-motor neurons. Loss of this circuit causes REM sleep behaviour disorder.

3. Bedside approach to a sleep complaint

Eight questions that organise the visit

  1. What is the main problem — falling asleep, staying asleep, daytime sleepiness, or abnormal behaviour at night?
  2. What is your sleep schedule on workdays vs days off? (Reveals circadian misalignment, social jet lag.)
  3. How long does it take to fall asleep, how many awakenings, total sleep time? (Insomnia phenotype, sleep efficiency.)
  4. Do you snore? Has anyone seen breathing pauses or gasping? Have you woken up choking? (OSA.)
  5. Do you have an irresistible urge to move your legs, especially in the evening? Does movement relieve it? (RLS.)
  6. Have you ever fallen asleep at the wheel or in a conversation? (EDS — quantify with Epworth.)
  7. Do you act out your dreams — punching, kicking, shouting, falling out of bed? (RBD — synucleinopathy prodrome.)
  8. Caffeine, alcohol, prescription / OTC / recreational drug use, electronics, exercise pattern, daytime naps, shift work?

Examination essentials

  • Vitals — BP, BMI, neck circumference (> 17 in / 43 cm men, > 16 in / 41 cm women — OSA risk).
  • Upper airway — modified Mallampati class, tonsil size, retrognathia, septal deviation, nasal patency.
  • General — heart failure signs (CSA / Cheyne-Stokes), thyroid (hypothyroidism worsens OSA), depression screen (PHQ-9).
  • Neurological — focus on parkinsonism (link to RBD), cognitive screen, autonomic signs.

Defining OSA terms

  • Apnea: > 90% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10 s. Obstructive = effort preserved; central = no effort; mixed = both.
  • Hypopnea: ≥ 30% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10 s with either ≥ 3% (AASM) / ≥ 4% (Medicare) desaturation or arousal.
  • Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI): events per hour of sleep. 5–14 mild; 15–29 moderate; ≥ 30 severe.
  • Respiratory Disturbance Index (RDI): AHI + respiratory effort-related arousals (RERAs).
  • Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI): desaturations ≥ 3 or 4% per hour.

5. Insomnia disorder

  • Difficulty initiating, maintaining, early-morning waking, or non-restorative sleep ≥ 3 nights/week for ≥ 3 months despite adequate opportunity + daytime consequences. ICSD-3-TR / DSM-5-TR.
  • Up to 30% of adults have insomnia symptoms; ~10% chronic insomnia disorder.
  • Always exclude / treat comorbid conditions first: OSA, RLS, depression / anxiety, chronic pain, caffeine/alcohol/stimulants, drugs (β-blockers, steroids, decongestants, SSRIs at start), hyperthyroidism, GORD, prostate.

Treatment

CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) is first-line and the only insomnia treatment with durable benefit after discontinuation. Components:
  • Stimulus control: bed for sleep + sex only; leave bed if not asleep within ~20 min and return only when sleepy; consistent wake time.
  • Sleep restriction therapy: limit time in bed to actual sleep time to increase sleep drive; gradually expand as efficiency rises > 85–90%.
  • Cognitive restructuring: address catastrophising about sleep loss.
  • Sleep hygiene: a useful adjunct but insufficient alone.
  • Relaxation: progressive muscle relaxation, paced breathing.
Digital CBT-I apps and brief 4-session CBT-I are evidence-based when in-person therapy is unavailable.

6. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)

  • Prevalence: ~14% of men, ~5% of women in middle age (likely higher with current obesity rates).
  • Pathophysiology: upper airway collapses during sleep → arousal → fragmented sleep + hypoxaemia + autonomic activation.
  • Risk factors: obesity (BMI > 30), male sex, age, post-menopausal in women, neck circumference, retrognathia, large tonsils, nasal obstruction, hypothyroidism, acromegaly, Down syndrome.
  • Clinical: loud habitual snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping/choking arousals, non-restorative sleep, morning headache, daytime sleepiness (or fatigue without sleepiness, especially in women), poor concentration, mood changes, nocturia.
  • Consequences: hypertension (especially treatment-resistant), atrial fibrillation, stroke, coronary disease, type 2 diabetes worsening, pulmonary hypertension, MVA risk, perioperative complications.

Diagnosis

  • STOP-BANG ≥ 3 → diagnostic test. Home sleep apnea test (HSAT) for uncomplicated suspected moderate-severe OSA. In-lab PSG for complex patients (CHF, CSA suspected, hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease, drug-resistant hypertension with negative HSAT, suspected non-OSA sleep disorder).
  • AHI 5–14 mild; 15–29 moderate; ≥ 30 severe.

Treatment

  1. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) — first-line for moderate to severe OSA. Titrated by autoPAP or in-lab. Adherence (≥ 4 h/night ≥ 70% nights) is the key challenge — humidification, mask fit, troubleshooting, behavioural support all matter.
  2. Bi-level (BiPAP) — for CPAP intolerance, high pressures, coexistent hypoventilation.
  3. Mandibular advancement device — mild-moderate OSA, CPAP-intolerant; needs adequate dentition.
  4. Weight loss — 10% body weight reduction → ~25–30% AHI improvement; bariatric surgery for severe obesity.
  5. Positional therapy for predominantly supine-related OSA.
  6. Surgery: tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy in children with adenotonsillar hypertrophy is first-line. UPPP, maxillomandibular advancement, septoplasty in select adults. Tracheostomy reserved for life-threatening OSA refractory to other care.
  7. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Inspire, FDA-approved): selected moderate-severe OSA, BMI < 32 (some programmes < 35), CPAP-intolerant, no concentric palatal collapse on DISE.
  8. Modafinil / armodafinil / pitolisant / solriamfetol for residual EDS despite adequate CPAP adherence.
  9. Treat comorbidities: hypothyroidism, allergic rhinitis, GORD.
  10. Driving: counsel to not drive when sleepy; legal reporting obligations vary by country.
CPAP improves daytime symptoms, mood, and BP; large RCTs (SAVE, ISAACC) did not show cardiovascular event reduction in non-sleepy OSA — most clear benefit is for sleepy patients, severe OSA, and treatment-resistant hypertension.

7. Central sleep apnea (CSA), Cheyne-Stokes & hypoventilation

  • Cheyne-Stokes respiration: crescendo-decrescendo respiration with apnoeas, classically in heart failure (~30–50% of HF with reduced EF). Treat heart failure; CPAP often helps; adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) is contraindicated in HFrEF with LVEF ≤ 45% (SERVE-HF trial: increased cardiovascular mortality).
  • High-altitude periodic breathing: physiological at altitude; acetazolamide effective.
  • Opioid-induced CSA: classic ataxic / Biot breathing; ↓ opioid; consider naltrexone; CPAP / ASV variable success.
  • Treatment-emergent CSA (formerly "complex sleep apnea"): emerges on CPAP initiation; resolves spontaneously in many; ASV may help if persistent.
  • Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian): BMI ≥ 30 + daytime PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg without alternative cause; treat with BiPAP + weight loss; high mortality untreated.
  • Neuromuscular hypoventilation (ALS, muscular dystrophies — Module 9): nocturnal NIV improves symptoms and survival; trigger criteria include orthopnea, FVC < 50% predicted, sniff nasal inspiratory pressure (SNIP) < 40 cm H₂O.

8. Narcolepsy & idiopathic hypersomnia

Narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy)

  • Loss of hypothalamic orexin (hypocretin)-producing neurons; HLA-DQB1*06:02 in > 95%; autoimmune (post-H1N1 / Pandemrix association).
  • Onset usually in second decade.
  • Tetrad: excessive daytime sleepiness (universal); cataplexy (sudden brief loss of muscle tone triggered by emotion, especially laughter, lasting seconds–minutes, consciousness preserved — pathognomonic); sleep paralysis; hypnagogic / hypnopompic hallucinations.
  • Disrupted nocturnal sleep, automatic behaviour, vivid dreams in short naps, refreshing naps.
  • MSLT: mean sleep latency ≤ 8 min + ≥ 2 SOREMPs.
  • CSF orexin-A < 110 pg/mL confirms.

Narcolepsy type 2 (without cataplexy)

  • Less consistent orexin loss; MSLT criteria as above; CSF orexin usually normal; some patients later develop cataplexy and are reclassified.

Idiopathic hypersomnia

  • Chronic EDS with long unrefreshing naps, severe sleep inertia ("sleep drunkenness"), often long total sleep time.
  • MSLT: mean sleep latency ≤ 8 min + < 2 SOREMPs or total 24-hour sleep ≥ 11 hours documented.

9. REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) & the synucleinopathy prodrome

  • Loss of normal REM-sleep atonia → dream enactment behaviour: punching, kicking, shouting, jumping out of bed; often violent; vivid action / threat content.
  • Bed-partner reports first; injury to self or partner common.
  • Predominantly males > 50.
  • PSG: REM sleep without atonia + clinical history of dream enactment.
  • Conditions to differentiate from RBD: NREM parasomnias (occur in first half of night, no dream recall, sit-up confused), nocturnal seizures (stereotyped, brief), severe OSA (respiratory arousals with motor activity), trauma-related nightmares.
Isolated (idiopathic) RBD is a prodromal synucleinopathy.
  • Up to 80% of patients with isolated RBD convert to Parkinson disease, Lewy body dementia, or multiple system atrophy within 10–15 years.
  • Counsel sensitively at diagnosis; offer surveillance (annual cognitive, motor, autonomic exam); avoid alarmism, but enable preparation and ongoing research participation.
  • Secondary RBD: antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, mirtazapine, TCAs), narcolepsy, structural brainstem lesions, post-traumatic.

Treatment of RBD

  1. Bedroom safety first: pad sharp corners, move bedside tables, mattress on floor, partner sleeps separately if injury risk.
  2. Melatonin 3–12 mg at bedtime — first-line in many guidelines (better tolerability, modest evidence).
  3. Clonazepam 0.25–1 mg at bedtime — historically first-line; falls in elderly, daytime sedation, cognitive effects, worsens OSA.
  4. Stop or reduce SSRI / SNRI if implicated.
  5. Refer to sleep clinic + neurology for surveillance.

10. Restless legs syndrome (RLS) & periodic limb movement disorder (PLMD)

RLS — IRLSSG diagnostic criteria (all five)

  1. Urge to move legs, usually with uncomfortable sensations.
  2. Rest worsens (sitting / lying).
  3. Gets better with movement (at least partially, as long as activity continues).
  4. Evening / night worsening.
  5. Not solely explained by another condition (leg cramps, positional discomfort, arthritis).

Workup

  • Serum ferritin (with TSAT) — iron deficiency is the most important reversible cause. Target ferritin ≥ 75 ng/mL (some recommend ≥ 100) AND TSAT > 20%; supplement with oral iron (ferrous sulfate + vitamin C; or alternate-day dosing) or IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose) for refractory or severe cases.
  • Exclude pregnancy, ESRD, neuropathy (especially small-fibre), MS (Module 8), drugs (antihistamines, antidepressants — especially mirtazapine, SSRIs/SNRIs, dopamine antagonists, lithium).

Periodic limb movement disorder

Stereotyped repetitive leg movements during sleep (PLMS); diagnosis requires PSG-documented PLMS + clinical sleep disturbance not explained otherwise. Often overlaps with RLS. Iron and gabapentinoids first-line.

11. Parasomnias

NREM parasomnias (disorders of arousal — from N3)

  • Sleepwalking (somnambulism), sleep terrors, confusional arousals.
  • First third of the night; eyes open but confused; no recall; can return to sleep; family history common.
  • Triggers: sleep deprivation, alcohol, fever, OSA, stress.
  • Management: safety (lock doors/windows, alarms), trigger avoidance, treat OSA; pharmacotherapy (clonazepam) only for high-injury risk.

REM parasomnias

  • RBD (above).
  • Sleep paralysis: inability to move at sleep onset (hypnagogic) or wake (hypnopompic), seconds–minutes; may co-exist with narcolepsy or be isolated; reassurance, regularise sleep, treat OSA.
  • Nightmare disorder: distressing dreams from REM; PTSD-related → image rehearsal therapy first-line; prazosin can reduce trauma-related nightmares.

Other parasomnias

  • Sleep-related eating disorder: complex eating behaviour during partial arousal; OSA, RLS, zolpidem-associated.
  • Sexsomnia: sexual behaviour during partial arousal from NREM; medico-legal implications.
  • Exploding head syndrome: sudden loud sound at sleep onset; benign; reassurance.
  • Hypnic jerks / sleep starts: physiological.
NREM vs RBD differentiation: NREM parasomnia → first half of night, no dream recall, sit-up confused, family history common, EEG slow-wave arousal. RBD → second half / REM-dense periods, vivid dream recall, dream content matched to movements, older male, PSG: REM without atonia.

13. Kleine-Levin syndrome & recurrent hypersomnias

  • Episodic severe hypersomnia (15–20 h/day), cognitive disturbance ("dreamy" state), hyperphagia, hypersexuality, derealisation — over days–weeks, with intervals of normal function.
  • Onset typically adolescence; male predominance.
  • Self-limiting over years in most; supportive care; lithium has some evidence for prevention.

14. Sleep in neurological disease

Parkinson disease (Module 6)

  • RBD (often predates motor onset by years), insomnia, EDS, "sleep attacks" on dopamine agonists, RLS, OSA, vivid dreams.
  • Treat motor fluctuations affecting nocturnal mobility; avoid antihistamines; treat RBD (melatonin first); modafinil for EDS; treat OSA.

Dementia / Alzheimer disease (Module 7)

  • Sundowning (evening agitation), fragmented sleep, irregular sleep-wake rhythm.
  • Non-pharmacological first: structured day, daylight, exercise, social interaction, sleep hygiene; avoid benzodiazepines.
  • Trazodone, low-dose mirtazapine, melatonin used cautiously.

Stroke (Module 2)

  • OSA very common after stroke (~50%); CPAP improves outcomes in selected patients.
  • Insomnia, post-stroke fatigue (distinct from EDS).

Multiple sclerosis (Module 8)

  • RLS more common; fatigue distinct from EDS; nocturnal spasms; central hypersomnia rare with hypothalamic lesions.

Epilepsy (Module 3)

  • Sleep deprivation precipitates seizures; nocturnal seizures (esp. frontal-lobe) misdiagnosed as parasomnia. Video EEG monitoring helps differentiation.
  • Many AEDs affect sleep (sedation, weight gain → OSA).

15. When to refer

  • Same-week: suspected narcolepsy / IH; significant cataplexy; suspected RBD; OSA with treatment-resistant hypertension, motor-vehicle / occupational safety concerns, atrial fibrillation, or stroke; severe RLS with augmentation.
  • Routine: insomnia not responsive to CBT-I; uncomplicated suspected OSA via HSAT pathway; circadian disorders not responding to first-line measures.
  • Always: video-EEG referral for stereotyped nocturnal behaviour suggestive of seizures; cardiology / heart failure team for CSA/Cheyne-Stokes patients.
