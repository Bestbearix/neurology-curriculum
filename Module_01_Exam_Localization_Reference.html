1 · The clinical method

Adams & Victor's defines the clinical method as a five-step process. It is unchanged across editions because it works.

  1. Secure the symptoms and signs by history and examination.
  2. Interpret them in terms of physiology and anatomy — identify the function disturbed and the structures implicated.
  3. Localize: name the part(s) of the nervous system affected (the anatomic diagnosis). Recognise the resulting syndrome.
  4. From anatomy + tempo + course + past history + investigations, deduce the etiologic diagnosis.
  5. Estimate the functional impact and the prognosis.

Merritt's poses the same logic as four questions, asked simultaneously by experienced clinicians:

Four questions: (1) What anatomic structures are affected? · (2) What is the nature/syndrome of the disorder? · (3) What are the most likely etiologies? · (4) What "can't miss" diagnosis must be excluded?

2 · The neurologic history

The history alone establishes the diagnosis in the majority of neurologic encounters; the exam confirms it. Take it before you reach for imaging.

Five essentials of a neurologic history

ElementWhy it matters
Timing & tempo Speed of onset and course is the single best clue to mechanism. Seconds → vascular or seizure; minutes–hours → migraine, demyelination, toxic; days → infection, GBS; weeks–months → tumor; years → degenerative.
Quality of the experience "Dizzy" must become vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, or psychogenic. "Numb" must become loss of sensation, paresthesia, or weakness. Never accept a patient's neurologic label at face value.
Aggravating / alleviating Postural change (orthostasis), exertion (claudication, MG fatigability), heat (Uhthoff in MS), Valsalva (raised ICP), positions of head/neck (BPPV, vertebral dissection).
Associated symptoms Headache + focal deficit; weakness + bowel/bladder; vertigo + hearing loss; tremor + slowness — these clusters define syndromes.
Observable behaviour Always corroborate with a witness for: seizures, syncope, dementia, sleep events. Patients are unreliable narrators of their own loss of awareness.
Pearl: Both texts emphasise that the most common cause of a missed diagnosis is failure to consider it. Start with a broad differential and prune; do not start narrow.

3 · The neurologic examination

The standard sequence is top-down: mental status → cranial nerves → motor → reflexes → sensation → coordination → gait. The same order keeps you from forgetting components and lets you build localization as you go.

Screening vs comprehensive exam

A general physician should be fluent in the screening exam (5–7 minutes) and able to escalate to the comprehensive exam when the history demands it.

Mental status

Mental status is built of interdependent domains: arousal, language, attention, memory, visuospatial, executive, and higher-order function. Test arousal first — everything else assumes a wakeful patient. Always document the actual response, not just whether it was right or wrong.

Language — the seven components

Fluency · prosody · repetition · naming · comprehension · reading · writing. The pattern across these seven tells you the type and site of aphasia.

Practical tip: If the patient can repeat normally, the perisylvian language network is intact — think transcortical aphasia, often watershed ischemia or hypoxic injury.

Memory

Working (immediate, e.g. digit span), short-term (delayed recall after 3–5 minutes), and long-term (autobiographical, semantic). Test recall before recognition; a patient who fails free recall but succeeds with cueing has a retrieval (frontal/subcortical) pattern; one who fails both has a storage (hippocampal) pattern — the latter is the Alzheimer signature.

Other domains in brief

  • Attention: serial 7s, months backward, digit span.
  • Visuospatial: clock-drawing, intersecting pentagons, cube copy. Be aware of education effects.
  • Executive: Luria fist-edge-palm, alternating patterns, verbal fluency (list animals in 60 s — normal >15).
  • Higher order: proverb interpretation, judgment scenarios.
  • Praxis: ideomotor ("show me how to wave goodbye") and ideational ("show me how you would brush your teeth"). Impaired without weakness suggests dominant parietal lesion.

Cranial nerves I–XII

Examine in order I → XII, but describe in functional groups (vision, eye movements, lower face, bulbar, etc.) rather than nerve numbers.

HINTS exam pearls (acute vertigo): A central cause is suggested by an abnormal head-impulse (smooth eyes, no corrective saccade), direction-changing nystagmus, or skew deviation. Three reassuring HINTS features outperform early MRI for posterior-circulation stroke.

Motor system

Examine in this sequence: observation (posture, involuntary movement) → bulk → tone → strength → functional tests (drift, gait). Always compare left to right and proximal to distal.

Tone

  • Spasticity — velocity-dependent, "clasp-knife," UMN lesion (corticospinal).
  • Rigidity — velocity-independent, "lead-pipe" or "cogwheel" (Parkinsonism, basal ganglia).
  • Paratonia (gegenhalten) — variable resistance, frontal lobe / dementia.
  • Flaccidity — absent tone, LMN or acute UMN (spinal shock).

Sensation

Six modalities matter: light touch, pinprick, temperature, vibration, proprioception, cortical sensation. Pain & temperature travel in the spinothalamic tract (crosses near entry into the cord); vibration and proprioception travel in the dorsal columns (crosses in the medulla). Light touch uses both. This is why a hemicord (Brown-Séquard) lesion produces ipsilateral dorsal-column loss and contralateral pain/temperature loss below the level.

Coordination

Test rapid alternating movements, finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin, and rebound. Cerebellar disease produces ipsilateral findings: dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, hypotonia, and scanning dysarthria. Midline (vermis) lesions cause trunk & gait ataxia with relatively preserved limb coordination; hemispheric lesions cause limb ataxia on the same side.

4 · Localization — the heart of neurology

Localization is the discipline that gives neurology its diagnostic precision. Work it out before you order imaging: a study ordered without a localization is a study without a question.

A practical localization algorithm

  1. Is the lesion central or peripheral? Hyperreflexia, upgoing toes, spasticity, cognitive or cranial-nerve signs → central. Hyporeflexia, atrophy, fasciculations, dermatomal sensory loss → peripheral.
  2. If central: cortex, subcortex, brainstem, cerebellum, or cord?
    • Cortex: language, neglect, focal seizures, cortical sensory loss.
    • Subcortex / internal capsule: dense hemiparesis without cortical signs.
    • Brainstem: cranial-nerve signs ipsilateral + long-tract signs contralateral.
    • Cerebellum: ipsilateral ataxia without weakness.
    • Spinal cord: sensory level, bilateral long-tract signs below a level, sphincter involvement.
  3. If peripheral: root, plexus, nerve, NMJ, or muscle?
    • Root: dermatomal sensory + myotomal motor, often with pain.
    • Plexus: multi-root, multi-nerve in one limb.
    • Single nerve: classic nerve territory (e.g. ulnar, median, peroneal).
    • Polyneuropathy: symmetric, length-dependent.
    • NMJ: fatigable, ocular/bulbar, no sensory loss.
    • Muscle: proximal > distal, symmetric, no sensory loss, reflexes preserved early.

Classic syndromes on the exam alone

  • Horner's syndrome — partial ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis. Localizes to the sympathetic chain (hypothalamus → T1 root → carotid plexus). Painful Horner = carotid dissection until proven otherwise.
  • Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) — impaired adduction with abducting-eye nystagmus. Lesion of the ipsilateral MLF.
  • Argyll Robertson pupil — accommodates but does not react to light. Neurosyphilis.
  • Adie tonic pupil — sluggish, large, with absent DTRs. Benign, autonomic ganglion.
  • Gerstmann syndrome — agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia, left-right disorientation. Dominant inferior parietal.
  • Anton's syndrome — cortical blindness with denial. Bilateral occipital.
  • Foster Kennedy — ipsilateral optic atrophy + contralateral papilledema. Frontal mass.

5 · Red flags & when to refer

Refer urgently (same day / emergency):
  • Sudden focal deficit lasting >a few minutes (stroke / TIA window).
  • Thunderclap headache — "worst headache of life," peak in <1 min.
  • New headache + fever and/or neck stiffness.
  • New severe headache after age 50, especially with jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, or visual symptoms (giant-cell arteritis).
  • Status epilepticus or first-ever seizure.
  • Acute spinal cord syndrome: bilateral leg weakness, sensory level, or new bowel/bladder dysfunction.
  • Acute ascending weakness with areflexia (Guillain-Barré).
  • Rapidly progressive bulbar weakness, dyspnea, or dysphagia (MG crisis, GBS).
  • Sudden monocular vision loss (consider arteritic AION, central retinal artery occlusion, optic neuritis).
  • Acute vertigo with central HINTS features.
  • Painful third-nerve palsy with dilated pupil (PCom aneurysm).
  • Cauda equina symptoms.
Refer routinely: progressive cognitive decline; chronic headache not meeting primary headache criteria; tremor or movement disorder; chronic neuropathy needing aetiologic workup; refractory epilepsy; suspected MS; suspected motor neuron disease.
