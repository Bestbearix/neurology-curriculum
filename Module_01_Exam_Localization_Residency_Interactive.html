From checklist to localization

The general-physician Module 1 teaches a reliable screening exam. This enriched version trains the exam as the experiment that tests a localization hypothesis. The history asks where and what; the examination answers where; tempo answers what.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Run a structured top-down exam and justify what each manoeuvre localizes.
  2. Classify aphasia and non-dominant-hemisphere syndromes.
  3. Separate upper- from lower-motor-neuron weakness at the bedside.
  4. Read gait as a localizing sign; interpret Romberg correctly.
  5. Place a lesion at a level of the neuraxis, including crossed brainstem signs and cord syndromes.
  6. Distinguish organic patterns from non-anatomical (functional) findings.

The diagnostic method

Key points
  • Two questions: localize (where?) then characterize (what?).
  • Tempo is the best aetiology clue — hyperacute → vascular/seizure; subacute → inflammatory/infectious/neoplastic; chronic → degenerative/hereditary/compressive.
  • History and exam are complementary; the exam confirms or refutes the history's hypothesis.
TempoMechanismExamples
Hyperacute (secs–mins)Vascular, seizureStroke, ICH, Todd paresis
Acute–subacute (hrs–weeks)Inflammatory, infectious, neoplasticGBS, encephalitis, abscess, tumour
Chronic (months–years)Degenerative, hereditary, compressiveALS, CMT, spondylosis

Reading the history for localization

  • Interrogate "sudden": maximal-at-onset (vascular) vs march over minutes (seizure/aura) vs days (mass/inflammation).
  • Distribution: focal/multifocal/diffuse; proximal vs distal; length-dependent vs patchy; crossed.
  • Positive vs negative symptoms: loss of function (destruction) vs tingling/jerking (abnormal excitation).
If the exam contradicts the story, re-take the history rather than forcing the findings to fit.

The examination — top-down

Key points
  • Uniform order: mental status → cranial nerves → motor → reflexes → coordination → sensation → gait.
  • Cranial-nerve findings are localizing gold; forehead-sparing facial weakness = UMN, whole-hemiface = peripheral VII.
  • Find subtle weakness with pronator drift; the Babinski sign marks corticospinal damage.
Mental status & higher cortical function

Screen: orientation, digit span, serial 7s, three-item recall at 3 min. Characterize speech (fluent vs non-fluent), then comprehension, repetition, naming, reading, writing. Test praxis, visuospatial ability (clock/figure copy/line bisection), gnosis. Re-emergent primitive reflexes (grasp, snout, glabellar) → frontal disease.

Cranial nerves — localizing gold
  • Visual fields: monocular = pre-chiasmal; bitemporal = chiasm; homonymous = retrochiasmal.
  • Pupils/lids/eye movements; look for CN III palsy (down-and-out, dilated pupil), Horner, INO.
  • Facial: forehead-sparing = central (UMN) VII; whole-hemiface = peripheral VII.
  • Tongue deviates toward the weak (LMN) hypoglossal side; watch for atrophy/fasciculation.
Motor, tone & reflexes

Bulk, tone, power (MRC 0–5), involuntary movements. Tone: spasticity (velocity-dependent, corticospinal) vs rigidity (cogwheel, extrapyramidal). Pronator drift and forearm orbiting reveal mild pyramidal weakness. Babinski and slow triple-flexion both signify corticospinal damage. Root reflexes: biceps C5–6, triceps C7, patellar L3–4, Achilles S1.

Sensation & coordination

Dorsal columns = vibration/proprioception (ipsilateral); spinothalamic = pain/temperature (contralateral, crosses in 1–2 segments). Test sensation last and briefly; map deficits to dermatome, nerve, level, or hemibody. Coordination interpretable only with adequate power; cerebellar signs are ipsilateral.

Aphasia & cortical syndromes

Key points
  • Classify by fluency → comprehension → repetition.
  • Repetition is the discriminator: impaired → Broca/Wernicke/conduction; spared → a transcortical aphasia.
  • Non-dominant lesions cause neglect, extinction, anosognosia, dressing/constructional apraxia — not aphasia.

The first decision: UMN vs LMN

Key points
  • This single distinction splits the neuraxis in two and drives the workup.
  • Acute caveat: early UMN lesions (stroke, "spinal shock") can be flaccid/areflexic before spasticity evolves.
  • Mixed UMN + LMN without sensory loss → motor neuron disease.

Localizing along the neuraxis

Key points
  • Crossed signs (ipsilateral face + contralateral body) = brainstem; CN III→midbrain, VI/VII→pons, IX–XII→medulla.
  • Cortical sign present? Not a lacune. Equal face/arm/leg with no cortical signs → internal capsule.
  • A sensory level = spinal cord; fatigability = NMJ; symmetric proximal, sensation spared = muscle.

Case-based localization

Case 1. Right hand/arm weakness with non-fluent, effortful speech but preserved comprehension. Face and arm more than leg. Brisk right reflexes, right Babinski.
Localize it.

UMN + a cortical sign (Broca aphasia, impaired repetition) + face/arm-predominant deficit → left frontal cortex (superior-division MCA). The cortical sign rules out a pure capsular lacune.

Case 2. Vertigo and vomiting; left facial pinprick loss with right-body pinprick loss, left Horner, left limb ataxia, hoarseness.
Localize it.

Crossed sensory loss = brainstem; the constellation is lateral medulla (Wallenberg) — PICA/vertebral territory. Watch airway/swallow; consider vertebral dissection in a younger patient.

Case 3. Progressive leg weakness, a sensory level at the umbilicus, brisk leg reflexes with bilateral Babinski, and urinary retention.
Localize it — and the urgent step?

Sensory level + bilateral UMN legs + sphincter involvement → spinal cord (~T10). Image the cord urgently to exclude compression.

Case 4. A fluent patient produces jargon, cannot follow commands, and cannot repeat; no weakness.
Which aphasia, and where?

Fluent + impaired comprehension + impaired repetition → Wernicke aphasia (dominant superior temporal, inferior-division MCA). Lack of weakness fits the inferior-division territory.
