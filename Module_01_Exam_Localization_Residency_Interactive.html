From checklist to localization

The general-physician Module 1 teaches a reliable screening exam. This version trains the exam as the experiment that tests a localization hypothesis. The history asks where and what; the examination answers where; tempo answers what.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Run a structured top-down exam and justify what each manoeuvre localizes.
  2. Separate upper- from lower-motor-neuron weakness at the bedside.
  3. Place a lesion at a level of the neuraxis from the constellation of signs.
  4. Use dissociated sensory loss and a sensory level to localize within the cord and brainstem.
  5. Recognize crossed brainstem signs and the classic cord syndromes.
  6. Distinguish organic patterns from non-anatomical (functional) findings.
Localize first, then characterize. A precise location plus an accurate tempo (hyperacute → vascular/seizure; subacute → inflammatory/infectious/neoplastic; chronic → degenerative/hereditary/compressive) collapses a vast differential before any test is ordered.
Educational use only. Examination teaching reference; tailor depth to the clinical problem and follow institutional protocols at the point of care.

The diagnostic method

Neurologic diagnosis is two steps of reasoning: localize (which part of the nervous system would produce exactly these findings?) then characterize (given the site and the tempo, what disease is it?). History and examination are complementary — Merritt's frames the exam's job as to "accurately localize neurologic dysfunction and develop a differential diagnosis."

Reading the history for localization

  • Tempo — interrogate "sudden": maximal-at-onset (vascular) vs marching over minutes (seizure, aura) vs progressive over days (mass, inflammation).
  • Distribution — focal/multifocal/diffuse; proximal vs distal; length-dependent vs patchy; crossed.
  • Positive vs negative symptoms — loss of function (destruction/conduction block) vs positive phenomena like tingling or jerking (abnormal excitation).
  • Collateral history for any cognitive or episodic complaint.
If the exam contradicts the story, re-take the history rather than forcing the findings to fit.

The examination — top-down

Perform and record in a uniform order to avoid omissions; state any portions deliberately skipped. Sequence: mental status → cranial nerves → motor → reflexes → coordination → sensation → gait.

Mental status & higher cortical function

Cognitive screen: orientation, digit span forward/backward, serial 7s, three-item recall at 3 minutes. Characterize spontaneous speech (fluent vs non-fluent), then test comprehension, repetition, naming, reading, writing — the combination localizes aphasia type and hemisphere. Test praxis (commanded/imitated gesture), visuospatial ability (clock, figure copy, line bisection), and gnosis. Re-emergent primitive reflexes (grasp, snout, glabellar) suggest frontal disease.

Cranial nerves — localizing gold
  • Visual fields by confrontation, each eye separately: monocular = pre-chiasmal; bitemporal = chiasm; homonymous = retrochiasmal.
  • Pupils, lids, eye movements; pursue perimetry if a field defect is found.
  • Facial weakness: forehead-sparing = UMN (central VII); whole-hemiface = peripheral VII.
  • Tongue deviates toward the weak (LMN) hypoglossal side; watch for atrophy/fasciculation.
Motor — find subtle weakness

Assess bulk, tone, power (MRC 0–5), and involuntary movements. Tone separates spasticity (velocity-dependent, corticospinal) from rigidity (cogwheel, extrapyramidal). Record the pattern of weakness, not just the grade.

Pronator drift and asymmetric forearm orbiting reveal mild corticospinal weakness MRC testing misses; in the legs, resting hip external rotation and downward drift of the flexed prone leg betray a pyramidal lesion.

Reflexes — the Babinski and its mimics

Sample biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, Achilles; facilitate with Jendrassik. The Babinski sign (great-toe dorsiflexion + fanning) reliably marks corticospinal damage. Beware: brisk voluntary withdrawal is not pathological, but a slow triple-flexion response carries the same meaning as Babinski. Lost superficial (abdominal/cremasteric) reflexes support a corticospinal lesion.

Coordination, sensation & gait

Coordination (finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements) is interpretable only in limbs with adequate power. Sensation is performed last and briefly to limit fatigue and over-reporting; map any deficit to a dermatome, nerve territory, sensory level, or hemibody pattern. Gait integrates everything — hemiparetic circumduction, spastic scissoring, parkinsonian festination, cerebellar wide-based ataxia, sensory ataxia with a positive Romberg, steppage of foot drop, and waddling myopathic gait each have a signature.

The first decision: UMN vs LMN

When weakness is present, this single distinction splits the neuraxis in two and drives everything downstream.

FeatureUpper motor neuronLower motor neuron
ToneIncreased (spastic)Decreased (flaccid)
ReflexesBrisk; Babinski presentReduced/absent; plantar flexor
BulkPreserved (disuse atrophy late)Early, marked atrophy
FasciculationsAbsentPresent
Weakness patternPyramidal (arm extensors, leg flexors weaker)Root / nerve / muscle pattern
Acute caveat. Early in an acute UMN lesion (acute stroke, acute cord compression / "spinal shock") tone and reflexes may be reduced before spasticity evolves. Mixed UMN + LMN signs without sensory loss → suspect motor neuron disease.

Crossed-signs rule. Ipsilateral face + contralateral body (motor or sensory) = brainstem, because the long tracts have decussated (or are about to) while the cranial-nerve nucleus is ipsilateral. Pinpoint the level by the cranial nerve: CN III → midbrain; VI/VII → pons; IX–XII → medulla.

Case-based localization

Case 1. Right hand and arm weakness with a non-fluent, effortful speech but preserved comprehension. Face and arm more affected than leg. Brisk right reflexes, right Babinski.
Localize it.

UMN pattern + a cortical sign (Broca aphasia) + face/arm-predominant deficit → left frontal cortex (superior-division MCA territory). The cortical sign rules out a pure capsular lacune.

Case 2. Vertigo and vomiting; left facial pinprick loss with right-body pinprick loss, left Horner, left limb ataxia, hoarseness.
Localize it.

Crossed sensory loss = brainstem; the constellation (Horner, ataxia, dysphagia, vertigo, ipsilateral facial / contralateral body pain-temp loss) is lateral medulla (Wallenberg).

Case 3. Progressive leg weakness, a sensory level at the umbilicus, brisk leg reflexes with bilateral Babinski, and urinary retention.
Localize it — and what's the urgent step?

Sensory level + bilateral UMN legs + sphincter involvement → spinal cord (~T10). Image the cord urgently to exclude compression — this is a neurosurgical/time-critical pathway.

Case 4. Symmetric proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair and lifting arms overhead), normal sensation, reflexes preserved, no fatigability.
Localize it.

Symmetric proximal, sensory-sparing, non-fatigable → muscle (myopathy). Fatigability would point to NMJ; distal length-dependent loss would point to polyneuropathy.
