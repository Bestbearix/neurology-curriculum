Why this module?

"Weakness" is one of the most common complaints in primary care, and the differential ranges from benign deconditioning to fatal GBS. The five emergencies in this module — Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenic crisis, botulism, rhabdomyolysis with AKI, periodic paralysis — share one thing: they kill within hours to days if missed, and they recover well if recognised early.

After this module you will:

  • Localise weakness in seconds to one of seven anatomical sites: UMN, cord, motor neuron, root, plexus, nerve, NMJ, muscle.
  • Pick GBS, MG crisis, and botulism out of the differential and act before respiratory failure.
  • Run the first-tier polyneuropathy workup (glucose, B12, SPEP) before referring.
  • Prescribe pyridostigmine, steroids, and steroid-sparing agents in MG; know when to escalate to IVIG/PLEX or new biologics.
  • Recognise ALS, refer to multidisciplinary clinic, and counsel about riluzole/edaravone/tofersen.
  • Distinguish statin myalgia, statin myopathy, statin-rhabdomyolysis, and statin-IMNM (the persistent anti-HMGCR one).
The single most useful bedside habit: if a patient has progressive weakness AND any bulbar / respiratory / autonomic / red-flag feature → measure FVC and negative inspiratory force (NIF), send to ED, and start serial monitoring. "20/30/40 rule": consider intubation when FVC < 20 mL/kg, MIP less negative than −30, or MEP < 40.

2. Neuromuscular emergencies

Five to remember:

  1. GBS — admit; serial FVC/NIF; IVIG or PLEX. ICU if 20/30/40 rule breached or autonomic instability.
  2. Myasthenic crisis — IVIG or PLEX; ICU; remove precipitating drug/infection.
  3. Botulism — antitoxin; ICU; public-health reporting.
  4. Rhabdomyolysis — IV fluids, electrolytes, monitor renal function.
  5. Periodic paralysis — correct K+; cardiac monitoring.

Red flags: rapid progression, dyspnoea, can't hold head up, bulbar, autonomic instability, new bowel/bladder.

3. Approach to the polyneuropathy

For distal symmetric polyneuropathy, the AAN first-tier workup identifies a cause in > 80% of treatable cases: fasting glucose / OGTT · vitamin B12 (with MMA if borderline) · SPEP / IFE / serum free light chains. Add TSH, CBC, renal/liver, HbA1c, HIV/HepB/HepC.

EMG/NCS: demyelinating (slow conduction velocity, conduction block, prolonged distal latency) vs axonal (low amplitudes, normal conduction velocity).

Most common causes worldwide: diabetes > idiopathic > alcohol/B12 > chemotherapy > hereditary > autoimmune.

~20% remain idiopathic after a thorough workup.

4. Guillain-Barré syndrome

Ascending symmetric flaccid weakness + areflexia, often after recent infection (Campylobacter, CMV, EBV, Mycoplasma, Zika, COVID).

Diagnosis: CSF albuminocytologic dissociation (high protein, normal cells; sensitivity rises after week 1); EMG/NCS shows demyelinating (AIDP) or axonal (AMAN, AMSAN) pattern; anti-GQ1b in Miller-Fisher.

Treatment: IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days OR PLEX 5 sessions over 1–2 weeks. Steroids alone don't work. ICU monitoring with serial FVC, NIF; ICU admission if 20/30/40 rule breached.

Variants: AMAN (pure motor axonal, post-Campylobacter, East Asia); Miller-Fisher (ophthalmoplegia + ataxia + areflexia, anti-GQ1b); Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis; pharyngeal-cervical-brachial; pure sensory; acute pandysautonomia.
5. CIDP and multifocal motor neuropathy

CIDP: chronic GBS-like illness; progressive or relapsing > 8 weeks; symmetric proximal AND distal weakness + sensory + areflexia + demyelinating NCS + CSF dissociation. Treat with IVIG, PLEX, or steroids (steroids work in CIDP, unlike GBS). Maintenance often needed for years.

MMN: slowly progressive, asymmetric, pure motor weakness; distal arms first; conduction block at non-entrapment sites; anti-GM1 in ~50%. No UMN signs — that's the key differentiator from ALS. Treat with IVIG; steroids and PLEX can worsen.

6. Myasthenia gravis

Fluctuating fatigable weakness, no sensory loss, reflexes preserved. Ocular onset in 50%; half generalise within 2 years.

Antibodies: AChR (80%), MuSK (5–10% — often prominent bulbar/respiratory + atrophy), LRP4 (1–3%), seronegative (5–10%).

Diagnosis: serology + RNS (decrement >10% at 2–3 Hz) + single-fibre EMG (most sensitive). Ice-pack test for ocular ptosis. CT chest for thymoma.

Treatment ladder:

  1. Symptomatic: pyridostigmine.
  2. Chronic: prednisone + steroid-sparing (azathioprine, MMF, methotrexate, ciclosporin).
  3. Thymectomy: always for thymoma; for non-thymomatous AChR+ generalised MG age 18–65.
  4. Acute / crisis: IVIG or PLEX.
  5. Refractory (new biologics 2017–2024): eculizumab, ravulizumab (C5 inhibitors); efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab (FcRn antagonists); zilucoplan (C5 SC daily). Rozanolixizumab first agent approved for both AChR+ AND MuSK+ MG (2023).
Drugs to avoid in MG: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides (telithromycin contraindicated), β-blockers, high-dose CCBs, IV magnesium (eclampsia!), quinolones, quinine/quinidine/procainamide, neuromuscular blocking agents, checkpoint inhibitors (can induce MG).
7. Lambert-Eaton, botulism, and other NMJ disorders

LEMS: anti-VGCC antibodies; 60% paraneoplastic (especially SCLC). Proximal weakness with post-exercise facilitation (opposite of MG fatigue). Autonomic dysfunction (dry mouth, ED). RNS: incrementing response at high frequency. Treat the cancer; amifampridine (3,4-DAP), pyridostigmine, immunotherapy.

Botulism: pre-synaptic; toxin blocks ACh release. Descending paralysis starting with bulbar features; prominent autonomic (mydriasis, dry mouth, ileus). Sources: home-canned food, infant honey, wound (IV drug use), iatrogenic. Treat with equine antitoxin (BabyBIG for infants); supportive ICU; report to public health.

8. ALS and motor neuron diseases

Combined UMN + LMN signs in the same region, asymmetric onset, sparing of sensory and oculomotor function. Median survival 3–5 years from onset; 10% > 10 years.

Variants: PLS (pure UMN), PMA (pure LMN), progressive bulbar palsy, Kennedy (X-linked), SMA (childhood, now treatable: nusinersen, risdiplam, onasemnogene abeparvovec).

Don't miss the treatable mimics: MMN (IVIG-responsive), cervical spondylotic myelopathy with radiculopathy, IBM, lead toxicity, HIV-MND.

Treatment: riluzole (3-month survival benefit); edaravone (modest functional benefit); tofersen for SOD1-ALS (accelerated FDA approval 2023); multidisciplinary clinic care extends survival ~7 months; NIV when FVC < 50% or symptomatic; PEG before FVC < 50% (procedure-risk threshold).

AMX0035 (sodium phenylbutyrate / taurursodiol) was approved 2022 but withdrawn from market in 2024 after failed phase 3 confirmatory trial.
9. Myopathies

Proximal symmetric weakness, normal reflexes/sensation, often elevated CK. Gowers sign for proximal weakness.

Inflammatory: dermatomyositis (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, paraneoplastic; MDA5 with ILD), polymyositis (over-diagnosed historically), IMNM (anti-HMGCR — often statin-associated and persistent; anti-SRP), IBM (older adults, finger flexors + quadriceps, no immunotherapy works), antisynthetase syndrome (anti-Jo-1 + myositis + ILD + mechanic's hands).

Dystrophies: Duchenne (now treatable: exon-skipping ASOs, gene therapy delandistrogene moxeparvovec 2023), myotonic, FSHD, LGMD, oculopharyngeal.

Treatable metabolic myopathies: Pompe (enzyme replacement), McArdle (exercise + diet).

Channelopathies: hyper/hypokalaemic periodic paralysis; paramyotonia congenita.

10. Statin myopathy and drug-induced myopathies

Statin-associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) range from mild myalgia (5–10%) through myopathy (CK 10× ULN, resolves on stopping) to rhabdomyolysis to the rarer statin-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) — anti-HMGCR antibody + persistent weakness despite stopping the statin; requires immunotherapy (steroids + IVIG ± rituximab).

Steroid myopathy: chronic high-dose steroids; normal CK; proximal atrophy.

Other drug myopathies: colchicine (especially renal impairment), amiodarone (also neuropathy), hydroxychloroquine (vacuolar), older NRTIs (mitochondrial), checkpoint inhibitors ("3M syndrome" of myositis + myocarditis + MG).

Critical illness myopathy/neuropathy: develops in ICU especially with sepsis; failure to wean. Recovery slow but largely complete.

11. When to refer
  • Emergency: acute progressive weakness with dyspnoea, bulbar, or autonomic instability.
  • Myasthenic crisis or impending crisis.
  • Rhabdomyolysis with AKI risk.
  • Acute foot drop in young patient (peroneal, L5, demyelinating).
Routine: suspected ALS, suspected MG, suspected inflammatory myopathy, CIDP suspected, distal symmetric polyneuropathy with no cause after first-tier workup, suspected hereditary disease, refractory carpal tunnel, plexopathy.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 35-year-old with 5 days of ascending leg weakness after diarrhoea

Two weeks ago he had Campylobacter gastroenteritis. Now: bilateral leg weakness progressing to hip flexors; weak hand grip; areflexia. No sensory level. FVC 45% predicted (declining). HR variable; orthostatic. CSF: protein 95 mg/dL, 3 WBC/µL (albuminocytologic dissociation).

Diagnosis: Guillain-Barré syndrome, likely AMAN (post-Campylobacter).

Plan: Admit ICU (declining FVC, autonomic instability). Start IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days (or PLEX × 5). Serial FVC/NIF/MIP/MEP. Intubate semi-electively if FVC < 20 mL/kg or MIP less negative than −30. Cardiac telemetry for autonomic arrhythmias. DVT prophylaxis. Early PT/OT. Do not use steroids alone.

Case 2 — A 28-year-old woman with double vision and droopy lids worse by evening

For 3 months. Initially intermittent ptosis; now constant by mid-afternoon. Diplopia variable. Last week began choking on water and her voice has become nasal. Exam: bilateral ptosis worse on sustained upgaze; ice-pack test: ptosis improves > 2 mm in 2 min. Limb power normal. Reflexes normal. No sensory loss.

Diagnosis: Myasthenia gravis, generalised, evolving from ocular onset.

Plan: AChR antibody, MuSK if AChR negative. RNS / single-fibre EMG. CT chest (thymoma). Start pyridostigmine 30–60 mg q4–6h. Add prednisone 1 mg/kg/day with slow titration (start low and titrate, or admit during initial rise to watch for transient worsening). PCP prophylaxis, bone protection. Steroid-sparing agent (azathioprine or MMF) for chronic management. Avoid aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, IV magnesium, β-blockers. Discuss thymectomy if no thymoma but age 18–65 with AChR+ generalised disease. If refractory: efgartigimod / rozanolixizumab / eculizumab / ravulizumab / zilucoplan are now options.

Case 3 — A 65-year-old man with 18 months of progressive weakness and twitching

Initially weakness in his right hand (couldn't button shirts), then atrophy of the first dorsal interosseous. Now weakness of both hands, mild dysarthria. Exam: tongue fasciculations and atrophy; brisk jaw jerk; bilateral hand wasting with fasciculations; biceps and triceps reflexes hyperactive; Babinski equivocal. Normal sensation. Normal cognition.

Diagnosis: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (combined UMN + LMN, asymmetric, sensory spared, no oculomotor or sphincter involvement).

Plan: EMG showing widespread denervation in multiple regions, fasciculations. MRI brain & cervical spine (rule out compressive cervical myelopathy with radiculopathy). Send C9orf72 testing if FTD features or family history. Start riluzole 50 mg BID; consider edaravone. Refer to multidisciplinary ALS clinic. Discuss SOD1 testing — if positive, tofersen available. Counsel on advance care planning, PEG and NIV timing. Test FVC for baseline; start NIV when FVC < 50% or symptomatic. Do not delay PEG until FVC < 50% (placement is safer above that threshold).

Case 4 — A 60-year-old diabetic woman with 4 months of tingling and burning in both feet

Type 2 diabetes for 12 years, HbA1c 8.7%. Burning soles, numbness extending up to mid-shin. Loss of ankle jerks; reduced vibration and pinprick at toes; touch preserved in trunk. No weakness. EMG/NCS: distal axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy.

Diagnosis: Distal symmetric diabetic polyneuropathy (most common cause of polyneuropathy worldwide).

Plan: Optimise glycaemic control (target HbA1c ~7%). First-tier polyneuropathy workup: B12 + MMA, SPEP/IFE, TSH. Symptomatic: duloxetine 30–60 mg/day or pregabalin 75–300 mg BID or gabapentin 300–1200 mg TID. Foot care education. Address vascular risk. Consider tapentadol or topical capsaicin/lidocaine if first-line fails. Acupuncture or PT may help.

Case 5 — A 70-year-old man with bilateral foot drop and weak hand grip after weeks of weight loss

Lost 8 kg unintentionally. Now bilateral foot drop, weak hand grip, severe shin and forearm pain. Asymmetric. EMG: multifocal axonal neuropathy involving sural, peroneal, ulnar, median nerves. ANCA negative; cryoglobulins negative; hepatitis serologies negative; ANA modestly positive; rheumatoid factor positive. ESR 80.

Diagnosis: Mononeuritis multiplex, likely vasculitic neuropathy. Need to identify systemic vs nonsystemic; classic causes include polyarteritis nodosa, ANCA-associated vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemia, RA-associated.

Plan: Nerve biopsy (sural) to confirm vasculitis. Pulse IV methylprednisolone + cyclophosphamide or rituximab. Search for systemic involvement (renal, ENT, skin, lung). Pain management. Long-term follow-up.

Case 6 — A 55-year-old man on rosuvastatin 40 mg presents with proximal weakness, CK 8,000

Difficulty arising from chair and climbing stairs for 6 weeks. No exercise, no fever. Statin started 4 months ago for ASCVD. Weakness persists 8 weeks after stopping statin. CK still 6,200. EMG: myopathic; MRI muscle: oedema and atrophy. Anti-HMGCR antibody positive.

Diagnosis: Statin-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

Plan: This is not typical statin myopathy — it does not resolve with statin withdrawal. Treat with high-dose steroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/day) plus IVIG 2 g/kg in divided doses; add rituximab or methotrexate if not responding. May need years of immunotherapy. Counsel: cannot rechallenge any statin. Alternative lipid-lowering: ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitor.

Anti-HMGCR myopathy is a key clinical pearl: persistent weakness with elevated CK after stopping statin is not just bad luck — it's an autoimmune myopathy that needs immunotherapy.
