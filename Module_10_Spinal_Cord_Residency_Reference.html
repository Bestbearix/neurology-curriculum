1 Cord anatomy & localization

▎Key points
  • Localize in two dimensions: the vertical level (a sensory level = the cord's fingerprint) and the cross-sectional tract pattern.
  • Three tracts do the work: corticospinal (ipsilateral weakness below), dorsal columns (ipsilateral vibration/proprioception), spinothalamic (contralateral pain/temp, crosses within 1–2 segments).
  • The cord ends at the conus (~L1–L2) — below that is cauda equina (roots). Landmarks: nipple T4, umbilicus T10, inguinal L1.
  • Acute cord lesions cause spinal shock — initial flaccidity/areflexia, not spasticity.

Cord disease is localized by asking where along the length and which part of the cross-section. The vertical level is given by the highest dermatome of sensory loss and the myotomes of segmental (lower-motor-neuron) weakness. The cross-sectional pattern is read off the long tracts and grey matter.

Vertebral–cord mismatch. Because the cord ends around L1–L2, a low-thoracic vertebral lesion can strike the conus or cauda equina. Always translate the bony level to the neural level.
Spinal shock trap. In the first hours–days of an acute cord lesion the exam below the level is flaccid and areflexic, not spastic. Absent tone does not exclude a severe myelopathy — it evolves to hyperreflexia over days to weeks.

2 The cord emergency: recognize & image

▎Key points
  • Acute/subacute myelopathy = time-critical emergency. The reflex: pattern → emergent whole-spine MRI with contrast → treat the cause simultaneously.
  • Image the entire spine — lesions are often multiple or at a different level than the clinical guess.
  • If MRI is unavailable, use CT myelography.

Non-traumatic cord compression is frequently missed at first presentation; the provided emergency-radiology primer stresses a low threshold to image. The residency instinct is to treat any myelopathic pattern as a compressive emergency until imaging says otherwise.

3 Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC)

▎Key points
  • Usual primaries: lung, breast, prostate, plus myeloma/lymphoma. Back pain (worse recumbent/at night) precedes neurologic decline.
  • Ambulatory status at treatment is the strongest predictor of walking afterward — treat fast.
  • Patchell: decompressive surgery + radiotherapy > radiotherapy alone for preserving/regaining ambulation.
  • Give dexamethasone at diagnosis; consider separation surgery + SABR for radioresistant tumours.
Patchell (Lancet 2005). In cord compression from metastatic (non-radiosensitive) cancer, direct decompressive surgery followed by radiotherapy was superior to radiotherapy alone — more patients retained or regained the ability to walk, for longer. This established a role for upfront surgery in selected patients.

Give corticosteroids (dexamethasone) at diagnosis to reduce vasogenic edema while arranging definitive therapy. Modern practice increasingly uses separation surgery followed by stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) for radioresistant tumours (per the provided meta-analysis) — the surgery decompresses the cord and creates a margin, and focused radiation controls the tumour. Decisions are multidisciplinary (spine surgery, radiation and medical oncology), weighing tumour radiosensitivity, spinal stability (SINS score), and overall prognosis.

4 Epidural abscess & epidural hematoma

▎Key points
  • Spinal epidural abscess — classic triad fever + back pain + deficit (complete in a minority, so it's often missed). S. aureus predominates.
  • Risk: diabetes, IV drug use, indwelling lines/recent spinal procedures, immunosuppression, bacteraemia.
  • Low threshold for MRI + ESR/CRP; treat with surgical drainage + targeted antibiotics (medical management only in selected deficit-free patients).
  • Epidural hematoma (often on anticoagulation) — emergent decompression + reverse coagulopathy.

The provided review advocates a risk-factor-driven pathway with a low threshold for MRI plus inflammatory markers to shorten the diagnostic delay that drives poor outcomes in abscess. For hematoma, outcome depends on the speed of evacuation and preoperative deficit (the provided octogenarian and postoperative-hematoma series reinforce urgency).

5 Cauda equina & conus medullaris syndromes

▎Key points
  • Cauda equina = lumbosacral roots below the conus (usually a large central disc): bilateral radicular pain/weakness, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention with overflow — LMN, areflexic, often asymmetric.
  • Conus medullaris = more symmetric, early prominent sphincter loss, mixed UMN/LMN.
  • Surgical emergency: suspected CES → emergent MRI + urgent decompression. Don't wait for complete retention.

Cauda equina is a surgical emergency. Recovery is time-dependent and can be incomplete even with expeditious surgery — early/incomplete CES (retention not yet established) has the best prognosis, so image and decompress early.

6 Traumatic spinal cord injury (tSCI)

▎Key points
  • Classify with ISNCSCI + the ASIA Impairment Scale (A–E) (more reproducible than Frankel); determine level, motor/sensory scores, completeness (sacral sparing).
  • Early decompression (<24 h) → better neurological recovery (pooled IPD).
  • Hemodynamics: MAP ~85–90 mm Hg for ~5–7 days; avoid hypotension/hypoxia; ICU care.
  • Riluzole (RISCIS): negative primary endpoint. High-dose methylprednisolone: controversial, not universally recommended.

Timing of decompression (pooled IPD analysis). Early surgical decompression (<24 h) after acute SCI is associated with greater AIS-grade improvement than late (≥24 h) — "time is spine." The central cord syndrome analysis similarly compared early vs late decompression.
Hemodynamic management (AO Spine / acute-SCI CPG). Augment mean arterial pressure (commonly ~85–90 mm Hg for the first ~5–7 days) and avoid hypotension/hypoxia to protect the penumbral cord. Manage in critical care.
Neurogenic shock (hypotension + bradycardia, from sympathetic disruption in injuries above ~T6) is distinct from hypovolemic and spinal shock — treat with fluids, vasopressors, and atropine for bradycardia. Cervical injuries also threaten the airway/diaphragm (C3–C5).
Riluzole — RISCIS. In acute cervical tSCI, RISCIS did not meet its primary endpoint for upper-extremity motor recovery overall; riluzole is not established as standard acute therapy.
The steroid controversy. High-dose methylprednisolone (NASCIS protocols) remains controversial and is not universally recommended — any modest benefit is offset by respiratory and infectious complications. If used at all it is an option within a narrow early window, per local protocol; many centres have abandoned it.

8 Vascular myelopathy

▎Key points
  • Cord infarction — hyperacute painful anterior-cord syndrome; aortic disease, atherosclerosis, fibrocartilaginous embolism; poor prognosis; treatment mostly supportive.
  • Spinal dural AV fistula — the "great mimic": older men, progressive congestive myelopathy, LETM + perimedullary flow voids, ~⅓ misdiagnosed as transverse myelitis — but treatable.
  • Cavernous malformation — stepwise deficits from recurrent microhemorrhage.

Spinal cord infarction

Presents as a hyperacute painful myelopathy, typically in the anterior spinal artery territory (para/tetraparesis + dissociated sensory loss, dorsal columns spared). In the provided 41-patient series the leading aetiologies were aortic dissection/surgery, atherosclerosis, and fibrocartilaginous embolism; hypotension and pre-existing vascular disease worsen an already poor prognosis. MRI shows T2 cord signal (owl's-eye) sometimes with vertebral-body infarction; DWI can be positive early. Treatment is largely supportive — treat the cause, optimise perfusion/BP; thrombolysis has only anecdotal support. Note the described association of spinal cord ischaemia with SARS-CoV-2, and the PAPAartis trial's focus on preventing paraplegia during aortic repair.

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (SDAVF)

The great mimic. SDAVF — most common in middle-aged/older men, thoracolumbar — causes a progressive congestive (venous hypertensive) myelopathy frequently misdiagnosed (~35% in one provided series, with years of delay) as transverse myelitis or degenerative disease. Clue: progressive stepwise myelopathy with longitudinally extensive T2 cord signal and dilated perimedullary flow voids; confirm with spinal angiography. It is treatable (endovascular embolization or surgical ligation) — earlier treatment means better recovery.
Spinal cavernous malformations cause stepwise deficits from recurrent microhemorrhage; the provided natural-history cohort guides the surgery-vs-observation decision by hemorrhage risk and surgical accessibility.

9 Inflammatory & infectious myelopathy

▎Key points
  • Transverse myelitis is a syndrome, not a diagnosis — always ask what is causing it and exclude compression first.
  • Longitudinally extensive TM (≥3 segments) → think NMOSD (AQP4-IgG) and MOGAD (send antibodies); short-segment, peripheral → more MS-like.
  • Infectious causes: viral (HSV/VZV, enterovirus, HIV), tuberculous, and, in endemic areas, schistosomal — plus HTLV-1 tropical spastic paraparesis.

When the cord shows T2 signal without compression, the differential broadens to inflammatory, infectious, vascular (SDAVF above), and neoplastic causes. Autoimmune myelitis spans MS (typically short-segment, eccentric), NMOSD and MOGAD (typically longitudinally extensive — cross-links to Module 8), and systemic disease (sarcoid, lupus, Sjögren). Work-up: contrast MRI brain + whole spine, CSF (cells, protein, oligoclonal bands), serum AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG, and infectious/serologic screens guided by exposures. Treat the cause; acute inflammatory myelitis usually receives high-dose corticosteroids ± plasma exchange for severe or steroid-refractory attacks. Subacute progressive infective myelopathy (e.g., TB, HTLV-1) needs directed therapy.

Exclude compression before labelling "transverse myelitis." The most dangerous error is treating a compressive lesion (or a treatable SDAVF) as idiopathic myelitis with steroids alone.

10 Degenerative & structural myelopathy

▎Key points
  • Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the commonest cause of non-traumatic cord dysfunction in adults — insidious gait/hand clumsiness + myelopathic signs.
  • Surgical decompression halts progression and often improves function; riluzole did not help (CSM-PROTECT) — surgery is the mainstay.
  • Syringomyelia → suspended "cape" dissociated loss; often with Chiari I (posterior-fossa decompression when symptomatic).

DCM: diagnosis integrates history/exam and MRI cord compression/signal change; the provided update and history/exam papers stress earlier recognition to intervene before irreversible injury.

Riluzole in DCM — CSM-PROTECT (secondary analysis). Riluzole did not improve the primary mJOA outcome, though secondary analyses explored neck-pain and multidimensional recovery. Surgery, not neuroprotective drugs, remains the mainstay.

Syringomyelia/hydromyelia: a fluid cavity producing a "cape" suspended dissociated sensory loss and lower-motor-neuron signs at the level, often associated with Chiari malformation; the provided reviews cover current neurosurgical management (decompression, and treating the underlying cause of the syrinx).

11 Inherited & toxic/nutritional myelopathy

▎Key points
  • Hereditary spastic paraplegia — progressive spastic paraparesis, length-dependent corticospinal degeneration, many genes.
  • Friedreich ataxia — FXN GAA repeat; sensory ataxia + cardiomyopathy + diabetes; omaveloxolone is the first disease-directed therapy.
  • Subacute combined degeneration — B12 deficiency or nitrous-oxide misuse (B12 may be normal → check MMA/homocysteine).

Nitrous-oxide myelopathy is rising. Recreational N₂O inactivates B12, so the serum B12 can be normal — the diagnosis rests on methylmalonic acid/homocysteine and the exposure history. Treat with B12 and stop the exposure.

12 Chronic SCI complications

▎Key points
  • Autonomic dysreflexia (injury ≥T6): noxious stimulus below the level → reflex hypertension + bradycardia + pounding headache. Sit up, remove the trigger, then antihypertensive.
  • Commonest triggers: bladder distension and bowel impaction.
  • Also manage: neurogenic bladder (protect kidneys), neurogenic bowel, pressure injury, spasticity, VTE, respiratory care.

Autonomic dysreflexia is a life-threatening complication of injuries at or above ~T6. Management, in order: sit the patient up (orthostatic drop helps), loosen constrictions, find and remove the trigger (catheterize a blocked/distended bladder, disimpact the bowel), and give a rapid-onset short-acting antihypertensive if the BP stays high. Untreated, it can cause intracranial haemorrhage, seizures, or death. Beyond dysreflexia, chronic SCI care means kidney-protective neurogenic bladder management, a structured neurogenic bowel programme (diet as an adjunct), pressure-injury prevention, spasticity treatment, VTE prophylaxis, and respiratory care — all covered in the provided SCI-management papers.
