Why this module?

Brain tumors and their consequences arrive at the general physician's door first: new headache with red flags, first seizure in an adult, an unexpected mass on a scan, a cancer patient with back pain, or a confused patient who turns out to have a paraneoplastic syndrome. The job is to recognise, image promptly, manage symptoms safely (especially raised ICP and MESCC), and route the patient through a multidisciplinary team without delay.

After this module you will:

  • Place a new brain lesion into a working differential using age, location, imaging, and tempo.
  • Read a pathology report with WHO CNS5 (2021) vocabulary — IDH, 1p/19q, MGMT, BRAF, H3 K27M.
  • Manage the Stupp protocol patient at follow-up and recognise pseudoprogression.
  • Recognise and triage MESCC, pituitary apoplexy, and leptomeningeal disease.
  • Identify classic paraneoplastic syndromes and the right antibody/tumor pairings.
  • Recognise NF1, NF2, TSC, vHL, and Sturge-Weber at the bedside.
  • Know when not to give steroids before biopsy (suspected lymphoma).
The single principle that prevents disasters: a cancer patient with new back pain has MESCC until proven otherwise — give dexamethasone and image the whole spine within hours, not the next morning. A young woman with subacute psychiatric symptoms, seizures, and orofacial dyskinesias has anti-NMDA-R encephalitis — image the ovaries, send antibodies, and start immunotherapy empirically.

2. WHO CNS5 (2021) classification — what changed

WHO CNS5 (2021) integrates histology with molecular markers. Three adult diffuse-glioma categories now drive prognosis and treatment:

  1. Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (grades 2–4).
  2. Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted (grades 2–3).
  3. Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (grade 4).

3. Glioblastoma — Stupp protocol and TTF
  1. Maximal safe resection.
  2. Concurrent chemoradiation: 60 Gy / 30 fractions + daily temozolomide 75 mg/m²/day.
  3. Adjuvant temozolomide: 6 cycles, 150–200 mg/m²/day × 5 days each 28-day cycle.
  4. Tumor-treating fields (TTF) — scalp electric fields; EF-14 trial: median OS 20.9 vs 16.0 months with maintenance temozolomide.
  5. Elderly / frail: hypofractionated RT 40 Gy / 15 fx ± temozolomide; MGMT-methylated patients may have temozolomide monotherapy.
  6. Recurrence: bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) reduces oedema and steroid need; re-resection in select cases; clinical trials.
Pseudoprogression: after chemoradiation, MRI may transiently worsen in the first 3 months. Don't switch therapy on that scan alone — re-image at 1–3 months; advanced perfusion / spectroscopy can help.
4. Meningioma

Most common primary brain tumor (~36%). Female predominance 2:1. Sex-hormone-receptor positive (may enlarge in pregnancy or with anti-androgen/progestin exposure). Imaging: extra-axial, dural-based, homogeneously enhancing, dural tail, often calcified, may hyperostose adjacent bone.

  • Asymptomatic, incidental: observe with MRI at 6–12 months, then yearly.
  • Symptomatic or growing: surgical resection (Simpson grade).
  • Unresectable / residual / atypical (grade 2) / anaplastic (grade 3): SRS or fractionated RT.
  • NF2 → multiple meningiomas.
  • Prior cranial RT increases meningioma risk for decades.

Macroadenoma = ≥ 10 mm. Workup: pituitary MRI + full hormone panel + visual fields. Prolactinoma — cabergoline (medical first). Others — transsphenoidal surgery first-line, RT for residual.

Pituitary apoplexy: thunderclap headache + ophthalmoplegia + bitemporal hemianopia + panhypopituitarism. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat, MRI, urgent neurosurgery for visual/oculomotor compromise.
6. Vestibular schwannoma and CPA masses
  • Unilateral sensorineural hearing loss + tinnitus → image internal auditory meatus.
  • Larger tumors: trigeminal then facial nerve involvement, hydrocephalus.
  • Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = NF2 until proven otherwise.
  • Options: observation (small stable); Gamma Knife radiosurgery; microsurgery. Hearing preservation ~50%.
  • CPA differential: meningioma (broad dural base, no IAM extension), epidermoid (restricted diffusion), facial nerve schwannoma.
7. Primary CNS lymphoma
  • Aggressive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma confined to CNS. Median age 60s.
  • HIV/transplant → EBV-driven; immunocompetent → usually not.
  • MRI: periventricular, homogeneously enhancing (immunocompetent), restricted diffusion; may cross corpus callosum ("butterfly").
  • HIV may be multifocal ring-enhancing — mimics toxoplasmosis (cross-ref Module 11).
  • CSF cytology + flow cytometry; vitreous biopsy if ocular; EBV PCR in HIV.
  • Do not give steroids before biopsy — they shrink lymphoma and abolish histology.
  • Induction: high-dose methotrexate–based (e.g., MATRix). Consolidation: autologous stem-cell transplant or whole-brain RT.
8. Brain metastases & leptomeningeal disease

Most common intracranial tumor overall. Primaries: lung (~50%), breast, melanoma, renal, colorectal. Haemorrhagic mets: melanoma, renal, choriocarcinoma, thyroid, lung.

Pattern: well-circumscribed, multiple, grey-white junction, disproportionate vasogenic oedema.

Treatment

  • Symptom control: dexamethasone 4–8 mg po BID (titrate up to 16 mg/day); antiseizure medication only if seizures.
  • Local: surgery or SRS for solitary/oligometastatic; SRS for up to ~10 lesions in modern programmes; WBRT reserved (cognitive cost).
  • Hippocampal-sparing WBRT + memantine reduces cognitive decline.
  • CNS-penetrating systemic therapy: tucatinib (HER2+ breast), osimertinib (EGFR lung), alectinib/lorlatinib (ALK lung), dabrafenib+trametinib (BRAF melanoma), ipi+nivo (melanoma).

Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis

Multifocal: CN palsies + polyradiculopathy + communicating hydrocephalus + encephalopathy. MRI pial / sulcal / CN enhancement. CSF cytology often needs ≥ 2 LPs. Intrathecal chemotherapy + CNS-penetrating systemic Rx + focal RT to symptomatic sites. Median survival weeks to months.

12. Oncologic neurologic emergencies
  • MESCC: dexamethasone + urgent MRI + neurosurgery/RO consult within 6 hours.
  • Symptomatic raised ICP: dexamethasone, head of bed up, mannitol/hypertonic saline if herniation, urgent neurosurgical referral.
  • Pituitary apoplexy: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat; urgent MRI & neurosurgery.
  • Status epilepticus in a tumor patient: standard SE pathway (Module 3); careful with phenytoin–dexamethasone interaction.
  • Symptomatic hydrocephalus (especially posterior-fossa mass): EVD ± shunt.
  • Stroke in cancer patient: consider Trousseau syndrome / NBTE; therapeutic LMWH if appropriate.
  • Neutropenic fever + neuro deficit: assume CNS infection (Module 11) — broad antibiotics + aciclovir empirically.
  • Tumor lysis syndrome with seizures: correct K, Ca, PO4, uric acid first.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 65-year-old with 3 weeks of progressive aphasia and a new generalised seizure

Subacute expressive aphasia, mild right facial droop, evolving over 3 weeks. MRI: 4 cm ring-enhancing mass in left temporal-parietal region with central necrosis, surrounding T2/FLAIR hyperintensity extending across the corpus callosum. No prior cancer history.

Diagnosis: Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype until proven otherwise.

Plan:

  1. Dexamethasone 8 mg po (or IV) BID for oedema; load with antiseizure medication (levetiracetam) since seizure occurred.
  2. Urgent neurosurgical referral for maximal safe resection (or stereotactic biopsy if eloquent area).
  3. Histology + molecular: IDH1/2, MGMT methylation, EGFR, TERT, +7/−10, ATRX, TP53.
  4. If GBM IDH-wildtype: Stupp protocol — 60 Gy / 30 fx + concurrent temozolomide 75 mg/m²/day → 6 cycles adjuvant temozolomide 150–200 mg/m²/day × 5 days each 28-day cycle.
  5. Consider TTF (Optune) added to maintenance temozolomide.
  6. MRI surveillance every 2–3 months; remember pseudoprogression in the first 3 months after chemoradiation.
  7. Discuss prognosis and palliative-care input early.
Case 2 — A 58-year-old woman with breast cancer (treated 4 years ago) presents with 1 week of progressive thoracic back pain and 2 days of leg weakness

On exam: hyperreflexia in lower limbs, sensory level at T6, urinary retention. Now ambulating only with assistance.

Diagnosis: Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC) until proven otherwise.

Plan within 6 hours:

  1. Dexamethasone 10 mg IV bolus, then 4 mg IV q6h.
  2. Urgent whole-spine MRI (not just the symptomatic level — multilevel disease common).
  3. Combined neurosurgery + radiation oncology consultation.
  4. If ambulatory at presentation, single level, life expectancy > 3 months: surgical decompression + post-op RT (Patchell trial: better than RT alone).
  5. If not surgical candidate: palliative external beam RT.
  6. Bladder catheter; thromboprophylaxis (high VTE risk in cancer + immobility); analgesia; bowel regimen.
  7. Restage systemic disease (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis ± PET); discuss systemic therapy change in tumor board.
Do not wait for "morning rounds" — the best ambulatory outcome correlates with treatment within ~6 hours of new weakness.
Case 3 — A 28-year-old woman with 3 weeks of bizarre behaviour, seizures, and now orofacial dyskinesias and autonomic instability

Initially anxiety and insomnia; then psychotic features; admitted to psychiatry; had a seizure; now has prominent orofacial dyskinesias and autonomic instability. CSF: 60 lymphocytes/µL, normal glucose, mildly elevated protein. MRI mostly normal. EEG: extreme delta brush. CSF anti-NMDA-R antibody positive. Pelvic US: 4 cm ovarian mass.

Diagnosis: Anti-NMDA-R encephalitis (paraneoplastic from ovarian teratoma).

Plan:

  1. First-line: high-dose IV methylprednisolone + IVIG (or plasma exchange).
  2. Surgical removal of the ovarian teratoma — early removal dramatically improves outcome.
  3. ICU monitoring for autonomic instability (BP swings, arrhythmias, hypoventilation).
  4. Antiseizure medication for seizures.
  5. If inadequate response by 2 weeks: second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide.
  6. Slow recovery (months); full or near-full in ~80% with adequate treatment.
Case 4 — A 50-year-old man with sudden thunderclap headache, double vision, and tunnel vision

Sudden retro-orbital headache; CN III palsy + partial IV/VI involvement; bitemporal hemianopia on confrontation; BP 90/60; lethargic. No prior pituitary diagnosis. Cortisol low, ACTH inappropriately normal.

Diagnosis: Pituitary apoplexy in a previously unrecognised macroadenoma.

Plan:

  1. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat for adrenal crisis, then 50 mg IV q6h.
  2. Urgent dedicated pituitary MRI.
  3. Full pituitary hormone panel (prolactin, free T4, TSH, ACTH, cortisol, LH/FSH, testosterone, GH/IGF-1).
  4. Visual fields formal testing.
  5. Urgent neurosurgery for transsphenoidal decompression — indicated if visual or oculomotor compromise; conservative management possible only if mild presentation, stable vision.
  6. Long-term hormone replacement counselling.
Case 5 — A 35-year-old man with chronic progressive unilateral hearing loss and now imbalance

2 years of progressive left-sided sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus; recent unsteady gait. Exam: reduced left corneal reflex, mild left facial weakness, ipsilateral cerebellar signs. Audiometry: asymmetric SNHL left. MRI IAM: 2.8 cm enhancing mass extending into the left IAM ("ice cream cone" sign).

Diagnosis: Left vestibular schwannoma.

Plan:

  1. Check the contralateral side — bilateral vestibular schwannomas = NF2. Also examine for cutaneous schwannomas and check for ophthalmologic features (cataracts).
  2. Audiometry baseline + speech discrimination.
  3. Options discussed at skull-base tumor board: observation if small/stable; stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife) for tumors typically < 3 cm; microsurgical resection (retrosigmoid, translabyrinthine, or middle fossa) — hearing preservation ~50% in selected cases.
  4. If NF2 confirmed: bevacizumab may provide hearing benefit for some patients.
Case 6 — A 6-year-old boy with multiple café-au-lait macules, axillary freckling, and now progressive vision loss in the left eye

9 café-au-lait macules > 5 mm, bilateral axillary freckling, Lisch nodules on slit lamp, several small cutaneous neurofibromas. MRI: enhancing fusiform mass involving the left optic nerve with proptosis.

Diagnosis: NF1 with optic pathway glioma (typically pilocytic).

Plan:

  1. Formal NF1 diagnosis (≥ 2 NIH criteria met: CALs + freckling + Lisch + first-degree relative typically).
  2. Neuro-ophthalmology evaluation: visual acuity, fields, OCT for surveillance.
  3. MRI brain/orbit with contrast — full extent of glioma and any other lesions (a plexiform neurofibroma, optic chiasm involvement, MOYAMOYA changes).
  4. Multidisciplinary NF clinic + pediatric oncology. Most optic pathway gliomas in NF1 are indolent — observe unless vision is threatened.
  5. If progression: carboplatin + vincristine chemotherapy first-line; selumetinib (MEK inhibitor) is FDA-approved (2020) for inoperable symptomatic plexiform neurofibromas and is being investigated in NF1 low-grade gliomas.
  6. Counsel family on genetics; surveillance for new tumors (plexiform NF with MPNST risk, hypertension from phaeo or renal artery stenosis, learning difficulties, scoliosis).
