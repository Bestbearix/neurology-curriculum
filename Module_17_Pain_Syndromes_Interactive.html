Why this module?

Pain is the commonest reason patients consult their doctor. It is also one of the most over- and under-treated symptoms in medicine. The general physician who can decide whether the pain is nociceptive, neuropathic, nociplastic, or mixed — and who can build a layered opioid-sparing treatment plan — has a vastly more powerful tool kit than one who simply reaches for an analgesic.

After this module you will:

  • Place every pain presentation into one of four mechanistic categories.
  • Use DN4, painDETECT, BPI, the Budapest criteria, and the 2016 ACR fibromyalgia framework fluently.
  • Apply NeuPSIG-aligned first-, second-, and third-line treatment for neuropathic pain.
  • Diagnose and treat trigeminal neuralgia and discuss MVD vs SRS rationally.
  • Recognise small-fibre neuropathy, send the right tests, and treat the right cause.
  • Use the WHO ladder for nociceptive and cancer pain; recognise when adjuvants matter more than opioids.
  • Identify CRPS by the Budapest criteria and lead with rehabilitation.
  • Steward opioids — initiate cautiously, taper safely, co-prescribe naloxone, identify opioid use disorder.
The single principle that prevents disasters: the mechanism dictates the drug. Opioids treat acute severe nociceptive pain reasonably well; they are poorly effective and harmful in chronic neuropathic and nociplastic pain. Reaching for opioids first in chronic pain is the most common and damaging error in pain medicine.

2. Pain pathways & modulating systems

IASP 2020 definition: "An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." Pain is always a personal experience; pain and nociception are different phenomena; verbal report is one of several behaviours; a person's report of pain should be respected.

Pathway: nociceptor (Aδ first/sharp, C second/dull/burning) → DRG → spinal cord lamina I, II, V → crosses within 1–2 segments → spinothalamic tract → VPL of thalamus → S1, insula, ACC.

Descending inhibition: PAG → RVM → spinal dorsal horn via NE (locus coeruleus → α2) and 5-HT (raphe); endogenous opioids. SNRIs and TCAs work largely by enhancing this pathway.

Spinal gate control (Melzack-Wall): Aβ touch input inhibits C/Aδ nociceptive transmission at lamina II — basis for TENS and "rubbing it better".

Central sensitisation: NMDA-mediated wind-up + reduced descending inhibition → amplification. Gabapentinoids reduce presynaptic Ca²⁺ via α2δ; ketamine blocks NMDA; clonidine enhances α2 inhibition.

Peripheral sensitisation: inflammatory soup (PGs, bradykinin, histamine, substance P, CGRP) lowers thresholds. NSAIDs work here.

3. Assessment — history, exam, tools

OPQRST + impact: Onset; Provoking/palliating; Quality (burning, electric → neuropathic; aching → nociceptive; widespread → nociplastic); Region/radiation; Severity (NRS 0–10); Timing. Impact on sleep, mood, function, work, relationships. Red flags + yellow flags. Prior treatments + substance use.

Exam: inspection (CRPS skin/temperature/colour), ROM, tone, reflexes, strength; sensory mapping (touch, pinprick, cold, warmth, vibration); allodynia, hyperalgesia; provocative tests; fibromyalgia tender survey.

5. Painful peripheral neuropathies — DPN, CIPN, SFN
  • DPN: most common painful PN. First-line duloxetine, pregabalin, gabapentin, amitriptyline; topical capsaicin 8% in selected. Tight glycaemic control (more in T1DM than T2DM); modifiable CV risk factors.
  • CIPN: platinum, taxane, bortezomib, vincristine, thalidomide. Duloxetine is the only agent with positive RCT (Smith JAMA 2013). Cryotherapy / compression gloves limited evidence.
  • SFN: burning feet + allodynia + autonomic; conventional NCS normal. Diagnose with skin biopsy IENFD (gold standard) or QST. Causes: diabetes / pre-diabetes (OGTT essential), Sjögren, sarcoidosis, B12, HIV, paraneoplastic, ATTR amyloid (patisiran/vutrisiran/tafamidis — cross-ref Module 15), Fabry (α-galactosidase A → agalsidase ERT or migalastat), SCN9A/10A/11A channelopathies (erythromelalgia, paroxysmal extreme pain).
  • HIV neuropathy: distal symmetric painful sensory; HIV itself ± older ARVs (stavudine, didanosine).
  • Vasculitic neuropathy: stepwise asymmetric multiplex; immunotherapy + treat vasculitis.
6. Painful radiculopathy & chronic low back pain
  • Acute radiculopathy: dermatomal pain ± weakness / numbness / reflex loss; MRI for surgical planning, not first-line within 6 weeks.
  • Most resolve with conservative management; neuropathic pain agents for sustained radicular pain.
  • ESI: short-term radicular pain relief in selected; specialist; rare serious risks.
  • Surgery: progressive deficit, cauda equina (Module 10), persistent disabling pain > 6 weeks with MRI correlation.

Chronic LBP: leading global cause of YLD. Identify red flags (fever, weight loss, IVDU, cancer, > 50 or < 18, night pain, neurology, trauma). First-line stay active, avoid bed rest, education, exercise / physio, CBT or mindfulness, brief paracetamol or NSAID. Don't image without red flags < 6 weeks. Many patients have mixed phenotype (nociceptive + radicular + nociplastic). Specialist procedures (SCS, RF facet denervation, intrathecal drugs) have mixed evidence.

7. Herpes zoster & postherpetic neuralgia
  • Acute zoster: aciclovir 800 mg 5×/day or valaciclovir 1 g TID × 7 days within 72 h of rash reduces severity/duration. Pain control + early gabapentinoid/amitriptyline. Steroids reduce acute pain but do NOT prevent PHN.
  • PHN: pain > 90 days post-rash; risks: age (~30% if > 80), severe prodrome/rash, ophthalmic zoster, immunosuppression.
  • PHN Rx: gabapentin, pregabalin, TCA, lidocaine 5% patch, capsaicin 8% patch; opioids only refractory.
  • Shingrix recombinant zoster vaccine: 2-dose subunit; > 90% efficacy; preferred over withdrawn Zostavax; recommended ≥ 50 (≥ 19 if immunocompromised).
  • Ramsay Hunt: VZV geniculate; facial palsy + auricular vesicles + hearing/balance → valaciclovir + steroid.
8. Trigeminal neuralgia & other facial pain
  • Paroxysmal unilateral electric/stabbing facial pain in V2/V3 > V1; triggered by light touch, brushing teeth, chewing, cold air; older women.
  • Burchiel: TN1 (classical, purely paroxysmal) vs TN2 (with concomitant continuous pain) vs secondary (MS, tumour) or post-traumatic.
  • MRI brain with FIESTA/CISS — vascular loop (SCA) on root entry zone; exclude MS plaque (Module 8), tumour (Module 12), aneurysm. Bilateral / young / atypical → suspect MS.
  • First-line: carbamazepine 100 mg BD → 600–1200 mg/day (70–90% response). Oxcarbazepine better tolerated alternative. HLA-B*1502 test in Han Chinese/Thai/Filipino before CBZ (Stevens-Johnson). Watch hyponatraemia.
  • Second-line: lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin, pregabalin.
  • Surgery: MVD (Jannetta) ~90% long-term relief in classical TN, fit younger patients; Gamma Knife SRS ~70–80%, preferred in older/frail; percutaneous at Gasserian ganglion (RFA, glycerol, balloon) higher recurrence + facial numbness.

Other: glossopharyngeal neuralgia (CBZ + MVD); PIFP — continuous V2 ache, TCA/duloxetine + CBT; cluster/PH/HC/SUNCT (Module 4); TMD — conservative; burning mouth syndrome — clonazepam swish-and-spit, TCA, gabapentinoid.

9. Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)

Disproportionate pain after minor injury/surgery + vasomotor/sudomotor/oedema/motor/trophic changes. Type 1 (no defined nerve lesion) vs Type 2 (defined nerve injury).

Budapest criteria (Harden 2010):
  1. Continuing disproportionate pain.
  2. ≥ 1 symptom in 3 of 4 categories: sensory (hyperalgesia, allodynia); vasomotor (temperature/colour asymmetry); sudomotor/oedema; motor/trophic (decreased ROM, weakness, tremor, dystonia; hair/nail/skin changes).
  3. ≥ 1 sign at evaluation in 2 of 4 categories.
  4. No other diagnosis better explains.
  • Cornerstone: early movement + rehabilitation — physio + OT + graded motor imagery + mirror box.
  • Pharm: gabapentinoids, TCAs, SNRIs; bisphosphonates (alendronate, neridronate) for early CRPS; ketamine infusion severe refractory; intrathecal baclofen if dystonia.
  • Sympathetic blocks — limited long-term effect.
  • CBT + fear-avoidance work.
  • Vitamin C 500 mg/day × 50 days after wrist fracture reduces CRPS incidence (Lancet 1999; Cochrane 2017 equivocal but widely used).
  • Spinal cord stimulation in refractory cases.
Partly maintained by disuse and cortical reorganisation. Restoring movement is the most important intervention. Drugs enable rehab; they don't cure CRPS.
10. Central pain syndromes
  • Central post-stroke pain (Dejerine-Roussy): thalamic or lateral medullary stroke; burning/constricting pain in affected body region; allodynia. First-line amitriptyline, gabapentinoid, lamotrigine; second-line duloxetine; rTMS/MCS specialist. Cross-ref Module 2.
  • MS pain: TN from pontine plaque (often bilateral, atypical, suspect MS in young — Module 8); painful tonic spasms; Lhermitte (also cervical compression, B12 deficiency, radiation myelopathy); central neuropathic burning.
  • SCI pain: at-level segmental neuropathic + below-level central deafferentation + nociceptive MSK from wheelchair (Module 10). Gabapentinoids, TCAs, intrathecal baclofen for spasticity.
  • Syringomyelia: dissociated sensory loss with burning (Module 10).
11. Fibromyalgia & overlap nociplastic syndromes

ACR 2016/2019: Widespread Pain Index (count regions) + Symptom Severity (fatigue, sleep, cognition, somatic); generalised pain (≥ 4 of 5 regions, axial); duration ≥ 3 months. Replaces 1990 tender-point criteria. Nociplastic mechanism (central sensitisation), not peripheral inflammation.

  • Investigations focused: RF, CCP, ESR/CRP, TSH, vitamin D; rule out PMR, depression, sleep apnea. Avoid elaborate workup unless red flags.
  • Treatment: education + self-management; graded aerobic exercise (strongest evidence — start very low, progress slowly; aquatic well tolerated); CBT, mindfulness; duloxetine (FDA), milnacipran (FDA), pregabalin (FDA), amitriptyline (off-label); tramadol with caution.
  • Avoid opioids and benzodiazepines.

Overlap: ME/CFS, IBS, interstitial cystitis, TMD, chronic pelvic pain, tension-type headache (Module 4), chronic LBP with central sensitisation, post-acute COVID-19 syndromes.

12. Cancer pain & opioid stewardship

WHO ladder for cancer pain. Adjuvants matter: bone pain (NSAID + bisphosphonate + palliative RT + radionuclides); neuropathic cancer pain (combine opioid with gabapentinoid + SNRI/TCA; regional/neuraxial procedures); visceral pain (opioid + antispasmodic; coeliac plexus block for upper abdo malignancy). Early palliative care input (Temel NEJM 2010).

Opioid stewardship in chronic non-cancer pain:

  • Time-limited goal-directed trial only after non-opioid pharm + rehab; document SOAPP/ORT; check PMP.
  • Reassess carefully at > 50 MME/day; avoid > 90 MME/day where possible (CDC 2022).
  • Co-prescribing risks: benzos (boxed warning), gabapentinoids (FDA 2019 boxed warning), alcohol, sedatives.
  • Take-home naloxone for patient + family.
  • Slow tapering (5–10% every 1–4 weeks); never abrupt discontinuation — withdrawal, mental-health crisis, suicide.
  • OUD: methadone or buprenorphine MAT (Module 13).

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 62-year-old with T2DM (HbA1c 8.5%) has bilateral burning feet, worse at night, with allodynia to bedsheets. Exam: reduced pinprick and vibration to mid-calf, absent ankle reflexes

DN4 score 6/10. NCS shows length-dependent axonal sensorimotor neuropathy.

Diagnosis: Painful diabetic polyneuropathy (definite neuropathic pain by NeuPSIG).

Plan:

  1. Tighten glycaemic control (cautious — symptomatic improvement is modest in T2DM); address cardiovascular risk (BP, lipids, smoking, exercise).
  2. First-line: choose one based on comorbidities — duloxetine 30 mg daily titrated to 60 mg (good evidence in DPN; mood benefit); OR pregabalin 75 mg BD → 300 mg/day (rapid effect, renal dose adjustment); OR gabapentin 300 mg TDS → 1800–3600 mg/day; OR amitriptyline 10–25 mg nocte → 75 mg (caution in elderly — anticholinergic, cardiac).
  3. Add topical capsaicin 8% patch q3 months for selected focal areas.
  4. Foot care education — daily inspection, footwear, podiatry; ulcer prevention.
  5. Counsel about expected effect size (~30% pain reduction in good responders); set functional goals; reassess in 4–6 weeks.
  6. Avoid opioids as first-line; reserve tramadol or strong opioids for refractory cases under specialist supervision.
Case 2 — A 72-year-old woman has 2 months of right-sided lancinating facial pain in V2–V3 distribution, triggered by brushing her teeth and a breeze. Pain-free between attacks. No sensory deficit

Pain duration 1–2 seconds in volleys. MRI brain (FIESTA): superior cerebellar artery loop on the right trigeminal root entry zone.

Diagnosis: Classical trigeminal neuralgia (Burchiel TN1; ICHD-3 "purely paroxysmal").

Plan:

  1. Carbamazepine 100 mg BD, titrated to 600–1200 mg/day in divided doses for response — first-line; 70–90% effective initially.
  2. Pre-treatment HLA-B*1502 testing in Han Chinese / Thai / Filipino patients (Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk). Monitor sodium (hyponatraemia), CBC, LFTs.
  3. If CBZ intolerant: oxcarbazepine 300 mg BD → 1200–1800 mg/day (better tolerated, fewer drug interactions, similar efficacy; higher hyponatraemia risk).
  4. Second-line agents: lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin, pregabalin.
  5. If drug-refractory or intolerant: discuss surgical options at multidisciplinary review — microvascular decompression (MVD/Jannetta) ~90% long-term relief in fit patients; Gamma Knife SRS ~70–80% for older/frail; percutaneous Gasserian procedures (RFA, glycerol, balloon) for those preferring quick procedure but accepting recurrence and facial numbness.
  6. Counsel about MS — bilateral or young TN should prompt MS workup (Module 8).
Case 3 — A 68-year-old man developed a thoracic dermatomal vesicular rash 4 months ago. The rash healed but the pain persists — severe burning + allodynia in T8 dermatome, ESS-impaired sleep, withdrawn mood

DN4 score 7/10. No sensory deficit beyond the affected dermatome.

Diagnosis: Postherpetic neuralgia (definite neuropathic pain).

Plan:

  1. Education and validation; explain that PHN is a neuropathic-pain condition that often improves over months.
  2. First-line: gabapentin or pregabalin (titrate to effect); amitriptyline or nortriptyline (10–25 mg nocte → 75 mg); duloxetine 30 mg → 60 mg.
  3. Topical lidocaine 5% patch — well-tolerated, especially with allodynia.
  4. Capsaicin 8% patch single in-clinic application; reapply every 3 months if effective.
  5. Sleep hygiene + treat depression (consider duloxetine for dual benefit).
  6. Reserve opioids for refractory cases under specialist supervision.
  7. Prevention: Shingrix recombinant zoster vaccine (2 doses) for all adults ≥ 50 (≥ 19 if immunocompromised) — > 90% efficacy against zoster and PHN. Encourage this patient's friends and family.
Case 4 — A 45-year-old develops swelling, intense burning pain, hyperhidrosis, and a cool blue right hand 6 weeks after a minor wrist fracture. Pain is grossly out of proportion to the injury. Limited active range of motion, trophic skin changes

Budapest criteria: continuing disproportionate pain; sensory (hyperalgesia, allodynia); vasomotor (temperature/colour asymmetry); sudomotor (sweating asymmetry, oedema); motor/trophic (reduced ROM, skin changes) — symptoms in 4 of 4 categories, signs in ≥ 2 of 4 — criteria met for CRPS type 1.

Diagnosis: Complex regional pain syndrome type 1.

Plan:

  1. Early rehabilitation is the cornerstone — physiotherapy + occupational therapy + graded motor imagery + mirror box therapy.
  2. Pharmacotherapy: gabapentinoid or TCA or SNRI for neuropathic component; bisphosphonate (alendronate, neridronate — best evidence in early CRPS).
  3. Topical capsaicin 8% if focal allodynia.
  4. Sympathetic nerve block — short-term benefit only; specialist procedure.
  5. CBT, fear-avoidance work, education.
  6. Vitamin C 500 mg/day × 50 days post-wrist fracture reduces CRPS recurrence (low-risk, widely used).
  7. Refractory: ketamine infusion, intrathecal baclofen if dystonia, spinal cord stimulation.
  8. Counsel: restoring movement matters more than blunting pain. Avoid splinting and disuse.
Case 5 — A 38-year-old woman has 5 years of widespread pain, severe fatigue, non-restorative sleep, "fibro fog", and tension headaches. WPI 12, SS 8. Examination: diffuse tenderness, otherwise normal. ESR, CRP, RF, CCP, TSH normal

ACR 2016/2019 criteria met (WPI ≥ 7, SS ≥ 5, generalised pain, > 3 months, no alternative diagnosis).

Diagnosis: Fibromyalgia (nociplastic pain syndrome).

Plan:

  1. Education and validation: the pain is real but the mechanism is central sensitisation. Explain in plain language; offer reliable resources.
  2. Graded aerobic exercise — start very low (e.g., 5–10 min walking) and progress slowly; aquatic exercise often well tolerated; consistency beats intensity.
  3. CBT, mindfulness, sleep hygiene.
  4. Pharmacotherapy: duloxetine 30 mg → 60 mg (FDA-approved); milnacipran; pregabalin 75 mg BD → 300 mg/day; amitriptyline 10–25 mg nocte. Combine if monotherapy inadequate.
  5. Address comorbid mood / sleep disorders.
  6. Avoid opioids and benzodiazepines — limited efficacy and significant harms.
  7. Limit further investigations unless red flags appear; over-investigation reinforces illness behaviour.
  8. Cross-ref overlapping syndromes (IBS, ME/CFS, IC, TMD, chronic pelvic pain, post-acute COVID).
Case 6 — A 55-year-old has had chronic non-cancer low back pain on oxycodone 60 mg/day + benzodiazepine for 5 years. Function has declined. He wants help with the opioids

OME ~90 mg/day. Comorbid depression. PHQ-9 = 14.

Plan (opioid review + opioid-sparing pathway):

  1. Validate the patient's experience and his motivation to taper. Set shared functional goals (not pain score alone).
  2. Check prescription monitoring program; SOAPP/ORT/PMQ; UDS as appropriate.
  3. Co-prescribing risk review: benzodiazepine + opioid + gabapentinoid combination carries FDA boxed warnings for respiratory depression and death. Plan tapering of one agent at a time, generally starting with the highest-risk combination.
  4. Take-home naloxone — prescribe and train the patient and family.
  5. Slow opioid taper (5–10% every 1–4 weeks); manage withdrawal symptoms (autonomic, GI, anxiety); never abrupt discontinuation.
  6. Initiate or optimise opioid-sparing pharmacotherapy: duloxetine (mood + chronic pain), gabapentinoid (taper-cautious), topical lidocaine if focal; reassess at each step.
  7. Physiotherapy + graded exercise; CBT for chronic pain; treat depression; sleep optimisation.
  8. If opioid use disorder: medication-assisted treatment with methadone or buprenorphine (cross-ref Module 13).
  9. Refer to pain medicine and addiction medicine as needed; this is a team task.
  10. Document the plan, monitor closely, and adjust as conditions change.
