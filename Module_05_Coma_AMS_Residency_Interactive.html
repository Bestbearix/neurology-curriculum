Own the comatose patient

The general-physician Module 5 teaches the approach and prognosis. This version assumes you run the resuscitation, localize by brainstem exam, prognosticate after arrest with multimodal data, and lead a guideline-concordant brain-death determination.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Resuscitate and work up coma in parallel (glucose, thiamine, naloxone, labs, CT, EEG).
  2. Grade depth (GCS/FOUR) and localize the lesion by brainstem reflexes and motor pattern.
  3. Separate structural from toxic-metabolic coma and recognize herniation.
  4. Distinguish the disorders-of-consciousness states, including cognitive-motor dissociation.
  5. Apply post-arrest temperature management and multimodal prognostication.
  6. Perform the 2023-consensus brain-death/DNC determination.
Educational use only. Brain-death determination must follow current guidelines and your institution's/jurisdiction's legal policy.

Treat and diagnose simultaneously

Coma requires either diffuse bilateral hemispheric dysfunction or a brainstem ARAS lesion. Stabilize while working through reversible causes with iterative reassessment.

  1. ABCs; C-spine precautions if trauma possible.
  2. Fingerstick glucose; thiamine before/with glucose; dextrose if low.
  3. Naloxone for opioid toxidrome.
  4. Labs: electrolytes, Ca, renal/hepatic, ammonia, ABG, osmolar gap, tox screen, TSH, cortisol, cultures.
  5. Non-contrast CT early; LP if CNS infection/SAH; EEG for nonconvulsive status.
  6. Re-examine as data return.
Empiric thiamine for any unexplained AMS — Wernicke is not just an alcohol disease, and serum levels/MRI are insensitive early.
Consider nonconvulsive status epilepticus whenever depressed consciousness is out of proportion to the apparent cause (especially in sepsis, post-arrest).

Exam & localization

GCS (3–15) is the common language but omits brainstem and can't be fully scored when intubated; the FOUR score adds brainstem and respiration and works in intubated patients.

Pupillary lightMidbrainFixed dilated = CN III / uncal; pinpoint reactive = pons/opioid; preserved reactive in deep coma = metabolic

Motor: localizing = cortex relatively spared; decorticate (flexor) = above red nucleus; decerebrate (extensor) = midbrain/upper pons; flaccid = deep/metabolic.

Structural vs metabolic: asymmetric/focal signs, fixed or asymmetric pupils, gaze deviation, posturing → structural. Symmetric exam with preserved reactive pupils despite deep coma, asterixis/myoclonus → metabolic.
Blown pupil + declining GCS = uncal herniation: hyperosmolar therapy, head up, brief hyperventilation bridge, emergent neurosurgery.

The consciousness spectrum

Cognitive-motor dissociationPresentNo behavior, but command-following on fMRI/EEG

Covert consciousness is real. Behavioral scales misclassify some conscious patients; multimodal assessment (task fMRI/EEG, connectivity, heartbeat-evoked responses) detects cognitive-motor dissociation — so avoid overconfident early prognostication in disorders of consciousness.
Always exclude locked-in syndrome before calling a patient comatose — test vertical gaze and blink to command (ventral pontine/basilar lesion).

Post-cardiac-arrest care & prognostication

HACA (NEJM 2002). Mild hypothermia 32–34 °C for 24 h after out-of-hospital VF arrest improved favorable neurologic outcome — established temperature management.
TTM trial (NEJM 2013). 33 °C vs 36 °C — no difference in mortality (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.89–1.28) or neurologic outcome. Practice shifted to targeted temperature management with strict fever avoidance.
Prognostication is multimodal & delayed. Avoid early nihilism and sedation confounding. Combine exam (pupil/corneal reflexes, malignant myoclonus), EEG (very-early patterns, burst-suppression), SSEP (bilateral N20 loss), NSE, and MRI — no single test decides.

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A deeply unresponsive patient has symmetric, small, briskly reactive pupils, roving eye movements, and asterixis. No focal weakness.
Structural or metabolic?

Toxic-metabolic coma — symmetric exam with preserved reactive pupils despite deep unresponsiveness, plus asterixis. Pursue glucose, electrolytes, ammonia, ABG, tox screen, and give empiric thiamine; get an EEG for nonconvulsive status.

Case 2. A patient with a large hemispheric hemorrhage develops a new fixed, dilated right pupil and left hemiparesis, then declining consciousness.
What is happening and what do you do?

Uncal (transtentorial) herniation — CN III compression (blown pupil) with contralateral hemiparesis. Emergent hyperosmolar therapy (hypertonic saline/mannitol), head-of-bed up, brief hyperventilation as a bridge, and immediate neurosurgery.

Case 3. 48 h after resuscitation from VF arrest, a patient remains comatose off sedation. The team asks for prognosis.
How do you prognosticate?

Multimodal and not too early. Ensure temperature management and no sedation confounding; combine exam (pupil/corneal reflexes, malignant myoclonus), EEG, SSEP (bilateral N20 loss), NSE, and MRI. TTM showed 33 °C is not superior to 36 °C — the priority is targeted temperature control and fever avoidance, then delayed multimodal prognostication.
