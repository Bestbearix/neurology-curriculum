1. Framework — efferent vs afferent + sidedness

Neuro-ophthalmology is the most localising part of clinical neurology. Every problem splits into two systems and then into a side:

  • Afferent — input pathway (retina → optic nerve → chiasm → tract → LGN → optic radiations → occipital cortex). Disease causes visual loss or field defects.
  • Efferent — output pathway (cortical and subcortical gaze centres → MLF → CN III, IV, VI → muscles, or the pupillary motor pathway). Disease causes diplopia, nystagmus, anisocoria, or gaze palsy.
  • Sidedness — monocular (in front of the chiasm) vs binocular (chiasm or behind). One eye covered → does it go away? — instant 2-second triage.
The single principle: ask three questions and you have the diagnosis nearly cornered — (1) afferent or efferent, (2) one eye or both, (3) acute, subacute, or chronic. With these three, every patient lands in a small differential.

2. Visual pathway anatomy

  1. Retina → photoreceptors → bipolar cells → ganglion cells → ~1.2 million axons per side form the optic nerve. The macula carries central vision, with the highest cone density at the fovea.
  2. Optic nerve (CN II) — myelinated by oligodendrocytes (so a CNS structure, not a true cranial nerve in embryologic terms). Travels through the optic canal carrying nasal fibres (which will cross) and temporal fibres (which will not).
  3. Optic chiasm — nasal fibres from each eye cross to the contralateral optic tract; temporal fibres remain ipsilateral. Sits above the pituitary, just below the third ventricle.
  4. Optic tract — carries fibres from both eyes representing the contralateral visual hemifield.
  5. Lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) — synapse in the thalamus; six-layered.
  6. Optic radiations:
    • Upper (parietal) — represent inferior visual field; contralateral inferior quadrantanopia from parietal lesion.
    • Lower (temporal — Meyer's loop) — represent superior visual field; contralateral superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky") from temporal lobe lesion.
  7. Primary visual cortex (V1, calcarine cortex, occipital pole) — retinotopically mapped; macular representation at the occipital pole with collateral blood supply from MCA, giving macular sparing in PCA strokes.

Two rules of thumb: (1) Congruity — the further back along the pathway the lesion, the more similar the field defects in each eye. Occipital lesions are perfectly congruous; tract lesions are incongruous. (2) Macular sparing implies cortical lesion (calcarine cortex with collateral MCA flow protects the macular representation at the occipital pole).

ONTT (Optic Neuritis Treatment Trial): IV methylprednisolone speeds recovery but does not improve final visual acuity. Oral prednisolone alone is contraindicated — increases recurrence (cross-ref Module 8). MRI brain at presentation predicts MS conversion (~50% over 15 years with ≥ 1 typical lesion).

Workup of suspected optic neuritis

  1. Visual acuity, colour vision (Ishihara — preferentially affected), RAPD, visual fields, dilated fundoscopy.
  2. Orbital and brain MRI with gadolinium + fat suppression.
  3. Anti-AQP4 (NMOSD) and anti-MOG (MOGAD) serology, especially if bilateral, severe, recurrent, or with myelitis / brain features.
  4. LP if MS workup needed (OCBs, IgG index).
  5. OCT (retinal nerve fibre layer + ganglion cell layer) — thinning develops over weeks; useful for follow-up.

Suspected GCA in any patient > 50 with new visual loss:
  • Start IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3 days, then oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day immediately — do not wait for biopsy.
  • ESR, CRP, FBC, LFTs — start urgent workup.
  • Temporal artery biopsy within 1–2 weeks (yield preserved up to 2 weeks of steroid). Skip lesions — adequate length (≥ 1 cm).
  • Temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign — replaces biopsy in many centres per EULAR 2018).
  • Add tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor antagonist; GiACTA trial 2017) as steroid-sparing in selected patients.
  • Aspirin 75 mg daily reduces ischaemic events.
  • Vitamin D + calcium + bisphosphonate; PCP prophylaxis if prolonged high-dose steroid.
  • Long taper over 12–24 months; relapse common — monitor ESR/CRP and symptoms.

7. Papilloedema and disc swelling differential

  • Papilloedema strictly means optic disc swelling due to raised intracranial pressure — bilateral, often more on one side; visual acuity initially preserved; transient visual obscurations (seconds, with posture change); enlarged blind spot; ± abducens palsy from raised ICP (false-localising).
  • Distinguish from optic disc swelling from other causes (more accurate term: "optic disc oedema"):
    • Optic neuritis (papillitis) — usually unilateral + visual loss + pain + RAPD.
    • AION (non-arteritic or arteritic) — unilateral, sudden, altitudinal defect.
    • Hypertensive emergency retinopathy.
    • Diabetic papillopathy.
    • CRVO — diffuse haemorrhages + cotton-wool spots ("blood and thunder").
    • Optic nerve infiltration (leukaemia, lymphoma, sarcoid, metastatic).
    • Toxic / methanol (Module 13).
    • Pseudo-papilloedema — optic disc drusen, hyperopic disc.

Workup of bilateral disc swelling

  1. Urgent neuroimaging — MRI brain with gadolinium + MR venogram (rule out mass and CVST, Module 2).
  2. If imaging unremarkable: LP with opening pressure in lateral decubitus; analyse CSF.
  3. If opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O (or > 28 in children) with normal CSF and unremarkable imaging → idiopathic intracranial hypertension (next section).
  4. Examine for systemic causes: hypertension, anaemia, hypoxia, dural sinus thrombosis (Module 2), space-occupying lesion (Module 12), meningitis (Module 11), drug toxicity (vitamin A, tetracyclines, retinoids, growth hormone — IIH look-alike).

8. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH)

  • Headache + papilloedema + transient visual obscurations + pulsatile tinnitus, especially in obese women of childbearing age. Cross-ref Module 4.
  • Modified Dandy criteria (Friedman 2013):
    1. Signs and symptoms of raised ICP (headache, TVOs, papilloedema, pulsatile tinnitus).
    2. No localising neurological signs (except CN VI palsy).
    3. Normal neuroimaging — MRI brain + MRV — with possible empty sella, optic nerve sheath enlargement, posterior globe flattening, transverse sinus stenosis.
    4. Elevated opening pressure on LP (> 25 cm H₂O adults; > 28 children) with normal CSF.
    5. No other cause identified (drug, secondary).
  • Visual fields formal perimetry — protect the patient's vision.
  • Management:
    • Weight loss — primary treatment; 6–10% weight reduction often resolves IIH.
    • Acetazolamide 250 mg BD titrated to 1–2 g/day (Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension Treatment Trial — IIHTT 2014).
    • Topiramate as alternative or adjunct (weight-loss benefit + CA inhibition).
    • Headache treatment (Module 4) — avoid medication-overuse headache.
    • Optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting (VP or LP) for fulminant or progressive vision loss.
    • Venous sinus stenting in selected cases with significant transverse sinus stenosis.

9. Pupillary disorders

Examination essentials

  • Inspect: size, shape, anisocoria.
  • Light response — direct and consensual.
  • Near response (accommodation).
  • Swinging flashlight test for RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil): paradoxical dilation of the affected eye when light swung to it from the normal eye — implies asymmetric afferent pathway disease anterior to the chiasm.
  • Anisocoria: worse in dark = poorly-dilating pupil is the abnormal one (Horner, atropine-blocked); worse in light = poorly-constricting pupil is the abnormal one (CN III, Adie, pharmacologic).

Light-near dissociation — causes

  • Dorsal midbrain syndrome (Parinaud) — pretectal lesion (pineal tumour, hydrocephalus, MS, stroke) → upgaze palsy + light-near dissociation + lid retraction (Collier sign) + convergence-retraction nystagmus.
  • Adie tonic pupil.
  • Argyll-Robertson pupils (neurosyphilis).
  • Severe afferent disease (bilateral blindness).
  • DM, MS.

10. Diplopia — approach

Three questions answer almost every diplopia:

  1. Monocular or binocular? (Cover each eye separately — monocular persists with one eye covered → refractive/lens/retinal/dry eye/functional. Binocular resolves with one eye covered → ocular motility problem.)
  2. Horizontal, vertical, or oblique? Worse in which direction of gaze? At what distance? With head tilt?
  3. Constant or variable? Fatigable (ocular MG — Module 9), painful (orbital, dissection, GCA), pupillary involvement (CN III + posterior communicating artery aneurysm).

11. Isolated CN III, IV, VI palsies

CN III (oculomotor)

  • Innervates medial, superior, inferior rectus; inferior oblique; levator palpebrae; parasympathetic to pupil and ciliary muscle.
  • Complete palsy: ptosis + eye "down and out" + dilated unreactive pupil (if compressive).
  • Pupil-involving CN III palsy = posterior communicating artery aneurysm until proven otherwise → urgent CTA or MRA. Parasympathetic fibres run on the surface of CN III — most vulnerable to external compression.
  • Pupil-sparing CN III palsy in a vasculopathic patient (DM, HTN, age > 50) — usually ischaemic (microvascular); recovers over 3 months; image if any atypical features, age < 50, or no recovery.
  • Other causes: midbrain stroke (with Weber syndrome — contralateral hemiparesis), cavernous sinus / superior orbital fissure syndromes, basal meningitis, trauma, uncal herniation.

CN IV (trochlear)

  • Innervates superior oblique. Longest intracranial course → most vulnerable to head trauma.
  • Vertical or oblique diplopia worse on contralateral gaze and ipsilateral head tilt; head tilted away from the affected side compensates (Bielschowsky head tilt test).
  • Most common causes: congenital (often decompensates in adulthood with old photos showing head tilt), trauma, microvascular ischaemic.

CN VI (abducens)

  • Innervates lateral rectus. Longest extracranial course; vulnerable to raised ICP — false-localising sign in IIH and other raised-ICP states.
  • Causes: microvascular ischaemic, raised ICP, brainstem stroke (with horizontal gaze palsy — see INO below), tumour at clivus / cerebellopontine angle, GCA, basal meningitis, trauma.
  • Painful CN VI palsy → consider GCA in elderly, cavernous sinus pathology, Tolosa-Hunt syndrome.

12. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia and gaze palsies

INO

  • Lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) between abducens and oculomotor nuclei.
  • On attempted horizontal gaze: failure of adduction of the ipsilateral eye + abducting nystagmus of the contralateral eye + preserved convergence.
  • "Adduction failure on the side of the lesion".
  • Causes: MS in younger patients (often bilateral; cross-ref Module 8); brainstem stroke in older patients (often unilateral).
  • "One-and-a-half syndrome" — INO + ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy from concurrent abducens nucleus or PPRF lesion; only one eye moves (the contralateral eye in abduction).

Horizontal gaze centre — PPRF (paramedian pontine reticular formation)

PPRF lesion → ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy.

Vertical gaze centre — dorsal midbrain (riMLF, INC)

Dorsal midbrain syndrome (Parinaud): upgaze palsy + light-near dissociation + lid retraction (Collier) + convergence-retraction nystagmus → pineal tumour, hydrocephalus, MS, stroke.

Supranuclear gaze palsies

  • Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) — vertical gaze palsy + axial rigidity + early falls (cross-ref Module 6).
  • Niemann-Pick C — vertical supranuclear gaze palsy in a young patient with cognitive features.
  • Frontal eye fields stroke → contralateral conjugate gaze paresis (patient "looks toward the lesion").

14. Chiasmal lesions and pituitary apoplexy

  • Bitemporal hemianopia — pathognomonic when fields are formally tested.
  • Common causes: pituitary adenoma (Module 12) — macroadenoma compresses chiasm from below first affecting superior temporal fibres ("upper outer bitemporal"); craniopharyngioma compresses from above; meningioma of tuberculum sellae.
  • Pituitary apoplexy (cross-ref Module 12): thunderclap headache + ophthalmoplegia (CN III then IV/VI from cavernous sinus involvement) + bitemporal hemianopia + acute panhypopituitarism. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat, urgent MRI, neurosurgery for visual / oculomotor compromise.

15. Traumatic optic neuropathy and orbital syndromes

  • Traumatic optic neuropathy: deceleration injury (often from blunt trauma) → optic canal shearing. Sudden monocular visual loss with RAPD; disc initially normal; pallor over weeks. Steroid use controversial (CRASH trial — no benefit in TBI overall; optic-specific trials inconclusive); decompression of the optic canal in selected cases.
  • Orbital fracture with entrapment: vertical diplopia, restricted upgaze; "white-eyed blowout" in children — paediatric orbital floor fracture without external signs, urgent surgical release.
  • Cavernous sinus syndrome: CN III, IV, VI, V1 (and sometimes V2) deficits + ipsilateral Horner (sympathetic fibres travel with ICA); causes — tumour (meningioma, lymphoma, mets), cavernous sinus thrombosis (proptosis + chemosis + fever + ↑↑ inflammation), carotid-cavernous fistula (pulsatile proptosis + bruit + chemosis), Tolosa-Hunt syndrome (painful ophthalmoplegia, idiopathic granulomatous inflammation responsive to steroids).
  • Superior orbital fissure syndrome: III, IV, V1, VI; spares optic nerve.
  • Orbital apex syndrome: above + optic nerve involvement.
  • Thyroid eye disease (Graves orbitopathy): lid retraction, proptosis, restrictive ophthalmoplegia (especially upgaze and abduction limitation; "IMSLO" — inferior rectus most affected first), optic nerve compression in severe cases. Smoking cessation; selenium; IV methylprednisolone for active disease; teprotumumab (IGF-1R mAb, FDA 2020) transformative for active moderate-severe disease.

16. Neuro-ophthalmic emergencies

  • Suspected GCA — IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3 days then 1 mg/kg/day before biopsy.
  • Pupil-involving CN III palsy — urgent CTA/MRA for PCommA aneurysm.
  • Pituitary apoplexy — hydrocortisone IV stat + urgent MRI + neurosurgery if visual/oculomotor compromise (Module 12).
  • Sudden bilateral disc swelling — MRI + MRV first (rule out mass + CVST), then LP — IIH workup.
  • Sudden monocular visual loss in older patient — GCA workup; in younger patient — optic neuritis (MRI orbits/brain ± antibodies) or vascular (TIA pathway, Module 2).
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis — IV antibiotics + anticoagulation + ENT/ophthalmology + neurosurgery; high mortality.
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma — IOP lowering + iridotomy.
  • Anti-AQP4 / anti-MOG optic neuritis with severe vision loss — IV methylprednisolone + plasma exchange (consider early in severe / refractory cases).

17. When to refer

  • Same day: any of the emergencies above.
  • Within 1 week: new optic neuritis (neurology + ophthalmology); bitemporal hemianopia for pituitary workup; new INO for MS workup; CN III/IV/VI palsy with atypical features; unexplained anisocoria with pharmacologic testing.
  • Routine: established IIH on therapy; chronic CN palsies; functional visual loss; congenital nystagmus; thyroid eye disease in remission.
  • Always involve: ophthalmology / neuro-ophthalmology, neurosurgery for compressive lesions, endocrinology for pituitary disease, rheumatology for GCA, ENT for cavernous sinus / orbital infections.
