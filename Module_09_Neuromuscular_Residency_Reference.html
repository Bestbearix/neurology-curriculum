1. Localizing along the motor unit & electrodiagnosis

The neuromuscular exam is an exercise in localizing along the motor unit: anterior horn cell → root → plexus → peripheral nerve → neuromuscular junction → muscle. Each level has a signature (cross-links to the localization logic of Module 1).

Electrodiagnostics (NCS/EMG)

Interpret EDX in two axes. Nerve conduction studies distinguish axonal loss (reduced amplitudes, relatively preserved conduction velocity) from demyelination (slowed velocity, prolonged distal latency, conduction block, temporal dispersion, prolonged F-waves). Needle EMG localizes and dates: fibrillations/positive sharp waves indicate active denervation; large, long-duration, polyphasic motor units with reduced recruitment indicate chronic neurogenic reorganization; small, short, early-recruiting units suggest myopathy. Repetitive nerve stimulation and single-fibre EMG interrogate the junction — a decrement at low rates in myasthenia, an increment after exercise / high-rate stimulation in presynaptic disorders (LEMS, botulism).

EDX answers the localization question the exam raised — it should be ordered with a hypothesis, not as a fishing expedition. A stepwise NCS interpretation (amplitude → velocity/latency → block/dispersion → F-waves) prevents missed demyelination.

2. Anterior horn cell: ALS & motor neuron disease

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is defined by combined upper and lower motor neuron degeneration without sensory loss, spreading contiguously, with progressive weakness, atrophy, fasciculations, spasticity, dysarthria/dysphagia, and ultimately respiratory failure. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by EMG evidence of active and chronic denervation across multiple regions after excluding mimics (cervical myelopathy, MMN with conduction block, inclusion-body myositis, Kennedy disease, thyroid/parathyroid disease). Most cases are sporadic; TDP-43 proteinopathy underlies the majority, and pathogenic variants (C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, FUS) drive familial and some sporadic disease.

Disease-modifying therapy. Riluzole (anti-glutamatergic) confers a modest survival benefit and is standard. Tofersen — an intrathecal antisense oligonucleotide that lowers SOD1 synthesis — is the first approved gene-targeted ALS therapy, indicated only for SOD1-variant ALS; the VALOR trial and its open-label extension show reductions in neurofilament (a biomarker of neurodegeneration) and support long-term benefit, underscoring the value of early genetic testing. Additional agents (edaravone; the PARADIGM/PrimeC and SOD1 siRNA programmes in the resource set) reflect an accelerating pipeline, and plasma proteomic panels are being developed for prodromal detection.
Multidisciplinary care is the intervention with the largest effect on survival and quality of life: early non-invasive ventilation, nutrition/PEG timing, secretion and spasticity management, communication aids, and proactive goals-of-care discussion. Screen for the ~15% with frontotemporal dysfunction.

3. Genetic & treatable MND mimics

Two "MND-like" disorders must not be missed because they behave very differently. Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) — biallelic SMN1 loss — is now treatable with SMN-directed therapy (the resource set includes a phase-3 high-dose nusinersen trial), transforming a formerly fatal disease. Kennedy disease (spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy) — an X-linked CAG-repeat expansion in the androgen receptor — presents with slowly progressive bulbar and proximal weakness, prominent perioral fasciculations, gynecomastia and androgen insensitivity, and a sensory neuronopathy; its slow course and lack of UMN signs distinguish it from ALS, and genetic testing spares patients an ALS diagnosis.

4. Peripheral neuropathy: the diagnostic framework

Characterize every neuropathy along several axes, because the pattern narrows the cause: fibre type (motor/sensory/autonomic/mixed), distribution (length-dependent symmetric vs multifocal/mononeuritis multiplex vs non-length-dependent/ganglionopathy), tempo (acute/subacute/chronic), and pathology on NCS (axonal vs demyelinating). A symmetric length-dependent axonal sensory-predominant polyneuropathy is usually metabolic/toxic (diabetes, alcohol, B12, drugs, chemotherapy); a demyelinating pattern raises acquired immune (GBS/CIDP) or hereditary (CMT) causes; multifocal patterns suggest vasculitis, MMN, or infiltration. Non-length-dependent sensory loss with sensory ataxia points to a sensory ganglionopathy (paraneoplastic anti-Hu, Sjögren, cisplatin, RFC1/CANVAS).

Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy is common and often dose-limiting; per the provided ASCO guideline, prevention options are limited (no agent is firmly recommended for prevention), and duloxetine has the best evidence for painful CIPN — a high-yield practical point.

5. Guillain-Barré syndrome

GBS is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy: rapidly progressive, usually ascending, areflexic weakness ± sensory and autonomic involvement, often post-infectious (Campylobacter, viral, and post-vaccination/SARS-CoV-2 associations in the resource set), with albuminocytologic dissociation in CSF and supportive NCS. Variants include AMAN/AMSAN (axonal) and Miller Fisher syndrome (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia; anti-GQ1b).

Management (EAN/PNS and the "GBS in ten steps" guidance, provided). IVIG (0.4 g/kg/day ×5 days) and plasma exchange are equally effective; corticosteroids are NOT effective and should not be used. The cornerstone is anticipatory monitoring: up to ~20–30% develop respiratory failure needing ventilation, and autonomic instability (arrhythmia, labile BP) can be lethal — monitor vital capacity and telemetry, and intubate on trajectory rather than waiting for a crisis. Prognosis can be estimated (Erasmus GBS Outcome Score); the SID-GBS trial evaluated a second IVIG dose in poor-prognosis patients.
Never treat GBS with steroids. Monitor forced vital capacity serially (the "20/30/40 rule" — see §10) and admit any rapidly progressive case to a monitored bed.

6. CIDP, MMN & the chronic immune neuropathies

CIDP

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy is a treatable acquired demyelinating neuropathy: progressive or relapsing, typically symmetric proximal AND distal weakness with sensory involvement over >8 weeks, areflexia, elevated CSF protein, and demyelinating NCS. The 2021 EAN/PNS guideline replaced "atypical CIDP" with "CIDP variants" (distal/DADS, multifocal/MADSAM, focal, motor, sensory).

CIDP therapy (EAN/PNS, provided). First-line is IVIG or corticosteroids (both strongly recommended); plasma exchange if those fail; IVIG is preferred first-line in motor CIDP. Maintenance uses IVIG/SCIG or steroids. Newer options in the resource set include the FcRn inhibitor efgartigimod (ADHERE trial) and the (negative) FORCIDP fingolimod trial. Crucially, confirm the diagnosis and exclude mimics before committing to long-term immunotherapy — statistical-modeling and case-series papers show CIDP is frequently overdiagnosed.

Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN)

MMN is a pure motor, asymmetric, chronically progressive neuropathy with motor conduction block at non-compressible sites, often anti-GM1 antibodies (elevated in <half), and cold paresis — a critical ALS mimic because it is treatable. Per the EFNS/PNS guideline, IVIG is first-line; corticosteroids and plasma exchange can worsen MMN and are avoided.

MMN masquerades as ALS — asymmetric pure-motor weakness with conduction block and no UMN signs deserves an IVIG trial before an ALS label is finalized.

7. Mononeuropathies & plexopathies

Carpal tunnel syndrome (median at the wrist) is the commonest entrapment: nocturnal paraesthesiae, thenar wasting when advanced. The provided trials inform management — for confirmed CTS, surgical decompression outperforms steroid injection/splinting for durable relief (DISTRICTS; long-term open-vs-endoscopic follow-up shows both surgical techniques effective), while splint/injection help milder/early disease. Other common entrapments: ulnar (elbow), fibular/peroneal (fibular head — footdrop with spared inversion/ankle jerk distinguishing it from L5 radiculopathy). Neuralgic amyotrophy (Parsonage-Turner) — abrupt severe shoulder-girdle pain followed by patchy weakness/atrophy, often post-infectious/immune. Diabetic lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy) — an immune-mediated microvasculitic radiculoplexopathy causing painful asymmetric proximal leg weakness and weight loss; also cervical and non-diabetic forms exist. Also consider radiation-induced plexopathy (painless, myokymia) versus neoplastic (painful).

8. Neuromuscular junction: MG, LEMS, botulism

Myasthenia gravis

MG is a postsynaptic autoimmune NMJ disorder causing fatigable, fluctuating weakness with ocular (ptosis, diplopia), bulbar, and limb involvement. Antibodies: AChR (most), MuSK (bulbar-predominant, IVIG/rituximab-responsive), LRP4. Confirm with antibodies, RNS (decrement) or single-fibre EMG (jitter), the ice-pack test for ptosis, and CT chest for thymoma.

Treatment (ABN 2025 guideline + the new biologics, provided). Symptomatic pyridostigmine; immunotherapy with corticosteroids and steroid-sparing agents; thymectomy for thymoma and for AChR-positive generalised disease (randomised evidence favours early thymectomy). Crises are treated with IVIG or plasma exchange. A new generation of targeted biologics has arrived: complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab, zilucoplan), FcRn inhibitors (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab), anti-IL-6 (satralizumab, LUMINESCE), anti-CD19 (inebilizumab), and investigational siRNA (cemdisiran/NIMBLE), oral factor-B (vemircopan), and even BCMA CAR-T — reserved for refractory generalised MG. The resource set also addresses standardised outcome assessment and family-planning/pregnancy counseling.
Avoid drugs that worsen MG (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, magnesium, telithromycin, and abrupt high-dose steroids which can cause transient worsening). In crisis, prioritize airway and ventilation.

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome

LEMS is a presynaptic disorder from antibodies to P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing ACh release. Triad: proximal weakness (legs), autonomic features (dry mouth), and hyporeflexia that facilitates after exercise. NCS show a low baseline CMAP with post-exercise/high-rate increment. ~50% are paraneoplastic (small-cell lung cancer — always hunt for tumour). Treat the tumour; symptomatic amifampridine (3,4-diaminopyridine) ± immunotherapy.

Botulism

Botulinum toxin blocks presynaptic ACh release, producing symmetric descending flaccid paralysis beginning with cranial nerves (diplopia, ptosis, dysarthria, dysphagia), with autonomic features (dilated pupils, dry mouth, ileus) and no fever, normal sensation, and preserved mentation. Sources: foodborne (the provided outbreak reports), wound, infant, and iatrogenic. Treatment is early botulinum antitoxin (do not await confirmation) plus meticulous respiratory support; endopeptidase-mass-spectrometry enables rapid diagnosis.

The NMJ mimics each other and GBS. Descending pattern + pupils + autonomic signs → botulism; ascending + areflexia + albuminocytologic dissociation → GBS; fatigable ocular/bulbar → MG. RNS/SFEMG and antibodies resolve the ambiguity.

9. Muscle disease

Inflammatory myopathies

Reclassified into five subtypes: dermatomyositis (proximal weakness + rash: heliotrope, Gottron; MDA5 with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease; malignancy association), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (severe weakness, very high CK; anti-HMGCR — statin-associated — and anti-SRP), antisynthetase syndrome (anti-Jo-1; ILD, mechanic's hands, arthritis), overlap myositis, and inclusion-body myositis (older men, finger-flexor and quadriceps weakness, treatment-refractory — an ALS/CIDP mimic). Diagnosis integrates the clinical phenotype, myositis-specific antibodies, MRI, and biopsy.

Muscular dystrophies

Duchenne muscular dystrophy — X-linked dystrophin loss; the provided care standards emphasize corticosteroids (now including newer agents) plus cardiac and respiratory surveillance; emerging therapies include exon-skipping, gene therapy, and givinostat (EPIDYS). Other dystrophies (Becker, limb-girdle, FSHD, myotonic, oculopharyngodistal — with the recently described CGG/GGC repeat expansions in GIPC1/RILPL1, dystroglycanopathies) round out the genetic spectrum.

Metabolic & toxic myopathies

Consider mitochondrial myopathy (exercise intolerance, ptosis/ophthalmoplegia; the provided biomarker review), glycogen/lipid storage disorders, and statin-associated myopathy (spectrum from myalgia to the immune-mediated necrotizing HMGCR myopathy that persists after stopping the statin).

Channelopathies

Non-dystrophic myotonias and periodic paralyses arise from mutations in muscle ion-channel genes — SCN4A (sodium; paramyotonia congenita, hyper/hypokalemic paralysis), CLCN1 (chloride; myotonia congenita), CACNA1S (calcium; hypokalemic periodic paralysis), and KCNJ2 (Andersen-Tawil). Episodic weakness ± myotonia with normal interictal strength is the clue; landmark trials support agents such as mexiletine for myotonia.

10. Neuromuscular emergencies & respiratory failure

The neuromuscular emergency that kills is respiratory failure, and it arrives before hypoxia on the pulse oximeter. Monitor forced vital capacity and inspiratory/expiratory pressures serially — the practical "20/30/40 rule" flags impending failure at FVC < 20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure less negative than −30 cm H₂O, or maximal expiratory pressure < 40 cm H₂O. A rising respiratory rate, weak cough, staccato speech, orthopnea, and paradoxical abdominal breathing are bedside red flags; a falling single-breath count corroborates. Intubate electively on the trajectory — do not wait for gas exchange to fail. Non-invasive ventilation is appropriate in selected chronic/slowly progressive disease but is generally avoided in rapidly worsening GBS/MG crisis with bulbar failure or secretions. ICU-acquired weakness (critical-illness polyneuropathy/myopathy) and diaphragmatic dysfunction complicate ventilated patients and impair weaning.

Never rely on oxygen saturation to detect neuromuscular respiratory failure — it is a late sign. Trend the vital capacity and act on the trend.

Envenomation is a global neuromuscular emergency covered in the resource set: elapid (cobra, krait) and Russell's viper venoms produce descending neuromuscular paralysis via pre- and post-synaptic neurotoxins — assess for progressive bulbar/respiratory paralysis, support ventilation, and give antivenom (with emerging small-molecule adjuncts such as varespladib under study).
