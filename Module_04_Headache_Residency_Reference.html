1. First, exclude danger: SNOOP red flags

Before assigning a primary-headache label, actively exclude secondary causes. The SNOOP mnemonic captures the high-yield red flags; any positive should prompt imaging and/or further workup.

FlagConcern
S — Systemic symptoms/signs (fever, weight loss); Secondary risk (cancer, HIV, immunosuppression, pregnancy)Meningitis, metastasis, opportunistic infection, CVST
N — Neurologic deficit or altered consciousnessMass, stroke, encephalitis
O — Onset sudden/thunderclap (maximal <1 min)Subarachnoid hemorrhage, RCVS, CVST, pituitary apoplexy
O — Older age of onset (>50)Giant cell arteritis, mass lesion
P — Pattern change / Positional / Precipitated by Valsalva / PapilloedemaRaised or low ICP, posterior-fossa lesion, IIH
Thunderclap headache demands non-contrast CT and, if negative with ongoing suspicion, LP for xanthochromia (SAH), plus consideration of CVST (venography) and RCVS. New headache >50 with jaw claudication/scalp tenderness → check ESR/CRP and treat suspected giant cell arteritis immediately to protect vision.

2. ICHD-3: primary vs secondary headache

The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) is the diagnostic standard. It divides disorders into primary (migraine, tension-type, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, other primary), secondary (attributed to trauma, vascular, non-vascular intracranial, substance/withdrawal, infection, homeostasis, structures of the head/neck, psychiatric), and painful cranial neuropathies (e.g., trigeminal neuralgia). Diagnosis is operational — a defined number of attacks meeting specific feature criteria — which is why counting attacks, duration, and associated symptoms matters.

3. Migraine: diagnosis & pathophysiology

Migraine without aura (ICHD-3 1.1): ≥5 attacks lasting 4–72 h with ≥2 of (unilateral, pulsating, moderate–severe, aggravated by routine activity) AND ≥1 of (nausea/vomiting, or photophobia and phonophobia), not better explained by another diagnosis. Migraine with aura (1.2): recurrent fully reversible aura (visual, sensory, speech/language, motor, brainstem, retinal), each aura symptom 5–60 min, followed within 60 min by headache. Chronic migraine (1.3): ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months, of which ≥8 have migraine features.

Pathophysiology (resident depth): migraine is a disorder of brain excitability, not a "vascular headache." The aura corresponds to cortical spreading depression. Pain is generated by activation of the trigeminovascular system with release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and neurogenic inflammation, and involves the trigeminocervical complex and central sensitization (clinically, cutaneous allodynia). This CGRP biology is the rationale for the gepants and anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies. Timing/chronobiology and endocannabinoid modulation are areas of active study represented in the resource set.

Migraine is a systemic risk marker too. The provided AMPP analysis notes elevated Framingham-based cardiovascular risk estimates among people with episodic migraine — relevant when choosing therapies (e.g., avoid triptans/ergots in uncontrolled vascular disease).

4. Migraine — acute treatment

Treat early and at adequate dose; match agent to severity and comorbidity, and cap frequency to prevent medication-overuse headache.

ClassExamplesNotes
Simple analgesics/NSAIDsAspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, acetaminophenFirst step for mild–moderate
Triptans (5-HT₁B/₁D)Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptanMainstay for moderate–severe; contraindicated in coronary/cerebrovascular disease, uncontrolled HTN
Gepants (CGRP-receptor antagonists)Ubrogepant, rimegepantNo vasoconstriction — usable when triptans contraindicated or ineffective

Antiemetics/adjunctsMetoclopramide, prochlorperazineAid absorption and nausea
Ubrogepant (ACHIEVE program). An oral CGRP-receptor antagonist; in the placebo-controlled single-attack trials, ubrogepant (e.g., 50 mg) met the co-primary endpoints of 2-hour pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome associated symptom (photophobia, phonophobia, or nausea). Offers an acute option without triptan-type vasoconstriction.
Rimegepant. A gepant effective for both acute and preventive use (acute 75 mg; preventive every-other-day dosing) — a useful dual-purpose agent, and the provided safety data support combining it with anti-CGRP mAbs in selected patients.

5. Migraine — preventive treatment

Offer prevention when attacks are frequent (broadly ≥4 migraine days/month with impairment), long, disabling, or when acute therapy is contraindicated/overused. Set expectations (≈50% reduction is a success), titrate slowly, and give each oral agent an adequate trial (~8 weeks at target dose).

Established oral preventives

  • Topiramate — Cochrane-level evidence of efficacy for episodic migraine (≥50%-responder benefit vs placebo); watch cognitive effects, paresthesia, weight loss, nephrolithiasis, and teratogenicity.
  • Beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol), candesartan, amitriptyline, and valproate (avoid in pregnancy) — choose by comorbidity.

CGRP-targeted prevention (the practice-changing class)

Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies — erenumab (receptor), fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab (ligand). RCTs across episodic and chronic migraine (e.g., fremanezumab and galcanezumab pivotal trials, and the FOCUS trial in patients with 2–4 prior preventive failures) show reduced monthly migraine days with favorable tolerability, including in difficult-to-treat and multiple-prior-failure populations.
EHF 2022 guideline. The European Headache Federation found moderate-to-high-quality evidence to recommend eptinezumab, erenumab, fremanezumab, and galcanezumab for migraine prevention. Increasingly these are argued to be a first-line preventive option rather than reserved after multiple oral failures.
OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT). For chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month), the PREEMPT paradigm — 155 units across 31 fixed injection sites every 12 weeks — reduces headache days versus placebo. It is a chronic-migraine treatment (not indicated for episodic migraine).
Sequencing & response. Non-response to one CGRP mAb does not preclude response to another or to switching class; the provided galcanezumab non-responder analyses show some initial non-responders convert with continued therapy. Reassess at ~3 months.

6. Cluster headache & the TACs

Cluster headache is the prototypical trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia (TAC): severe strictly unilateral orbital/temporal pain lasting 15–180 min, occurring up to several times daily in bouts, with ipsilateral cranial autonomic features (lacrimation, conjunctival injection, rhinorrhea, ptosis/miosis) and a sense of restlessness/agitation (distinguishing it from migraine, where patients seek stillness).

PhaseTreatment
Acute attackHigh-flow 100% oxygen (12–15 L/min via non-rebreather) and subcutaneous sumatriptan
Transitional (bridge)Greater occipital nerve block; short oral corticosteroid course
PreventiveVerapamil (first-line; monitor ECG/PR interval); lithium, topiramate; galcanezumab for episodic cluster

Other TACs — paroxysmal hemicrania (absolutely responsive to indomethacin) and SUNCT/SUNA — must be distinguished, as management differs.

7. Tension-type headache

The most prevalent primary headache: bilateral, pressing/tightening (non-pulsatile), mild–moderate, not aggravated by routine activity, without the migrainous associated-symptom burden. Managed with simple analgesics/NSAIDs for episodic disease; for frequent/chronic TTH, amitriptyline is first-line preventive, alongside addressing sleep, stress, posture, and limiting analgesic frequency to avoid overuse.

8. Medication-overuse headache

MOH is headache present ≥15 days/month developing in a patient with a pre-existing primary headache who overuses acute medication (≥10 days/month for triptans, ergots, opioids, or combination analgesics; ≥15 days/month for simple analgesics) for >3 months. Management combines patient education, withdrawal of the overused agent, and starting an effective preventive. The provided evidence shows onabotulinumtoxinA and anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies can help patients transition out of overuse without a mandatory prior detoxification in many cases.

Butalbital and opioids are the highest-risk agents for MOH and chronification — avoid them for migraine and taper carefully when overused.
