1 Exclude danger first: SNOOP red flags

▎Key points
  • Assign a primary-headache label only after actively excluding secondary causes.
  • SNOOP captures the high-yield red flags; any positive → imaging and/or further workup.
  • Two can't-miss patterns: thunderclap (SAH/RCVS/CVST) and new headache >50 with GCA features (treat before biopsy to save vision).
FlagConcern
S — Systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss); Secondary risk (cancer, HIV, immunosuppression, pregnancy)Meningitis, metastasis, opportunistic infection, CVST
N — Neurologic deficit or altered consciousnessMass, stroke, encephalitis
O — Onset sudden/thunderclap (peak <1 min)Subarachnoid hemorrhage, RCVS, CVST, pituitary apoplexy

P — Pattern change / Positional / Precipitated by Valsalva / PapilloedemaRaised or low ICP, posterior-fossa lesion, IIH
Thunderclap headache → non-contrast CT; if negative with ongoing suspicion, LP for xanthochromia (SAH), plus consider CVST (venography) and RCVS. New headache >50 with jaw claudication/scalp tenderness → ESR/CRP and treat suspected GCA immediately to protect vision (biopsy can follow).

2 ICHD-3: primary vs secondary

▎Key points
  • ICHD-3 divides headache into primary, secondary (attributed to another disorder), and painful cranial neuropathies.
  • Diagnosis is operational — a defined number of attacks meeting specific feature criteria.
  • Count attacks, duration and associated symptoms — that's what separates migraine from TTH from a TAC.
GroupExamples
PrimaryMigraine, tension-type, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (cluster, paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT/SUNA), other primary (cough, exercise, sexual, hypnic, thunderclap, cold-stimulus, nummular)
SecondaryAttributed to trauma, vascular (SAH, CVST, RCVS, GCA), non-vascular intracranial (raised/low ICP, tumor), substance/withdrawal, infection, homeostasis, cranial/cervical structures, psychiatric
Painful cranial neuropathiesTrigeminal neuralgia, glossopharyngeal neuralgia, occipital neuralgia, Tolosa-Hunt

3 Migraine: diagnosis & pathophysiology

▎Key points
  • Without aura: ≥5 attacks, 4–72 h, ≥2 of (unilateral / pulsating / moderate-severe / worse with activity) + ≥1 of (nausea/vomiting or photophobia & phonophobia).
  • With aura: reversible aura (visual/sensory/speech/motor/brainstem/retinal), 5–60 min, ± headache within 60 min.
  • Chronic migraine: ≥15 headache days/month >3 months, ≥8 with migraine features.
  • Migraine is a disorder of brain excitability — aura = cortical spreading depression; pain = trigeminovascular activation + CGRP release (rationale for gepants/mAbs).
DiagnosisThresholdKey features
Migraine without aura (1.1)≥5 attacks, 4–72 h≥2 pain features + ≥1 associated symptom
Migraine with aura (1.2)≥2 attacksFully reversible aura 5–60 min
Chronic migraine (1.3)≥15 headache days/mo >3 mo≥8 migraine-feature days
Status migrainosusDebilitating attack >72 hConsider bridge therapy
Migraine is also a systemic risk marker. The AMPP analysis notes elevated Framingham-based cardiovascular risk among people with episodic migraine — relevant when choosing therapy (avoid triptans/ergots in uncontrolled vascular disease).

4 Migraine — acute treatment

▎Key points
  • Treat early and at adequate dose; match agent to severity and comorbidity.
  • Gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant) and ditans (lasmiditan) have no vasoconstriction → usable when triptans are contraindicated.
  • Cap acute-drug frequency to prevent medication-overuse headache (§8).
ClassExamplesNotes
Simple analgesics / NSAIDsAspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, acetaminophenFirst step for mild–moderate
Triptans (5-HT₁B/₁D)Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptanMainstay moderate–severe; avoid in coronary/cerebrovascular disease, uncontrolled HTN
Gepants (CGRP-R antagonists)Ubrogepant, rimegepantNo vasoconstriction; rimegepant is dual acute + preventive
Ditans (5-HT₁F)LasmiditanNo vasoconstriction; CNS/sedation, driving caution
Antiemetics / adjunctsMetoclopramide, prochlorperazineAid absorption & nausea
Ubrogepant (ACHIEVE). Oral CGRP-receptor antagonist; in single-attack RCTs, 50 mg met co-primary endpoints of 2-h pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome symptom — an acute option without triptan-type vasoconstriction.
Rimegepant. Effective for both acute (75 mg) and preventive (every-other-day) use; safety data support combining it with anti-CGRP mAbs in selected patients.

5 Migraine — preventive treatment

▎Key points
  • Offer prevention when attacks are frequent (≈≥4 migraine days/mo with impairment), long, disabling, or acute therapy is contraindicated/overused.
  • Set expectations (≈50% reduction = success), titrate slowly, give orals ~8 weeks at target dose.
  • Anti-CGRP mAbs (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) work even after multiple prior failures; increasingly argued first-line.
  • OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT) is for chronic migraine only.

CGRP-targeted prevention (the practice-changing class)

Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies. Erenumab (receptor), fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab (ligand). RCTs across episodic and chronic migraine — including FOCUS and LIBERTY in patients with multiple prior preventive failures — show reduced monthly migraine days with favorable tolerability.
EHF 2022 guideline. The European Headache Federation found moderate-to-high-quality evidence to recommend eptinezumab, erenumab, fremanezumab and galcanezumab for migraine prevention.
OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT). For chronic migraine, 155 units across 31 fixed sites every 12 weeks reduces headache days vs placebo. Not indicated for episodic migraine.
Sequencing. Non-response to one CGRP mAb doesn't preclude response to another or a class switch; some initial non-responders convert with continued therapy — reassess at ~3 months.

6 Cluster headache & the trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias

▎Key points
  • The TACs share strictly unilateral pain + ipsilateral cranial autonomic features; they separate on attack duration and frequency and on treatment response.
  • Cluster: 15–180 min attacks, up to 8/day, circadian/seasonal clustering, agitation/restlessness (patients pace — opposite of migraine); high suicidality during attacks.
  • Cluster acute = high-flow O₂ + SC/nasal sumatriptan; transitional = steroids / GON block; preventive = verapamil first-line (lithium/topiramate 2nd; galcanezumab for episodic).
  • The indomethacin-responsive TACs — paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua — must be identified because they are fully treatable.

Paroxysmal hemicrania2–30 min>5/day (often >20)ProminentIndomethacin — absolute response
Hemicrania continuaContinuous with exacerbationsUnremitting, side-lockedPresent during exacerbationsIndomethacin — absolute response
SUNCT / SUNA (SUNHA)1–600 sDozens–hundreds/daySUNCT: conjunctival injection + tearingLamotrigine; IV lidocaine; refractory → neuromodulation

Cluster diagnosis (ICHD-3): ≥5 attacks of severe unilateral orbital/supraorbital/temporal pain lasting 15–180 min untreated, with ≥1 ipsilateral autonomic sign (lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion/rhinorrhoea, eyelid oedema, forehead/facial sweating, miosis/ptosis) and/or restlessness. Episodic cluster = bouts separated by remissions ≥3 months; chronic = <3 months remission over a year.

Cluster is a medical emergency for the patient. Attacks are among the most severe pains in medicine with high suicidality ("suicide headache") — provide fast-acting abortive therapy (O₂ + injectable/nasal triptan), start a bridge, and never rely on oral analgesics. Women are under-diagnosed (male:female historically overstated).
Always trial indomethacin in a suspected short-lasting unilateral autonomic headache: an absolute response reclassifies "atypical cluster" as paroxysmal hemicrania or hemicrania continua and changes the whole treatment plan.

7 Tension-type headache

▎Key points
  • Most prevalent primary headache (~26% global) — bilateral, pressing/tightening (non-pulsatile), mild–moderate, not aggravated by routine activity, no nausea and at most one of photophobia/phonophobia.
  • Graded by frequency: infrequent episodic (<1 day/mo), frequent episodic (1–14 days/mo), chronic (≥15 days/mo).
  • Pericranial muscle tenderness + central sensitization in chronic TTH; addressing sleep, stress, posture and analgesic frequency is core.
  • Preventive of choice for frequent/chronic TTH is amitriptyline (network meta-analysis: amitriptyline ~100 mg ranked highest for reducing headache days).

Management by frequency

GradeApproach
Infrequent / frequent episodicAcute simple analgesics / NSAIDs (naproxen, ibuprofen, aspirin); lifestyle (sleep, stress, posture, hydration); limit acute-drug days to avoid MOH
Chronic TTHPreventive amitriptyline first-line (start low, titrate; ~100 mg most effective but more adverse effects); alternatives mirtazapine, venlafaxine; non-drug: biofeedback, CBT, physical therapy, exercise
Don't over-medicate TTH. Frequent analgesic use for "everyday headache" is a common route into medication-overuse headache; count acute-drug days and favour non-pharmacologic prevention. Botulinum toxin is not established for chronic TTH the way it is for chronic migraine (weak/uncertain evidence).
Reassess the label. "Chronic tension-type headache" that is disabling, throbbing, or comes with nausea/photophobia is frequently chronic migraine misclassified — re-take the history, because the effective treatments differ.

8 Medication-overuse headache

▎Key points
  • Headache ≥15 days/mo, for >3 months, in a primary-headache patient overusing acute medication — a secondary headache that maintains chronification.
  • Thresholds: ≥10 days/mo for triptans, ergots, opioids, combination analgesics, or any combination of these; ≥15 days/mo for simple analgesics (paracetamol/NSAIDs).
  • Three pillars: education, withdrawal of the overused drug, and start an effective preventive. Anti-CGRP mAbs and onabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT) work without mandatory prior detox.
  • Screen for and treat comorbid sleep disorders — chronic insomnia (~60%) and restless legs (~37%) are far more common in MOH and improve as the headache is treated.

Management pathway

  1. Educate — explain that the acute drug is now sustaining the headache; set expectations for a transient withdrawal worsening.
  2. Withdraw the overused agent — abrupt for triptans/NSAIDs/ergots; tapered for opioids and butalbital (withdrawal can be dangerous). A short bridge (e.g., NSAID course, steroids, or GON block) eases the transition.
  3. Start a preventive — topiramate, amitriptyline, an anti-CGRP mAb, or onabotulinumtoxinA for chronic-migraine-plus-MOH; both mAbs and botox reduce headache days and help patients transition out of overuse without obligatory inpatient detox.
  4. Treat comorbidities — screen for insomnia and restless legs; managing sleep improves headache outcomes.
  5. Follow up & relapse-prevent — set acute-drug day limits and re-check at 2–3 months.
Butalbital and opioids are the highest-risk agents for MOH and chronification — avoid them for migraine, and taper (do not stop abruptly) when overused because of withdrawal risk.
MOH hides other treatable problems. The sleep-disorder data show chronic insomnia and restless legs are markedly more common in MOH and reversible with headache treatment — a reminder that "refractory chronic headache" often improves once the overused drug is removed, a real preventive is started, and sleep is addressed together.

9 Other primary headaches

▎Key points
  • These are diagnoses of exclusion — several (cough, exercise, sexual, thunderclap) mandate imaging to rule out a structural or vascular secondary cause first.
  • A striking clue: many are indomethacin-responsive (primary cough headache, and the TACs hemicrania continua / paroxysmal hemicrania) — a positive response supports the diagnosis.
  • Hypnic headache ("alarm-clock headache") is the one that wakes older patients from sleep and responds to bedtime caffeine/lithium.
HeadacheHallmarkMust exclude / treat
Primary cough headacheBilateral, seconds–minutes, triggered by cough/Valsalva; older adultsImage to exclude Chiari I / posterior-fossa lesion; usually indomethacin-responsive
Primary exercise headachePulsating, brought on by sustained exertionExclude SAH/dissection/cardiac cause; indomethacin or beta-blocker prophylaxis
Primary headache assoc. with sexual activityBilateral dull building with arousal, or explosive at orgasmExplosive/orgasmic type → exclude SAH & RCVS; often self-limited, indomethacin/beta-blocker
Primary thunderclap headacheAbrupt severe, peak <1 min, recurs; normal workupDiagnosis only after excluding SAH, RCVS, CVST, dissection, apoplexy
Hypnic headacheWakes patient from sleep at a regular time; age >50Exclude nocturnal hypertension, apnea, mass; bedtime caffeine, lithium, indomethacin
Primary stabbing headache ("ice-pick")Single/repetitive jabs, seconds, no autonomic featuresReassure; indomethacin if frequent
Nummular headacheSmall, coin-shaped fixed area of scalp painExclude local scalp/skull lesion; often refractory, trial gabapentin
Cold-stimulus headacheBrief pain from cold food/exposure ("brain freeze")Benign; avoid trigger
New daily persistent headache (NDPH)Daily unremitting from a clearly remembered dayExclude low/high ICP, CVST, infection; often treatment-refractory
The indomethacin test. An absolute response to indomethacin is diagnostic for paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua, and typical for primary cough/exercise/sexual/stabbing headache — a cheap, high-yield clue. Co-prescribe gastroprotection.

10 Secondary headaches

▎Key points
  • A SNOOP flag (§1) points here. The vascular secondary causes cluster around thunderclap (SAH, RCVS, CVST, dissection, apoplexy).
  • Giant cell arteritis is the vision-threatening emergency of the >50 group — treat on suspicion, don't wait for biopsy.
  • Intracranial pressure problems are positional: high ICP (IIH) worse lying down / Valsalva; low CSF pressure (SIH) worse upright.
  • Cervicogenic headache is a common, treatable secondary cause — side-locked pain from the upper cervical spine, confirmed by diagnostic block.

Don't miss: thunderclap = image and tap; new headache >50 with systemic/visual features = GCA until proven otherwise; a strictly orthostatic headache = spontaneous intracranial hypotension, which is treatable and frequently over-labeled as chronic migraine.
Cervicogenic headache vs its mimics. The defining feature is that the pain is side-locked and reproducibly provoked by neck movement or sustained posture, with restricted cervical mobility — not just "headache plus neck ache" (which migraine and TTH also cause). Because the clinical picture overlaps occipital neuralgia, migraine and TTH, a positive diagnostic anaesthetic block of the suspected cervical source (facet/medial branch or C2–3) is the most reliable confirmation before proceeding to radiofrequency ablation.

11 Painful cranial neuropathies & neuralgias

▎Key points
  • Trigeminal neuralgia: paroxysmal, electric-shock, trigger-evoked facial pain; carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine first-line; MRI to separate classical (neurovascular contact) from secondary (e.g., MS, tumor).
  • Microvascular decompression is the most durable surgery when drugs fail and vascular compression is present (also first-line surgery for glossopharyngeal neuralgia).
  • Occipital neuralgia and Tolosa-Hunt round out the high-yield differential; diagnostic nerve blocks help confirm occipital neuralgia.
NeuralgiaFeaturesManagement
Trigeminal neuralgia (TN)Unilateral V2/V3 electric-shock jabs, cutaneous triggers; classical vs idiopathic vs secondary (EAN)Carbamazepine / oxcarbazepine first-line; lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin add-on; acute crisis IV fosphenytoin/lidocaine; refractory → microvascular decompression, gamma-knife, or rhizotomy
Glossopharyngeal neuralgiaParoxysmal throat/ear/tongue-base pain triggered by swallowing; rare syncopeCarbamazepine/oxcarbazepine; refractory → MVD (preferred surgery), gamma-knife, radiofrequency
Occipital neuralgiaShooting pain in greater/lesser occipital distribution; female predominance, links to migraine/neck traumaDiagnostic/therapeutic occipital nerve blocks; neuromodulators; consider secondary causes
Tolosa-Hunt syndromePainful ophthalmoplegia from granulomatous cavernous-sinus/orbital inflammationMRI; corticosteroid-responsive (rapid relief supports diagnosis) after excluding mimics
Post-herpetic neuralgia (V1)Burning pain after herpes zoster ophthalmicusGabapentinoids, TCAs, topical lidocaine; recombinant zoster vaccine prevents zoster/PHN
MRI is not optional in TN. The EAN guideline recommends MRI to exclude secondary trigeminal neuralgia; if MRI is impossible, trigeminal reflex testing can help. A demonstrated neurovascular contact supports but does not by itself confirm classical TN.

12 Landmark trials — who was included & excluded

▎Key points
  • The eligibility criteria are the indication — they tell you which patient in front of you the result applies to.
  • Acute gepant trials enrolled episodic migraine and excluded chronic migraine/overuse; prevention trials are split by episodic vs chronic and by prior-failure count.

Ubrogepant — ACHIEVE (acute)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Adults 18–75 y, migraine onset <50 y
  • History of migraine typically lasting 4–72 h; treat a single qualifying attack of moderate–severe pain
  • Randomized 1:1:1 to placebo / 25 mg / 50 mg; stratified by prior triptan response & concomitant prevention
  • Difficulty distinguishing migraine from tension-type/other headache
  • Current chronic migraine (ICHD-3); clinically significant cardiovascular disease
  • Primary endpoints: 2-h pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome symptom

Fremanezumab — episodic migraine (JAMA 2018)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Adults 18–70 y with episodic migraine: 6–14 headache days (≥4 migraine days) in a 28-day baseline
  • Randomized 1:1:1 — monthly vs single higher quarterly dose vs placebo
  • Chronic migraine; other significant headache disorders
  • Primary endpoint: mean change in monthly migraine days over 12 weeks

Fremanezumab — chronic migraine (NEJM)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Adults with chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/mo; baseline ~13 headache days)
  • Quarterly vs monthly fremanezumab vs placebo, SC
  • Primary endpoint: mean change in headache days (≥4 h, ≥moderate) per month
  • Result: ~38–41% ≥50% responders vs 18% placebo

FOCUS (fremanezumab, difficult-to-treat)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Adults 18–70 y, episodic or chronic migraine
  • Documented failure of 2–4 classes of preventives in the past 10 years
  • Failure of >4 classes; contraindication to study drug
  • Phase 3b, double-blind, placebo-controlled; primary endpoint change in monthly average migraine days

LIBERTY (erenumab, prior-failure) & galcanezumab (episodic)

TrialIncludedResult
LIBERTY (erenumab)Adults 18–65 y, episodic migraine, 2–4 prior preventive failures; stratified 4–7 vs 8–14 migraine days/moHigher ≥50% response at weeks 9–12
Galcanezumab (EM)Adults with episodic migraine; 120 or 240 mg vs placebo, 6 months~4.2–4.3 fewer monthly migraine days

OnabotulinumtoxinA — PREEMPT (chronic migraine)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Adults with chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/mo, ≥4 h)
  • 155 units / 31 fixed sites every 12 weeks (PREEMPT paradigm)
  • Episodic migraine (did not benefit) — indication is chronic only
  • Reduces headache days vs placebo
Reading these on rounds: a gepant trial's result doesn't automatically extend to chronic migraine or MOH (they were excluded). Botox is a chronic-migraine therapy — don't offer it for episodic migraine. Match the mAb evidence to the patient's episodic-vs-chronic status and prior-failure count.
