1. Framework — what is different about the pregnant nervous system

Three forces shape neurology in pregnancy: haemodynamic and hormonal change (greater venous capacitance, pro-thrombotic state, oedema, hypertension); fetal and maternal drug exposure (teratogenicity vs uncontrolled disease — both matter); and the postpartum window, when stroke risk, RCVS, postpartum angiopathy, CVST, and MS relapse all peak. The general physician's job is to know the emergencies that present only in pregnancy or peripartum, to manage chronic neurological disease across pre-conception, pregnancy, delivery, and breastfeeding, and to balance teratogenic risk against the risk of uncontrolled disease.

The single principle: uncontrolled maternal seizure, stroke, or eclampsia is more dangerous to mother and fetus than any antiseizure medication or antihypertensive. Treat the mother — usually the fetus benefits. Drugs matter, but withholding effective therapy from fear of teratogenicity routinely causes worse outcomes than careful selection.

2. Physiological changes that matter for neurology

  • Plasma volume ↑ ~50%, RBC mass ↑ ~30% → dilutional anaemia; serum drug concentrations fall (free fraction may not).
  • Hypercoagulable state from oestrogen — VTE, CVST, stroke, RCVS, PRES, antiphospholipid flare.
  • Renal clearance ↑ ~50% — clearance of many AEDs (lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine) rises markedly, often necessitating dose increases.
  • Hepatic metabolism shifts — CYP3A4 and UGT activity changes affect lamotrigine especially.
  • Pituitary doubles in size from lactotroph hyperplasia — vulnerable to apoplexy with haemorrhage / postpartum hypotension (Sheehan).
  • BP falls in the first half then rises near term — eclampsia / PRES, RCVS, ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke risk peak peripartum and postpartum.
  • Pelvic and lower-limb compression from gravid uterus + fluid shifts → carpal tunnel, meralgia paresthetica, lumbosacral plexopathy after instrumental delivery.
  • Immune modulation — Th2-skewed; many autoimmune diseases (MS) improve in pregnancy but rebound postpartum.
  • Hormonal effects on epilepsy: some women have catamenial-like pregnancy flares; many have stable seizures with optimisation; nausea and vomiting can compromise drug absorption.

3. Pregnancy-specific emergencies

Pre-eclampsia, eclampsia and PRES

  • Pre-eclampsia: new hypertension > 20 weeks + proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction. Severe features: SBP ≥ 160, DBP ≥ 110, thrombocytopenia, deranged LFTs, renal injury, pulmonary oedema, new neurological symptoms.
  • Eclampsia: pre-eclampsia + tonic-clonic seizure. Most occur antepartum or within 48 hours postpartum; can occur up to 6 weeks postpartum.
  • PRES (cross-ref Module 13): headache + cortical visual disturbance (cortical blindness) + seizures + encephalopathy with bilateral posterior parieto-occipital T2/FLAIR cortical/subcortical hyperintensity. Triggered by hypertensive crisis, eclampsia, calcineurin inhibitors, sepsis.
Eclampsia management — emergency:
  • Magnesium sulfate — loading 4–6 g IV over 15–20 min, then 1–2 g/hour infusion for 24 hours after seizure or delivery. Watch for toxicity (loss of patellar reflex → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest); antidote calcium gluconate 1 g IV.
  • Antihypertensive: labetalol (10–20 mg IV q10 min, max 220 mg), hydralazine (5–10 mg IV q20–30 min), nifedipine immediate release 10 mg po q20–30 min — target SBP 140–155, DBP 90–105 (avoid abrupt large drops which can impair placental perfusion).
  • Definitive treatment is delivery — coordinate obstetrics, anaesthesia, neonatology, ICU.
  • If recurrent or refractory seizures: additional benzodiazepine (lorazepam, midazolam), then conventional second-line ASMs (levetiracetam, phenytoin, valproate cautiously) per status epilepticus algorithm.
  • Postpartum: continue magnesium 24 h; monitor for further seizures up to 6 weeks postpartum.

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST)

  • Peripartum and especially postpartum (up to 12 weeks) risk substantially elevated; oestrogen-driven hypercoagulability.
  • Headache (the most common feature — present in > 90%), focal deficits, seizures, encephalopathy, raised ICP, papilloedema.
  • Diagnose with MRI brain + MR venography (CT venogram if MRI not feasible).
  • Treatment: therapeutic LMWH antepartum and postpartum (LMWH does not cross placenta); transition to warfarin postpartum if not breastfeeding (warfarin is compatible with breastfeeding); DOACs not yet established in pregnancy. Anticoagulation continues 3–12 months depending on cause.
  • Cross-ref Module 2 (CVST).

RCVS — reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome

  • Recurrent thunderclap headache + segmental vasoconstriction on cerebral angiography that resolves in 1–3 months.
  • Postpartum is a classic precipitant ("postpartum angiopathy" Call-Fleming syndrome). Other triggers: vasoactive drugs (cannabis, sympathomimetics, SSRIs/SNRIs, triptans, ergots), exertion / sexual activity.
  • Complications: cortical SAH, ischaemic stroke, ICH, PRES overlap.
  • Treatment: stop precipitants; nimodipine 30–60 mg po q4h often used (evidence weak); analgesia; BP control; supportive.

Pituitary apoplexy and Sheehan syndrome

  • Pituitary apoplexy (Module 12 / 19) — peripartum risk in patients with macroadenoma; thunderclap headache + ophthalmoplegia + bitemporal hemianopia + acute panhypopituitarism. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat + urgent MRI + neurosurgery for visual / oculomotor compromise.
  • Sheehan syndrome — postpartum pituitary necrosis from haemorrhagic hypotension; presents with failure to lactate (prolactin deficiency), amenorrhoea, fatigue, cold intolerance, panhypopituitarism. Lifelong hormone replacement.

4. Headache in pregnancy

Primary headaches

  • Migraine without aura often improves in pregnancy (60–70%), especially second and third trimesters; may worsen postpartum.
  • Migraine with aura may persist or worsen; new aura can appear in pregnancy.
  • Tension-type often unchanged.
  • Acute: paracetamol first-line; avoid NSAIDs in first trimester (cardiac defects) and especially after 30 weeks (premature ductal closure, oligohydramnios). Triptans — sumatriptan has the most safety data; modest risk consensus is favourable; weigh risk-benefit.
  • Preventive: avoid valproate (teratogenic) and topiramate (cleft lip/palate); avoid ergots and gepants pending data; propranolol, amitriptyline (low dose), and lifestyle changes are commonly used.
  • Cross-ref Module 4.

5. Epilepsy in pregnancy

Pre-conception counselling

  • ~95% of women with epilepsy have an uncomplicated pregnancy with optimised care.
  • Plan in advance — fertility / pregnancy intentions should be discussed at every visit with women of childbearing age.
  • Switch before conception if on a high-risk drug.
  • Folic acid — 0.4 mg/day for low risk; 4–5 mg/day for women on antiseizure medication or with prior NTD (start ≥ 3 months pre-conception, continue through first trimester at minimum; many guidelines continue throughout pregnancy).

Practical algorithm for the woman with epilepsy who is planning pregnancy or already pregnant:
  1. If on valproate: switch before conception to lamotrigine or levetiracetam (in consultation with neurology); if seizure-free on valproate and no safer alternative tolerated, consider lowest effective dose < 700 mg/day with extensive counselling.
  2. If on topiramate or phenobarbital: same — switch where feasible.
  3. If on lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine: continue, monitor levels frequently (every 4–8 weeks), increase dose to maintain therapeutic concentration. Lamotrigine often needs a 2–3-fold dose increase by third trimester.
  4. Folic acid 4–5 mg/day from pre-conception through (at least) the first trimester.
  5. Vitamin K 10 mg/day to mother in last month if on enzyme-inducing AEDs (CBZ, phenytoin, phenobarbital); IM vitamin K to newborn (routine in most places).
  6. Sleep, alcohol avoidance, illness management; remember ASM clearance changes.
  7. Delivery: continue ASMs, ensure pain control, avoid sleep deprivation.
  8. Postpartum: ASM levels rise back over 1–4 weeks → consider dose reduction; close clinical follow-up.
  9. Breastfeeding: most ASMs compatible with caution (see breastfeeding section).
  10. Register the pregnancy with EURAP, NAAED, or local AED-in-pregnancy registry.

6. Multiple sclerosis & related demyelinating disease

  • Most patients with MS have reduced relapse rates during pregnancy, especially 2nd and 3rd trimesters, but a postpartum rebound (~3 months postpartum) is common.
  • Pregnancy does not change long-term disability trajectory.
  • NMOSD (Module 8) can flare postpartum and antepartum — careful planning.

Other considerations

  • IV methylprednisolone for relapses is compatible in pregnancy.
  • Avoid live vaccines.
  • Vitamin D optimisation pre-conception.
  • Breastfeeding generally encouraged where possible; recent data suggest exclusive breastfeeding may reduce postpartum relapse rate. Restart DMT after weaning or after a planned window.

7. Myasthenia gravis and neonatal MG

  • MG course in pregnancy is variable — 1/3 improve, 1/3 stable, 1/3 worsen; postpartum exacerbation common.
  • Pyridostigmine compatible.
  • Steroids and azathioprine generally continued; mycophenolate teratogenic (avoid; switch before conception); rituximab limited data.
  • IVIG and plasma exchange for crises are safe in pregnancy.
  • Magnesium sulphate is contraindicated in MG — precipitates myasthenic crisis by impairing acetylcholine release. Eclampsia in a patient with MG requires careful collaboration; alternatives include benzodiazepines for seizure control.
  • Many medications worsen MG — avoid aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, β-blockers if possible, certain antipsychotics, magnesium (Module 9).
  • Delivery: vaginal delivery preferred where possible; second stage may be prolonged (smooth muscle uterus unaffected, but voluntary muscles affected); regional anaesthesia preferred; avoid neuromuscular blockers if general anaesthesia needed.
  • Transient neonatal myasthenia gravis: 10–20% of newborns of mothers with AChR-positive MG — transplacental antibodies; poor feeding, hypotonia, weak cry, respiratory weakness in first days; resolves over weeks; supportive care + pyridostigmine.
  • Cross-ref Module 9.

8. Stroke and intracranial haemorrhage in pregnancy

  • Pregnancy and especially the early postpartum period substantially increase stroke risk (~3–10× baseline).
  • Pre-eclampsia / eclampsia are the largest contributors; also CVST, cardio-embolic from peripartum cardiomyopathy, paradoxical embolus via PFO, dissection, RCVS, antiphospholipid syndrome, sickle cell.
  • Acute management:
    • Imaging: MRI brain ± MRV/MRA preferred (no ionising radiation, no contrast usually needed); CT acceptable when MRI unavailable.
    • IV thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase) — pregnancy is not an absolute contraindication; case-by-case with stroke team; benefits often outweigh risks for disabling stroke in late pregnancy or postpartum.
    • Mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion is increasingly used in pregnancy.
    • Antiplatelets and anticoagulation: aspirin low-dose compatible; LMWH for therapeutic anticoagulation antepartum (does not cross placenta); warfarin teratogenic in first trimester (warfarin embryopathy) and contraindicated near term (fetal intracranial haemorrhage); switch to LMWH 6 weeks before delivery; DOACs not yet established.
  • SAH: thunderclap headache + meningism + altered consciousness — non-contrast CT first 6 h has > 99% sensitivity; LP for xanthochromia after 12 h if CT normal. Coil / clip the aneurysm; mode of delivery individualised; secured aneurysm allows vaginal delivery in most.
  • Cross-ref Module 2.

9. Hyperemesis and Wernicke

Hyperemesis gravidarum + IV dextrose without thiamine = Wernicke encephalopathy (Module 13). Persistent vomiting + IV fluids must include thiamine. Caine criteria for Wernicke: ≥ 2 of nutritional deficiency, oculomotor abnormalities, cerebellar signs, altered mental status. Treat with thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 2–3 days BEFORE glucose, then 250 mg/day, then high-dose oral. Korsakoff in 80% of untreated survivors — largely irreversible. Other settings: bariatric surgery, prolonged TPN without thiamine, anorexia, refeeding (cross-ref Module 13).

10. Peripheral and cranial nerve disorders in pregnancy

  • Bell palsy — incidence triples in the third trimester and postpartum. Manage with prednisolone (within 72 h; cross-ref AAN Bell palsy guidelines); eye care; aciclovir if Ramsay Hunt suspected.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome — extremely common (up to 60% have symptoms); fluid retention. Splinting first-line; corticosteroid injection in severe cases; surgery rarely needed during pregnancy (most resolve postpartum).
  • Meralgia paresthetica — lateral femoral cutaneous nerve compression at inguinal ligament from gravid abdomen; reassurance, weight management, avoiding tight clothing; usually resolves postpartum.
  • Obstetric brachial plexus injury in the mother (rare) — from positioning / instrumental delivery.
  • Postpartum lumbosacral plexopathy — femoral, obturator, or peroneal nerve compression during delivery; mostly recover but can be persistent; physiotherapy.
  • Post-dural-puncture headache — postural headache after epidural / spinal; conservative (hydration, caffeine, analgesia) first; epidural blood patch for severe / refractory.
  • Restless legs syndrome (Module 14) — increased in pregnancy; iron studies and replace if ferritin < 75 ng/mL or TSAT < 20%; consider low-dose clonazepam, opioids in severe cases (gabapentinoids and dopamine agonists usually deferred until postpartum because of limited data).

11. Movement disorders & other considerations

  • Chorea gravidarum: rare; rheumatic fever / SLE / antiphospholipid syndrome / Sydenham reactivation; treat underlying cause; symptomatic with risperidone or haloperidol if disabling.
  • Wilson disease (Module 13): continue chelator (trientine or low-dose penicillamine); never stop abruptly (acute hepatic decompensation); zinc usually continued.
  • Migraine prophylaxis reviewed above; cluster headache poorly studied in pregnancy.
  • Restless legs: see above; check ferritin and TSAT.

12. Safe imaging and medication general principles

  • MRI without gadolinium is preferred in pregnancy; no proven teratogenic effect. Gadolinium crosses the placenta and is excreted into amniotic fluid — avoid if possible; use if clearly necessary for diagnosis and the benefit outweighs the theoretical risk.
  • CT: a single head CT delivers < 0.005 Gy to the fetus — well below any teratogenic threshold (0.1 Gy); do not withhold for headache or stroke workup when needed.
  • Iodinated contrast may affect fetal thyroid; check neonatal TSH after birth.
  • Medication general principles: balance risk of uncontrolled disease vs teratogenicity; use the lowest effective dose of the safest agent; monitor blood concentrations; involve multidisciplinary teams (obstetrics + neurology + clinical pharmacy + maternal-fetal medicine + neonatology).

13. When to refer

  • Same day: any new severe headache, focal deficit, seizure, visual disturbance, confusion in pregnancy / postpartum (consider eclampsia, PRES, CVST, stroke, RCVS, SAH, pituitary apoplexy); status epilepticus; suspected pituitary apoplexy; suspected MG crisis; suspected Wernicke encephalopathy in hyperemesis.
  • Pre-conception: any woman of childbearing age with epilepsy, MS, MG, Wilson, prior CVST / stroke, antiphospholipid syndrome should have planned pre-conception counselling.
  • Early pregnancy: review medications (valproate, topiramate, mycophenolate, teriflunomide, methotrexate, warfarin first trimester, fingolimod / siponimod / ozanimod); folic acid; sleep / nausea management; planned monitoring schedule.
  • Late pregnancy / delivery planning: delivery mode discussion (epilepsy — usually vaginal; MG — vaginal preferred; severe disability — case-by-case); regional vs general anaesthesia (MG, MS); neonatology alerted if neonatal MG risk or maternal AED.
  • Postpartum: ASM dose reduction; MS relapse surveillance; mood (postpartum depression / psychosis); breastfeeding planning; contraception.
  • Always involve: obstetrics + maternal-fetal medicine + neurology + clinical pharmacy + (where relevant) MS clinic, epilepsy clinic, neuromuscular clinic, stroke team, neuroanaesthesia, neonatology.
