Why this module?

Pregnancy reshapes neurology. The pro-thrombotic state drives CVST and stroke; the hypertensive disorders cause eclampsia and PRES; hormonal change can flare migraine and MS; the postpartum window concentrates a disproportionate share of disasters. At the same time, many women with chronic neurological disease — epilepsy, MS, MG, Wilson — need careful pre-conception planning, drug review, and multidisciplinary coordination. The general physician's job is to recognise the emergencies and to plan ahead with the chronic conditions.

After this module you will:

  • Recognise and triage eclampsia, PRES, CVST, RCVS, stroke, SAH, and pituitary apoplexy in pregnancy / postpartum.
  • Initiate magnesium sulphate for eclampsia and know its contraindication in MG.
  • Plan an antiseizure regimen in pregnancy using EURAP teratogenicity data, switching off valproate and topiramate where feasible.
  • Use 4–5 mg folic acid in women on AEDs from pre-conception through (at least) first trimester.
  • Plan MS DMT around pregnancy, recognising postpartum rebound risk.
  • Recognise and treat hyperemesis-induced Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine BEFORE glucose).
  • Manage MG in pregnancy and recognise transient neonatal MG.
  • Counsel about safe imaging (MRI without gadolinium preferred; CT acceptable when needed) and breastfeeding compatibility.
The single principle: uncontrolled maternal seizure, stroke, or eclampsia is more dangerous to mother and fetus than any antiseizure medication or antihypertensive. Treat the mother — the fetus benefits. Withholding effective therapy from fear of teratogenicity often causes worse outcomes than careful selection.

Lessons

1. Physiological changes that matter for neurology
  • Plasma volume ↑ ~50% → dilutional anaemia; serum drug levels fall (free fraction may not).
  • Oestrogen → hypercoagulable: VTE, CVST, stroke, RCVS, PRES, antiphospholipid flare.
  • Renal clearance ↑ ~50% — lamotrigine, LEV, OXC clearance rises markedly → need dose increases.
  • CYP3A4 / UGT shifts — lamotrigine PK changes.
  • Pituitary doubles in size — apoplexy + Sheehan risk.
  • BP falls early, rises late — eclampsia/PRES, RCVS, stroke peak peripartum + postpartum.
  • Pelvic + lower-limb compression — CTS, meralgia paresthetica, lumbosacral plexopathy postpartum.
  • Th2-skewed immunity — MS improves in pregnancy + postpartum rebound.
2. Eclampsia, PRES, magnesium sulfate
Eclampsia emergency:
  • Magnesium sulfate: 4–6 g IV bolus over 15–20 min → 1–2 g/h infusion × 24 h. Watch for toxicity: loss of patellar reflex → resp depression → cardiac arrest. Antidote: calcium gluconate 1 g IV.
  • Antihypertensive: labetalol IV (10–20 mg q10 min, max 220), hydralazine IV (5–10 mg q20–30 min), nifedipine IR po (10 mg q20–30). Target SBP 140–155 / DBP 90–105 — avoid abrupt drops (placental perfusion).
  • Definitive Rx is delivery — coordinate obstetrics + anaesthesia + neonatology + ICU.
  • Refractory seizures: benzodiazepine then conventional second-line ASMs per status epilepticus algorithm.
  • Continue Mg 24 h postpartum; monitor for further seizures up to 6 weeks postpartum.

PRES (Module 13): headache + cortical visual disturbance (cortical blindness) + seizures + encephalopathy + bilateral posterior parieto-occipital T2/FLAIR hyperintensity. Control BP gently (25% reduction in 1–2 h; 160/110 over 24 h); correct triggers; ASMs for seizures.

3. CVST, RCVS, postpartum angiopathy
  • CVST: peripartum / first 12 weeks postpartum especially. Headache > 90%, focal deficits, seizures, raised ICP. MRI + MRV diagnostic. Treat: therapeutic LMWH antepartum + postpartum (does not cross placenta); transition to warfarin postpartum if not BF (warfarin BF-compatible). Cross-ref Module 2.
  • RCVS / postpartum angiopathy (Call-Fleming): recurrent thunderclap headache + segmental vasoconstriction reversing in 1–3 months. Postpartum + vasoactive drugs (cannabis, sympathomimetics, SSRIs/SNRIs, triptans, ergots). Complications: cortical SAH, ischaemic stroke, ICH, PRES overlap. Stop precipitants; nimodipine 30–60 mg q4h often used; analgesia; BP control.
4. Pituitary apoplexy and Sheehan
  • Pituitary apoplexy (Modules 12 / 19) — peripartum risk in macroadenoma; thunderclap + ophthalmoplegia + bitemporal hemianopia + acute panhypopituitarism. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV STAT, urgent MRI, neurosurgery if visual / oculomotor compromise.
  • Sheehan syndrome — postpartum pituitary necrosis from haemorrhagic hypotension. Failure to lactate, amenorrhoea, fatigue, panhypopituitarism. Lifelong hormone replacement.
5. Headache in pregnancy

Migraine without aura often improves (60–70%, especially 2nd–3rd trimester); migraine with aura less so. Postpartum rebound common.

  • Acute: paracetamol first-line; avoid NSAIDs 1st trimester (cardiac defects) and after 30 weeks (ductus closure, oligohydramnios). Triptans — sumatriptan most data; modest risk consensus favourable; case-by-case.
  • Preventive: AVOID valproate (teratogen), topiramate (cleft lip/palate); propranolol + amitriptyline (low dose) + lifestyle commonly used. Module 4.

Secondary headache differential — high suspicion in pregnancy: eclampsia/PRES, CVST, RCVS, SAH, pituitary apoplexy, dissection, stroke, IIH (Modules 4, 19), PDPH.

6. Epilepsy — pre-conception & AED selection

~95% have an uncomplicated pregnancy with optimised care. Plan in advance — discuss intentions at every visit.

Folic acid: 0.4 mg/day low-risk; 4–5 mg/day for women on AEDs or prior NTD, from ≥ 3 months pre-conception through first trimester (many continue throughout).

7. MS and DMT planning

PRIMS (Confavreux NEJM 1998): pregnancy reduces relapse rate, especially 2nd–3rd trimester; postpartum rebound at ~3 months. Long-term disability trajectory unchanged. NMOSD (Module 8) can flare ante- and postpartum.

8. Myasthenia gravis & transient neonatal MG
  • MG course variable: 1/3 improve, 1/3 stable, 1/3 worsen; postpartum exacerbation common.
  • Pyridostigmine compatible. Steroids + azathioprine continued. Mycophenolate teratogenic — switch pre-conception. Rituximab limited data. IVIG + PLEX safe for crises.
  • Magnesium sulphate is CONTRAINDICATED in MG — precipitates myasthenic crisis (impairs ACh release). Use benzodiazepines for eclampsia in MG; close collaboration.
  • Avoid aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, β-blockers if possible, certain antipsychotics — all can worsen MG.
  • Vaginal delivery preferred; second stage may be prolonged; regional anaesthesia preferred; avoid NMBs if GA.
  • Transient neonatal MG: 10–20% of newborns of mothers with AChR-positive MG; transplacental antibodies; poor feeding, hypotonia, weak cry, respiratory weakness in first days; supportive + pyridostigmine; resolves over weeks.
  • Cross-ref Module 9.
9. Stroke and ICH
  • Pregnancy + early postpartum: stroke risk ~3–10× baseline.
  • Major contributors: pre-eclampsia / eclampsia, CVST, peripartum cardiomyopathy, paradoxical embolus via PFO, dissection, RCVS, antiphospholipid, sickle cell.
  • Acute imaging: MRI ± MRV/MRA preferred; CT acceptable when MRI unavailable. Do not withhold CT for needed stroke workup — < 0.005 Gy fetal dose, well below teratogenic threshold.
  • IV thrombolysis (alteplase / tenecteplase) and mechanical thrombectomy increasingly used; case-by-case decisions with stroke team.
  • Anticoagulation: LMWH antepartum (no placental transfer); warfarin teratogenic in 1st trimester and contraindicated near term (fetal ICH); DOACs not established.
  • SAH: thunderclap headache + meningism; non-contrast CT > 99% sensitive within 6 h; LP for xanthochromia after 12 h if CT normal; coil / clip; secured aneurysm allows vaginal delivery in most.
10. Hyperemesis-Wernicke and other metabolic
Hyperemesis + IV dextrose without thiamine = Wernicke encephalopathy (Module 13). Caine criteria (≥ 2 of 4): nutritional deficit, oculomotor abnormalities, cerebellar signs, altered mental status. Thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 2–3 days BEFORE glucose, then 250 mg/day, then high-dose oral. Korsakoff in 80% of untreated survivors — largely irreversible.
11. Peripheral / cranial nerve disorders in pregnancy
  • Bell palsy: incidence triples in 3rd trimester / postpartum. Prednisolone within 72 h; eye care; aciclovir if Ramsay Hunt suspected.
  • Carpal tunnel: very common; splinting first; steroid injection severe; most resolve postpartum.
  • Meralgia paresthetica: lateral femoral cutaneous nerve at inguinal ligament; reassurance, weight management, avoid tight clothing.
  • Postpartum lumbosacral plexopathy: instrumental delivery, prolonged second stage; most recover.
  • Post-dural-puncture headache: postural after epidural / spinal; conservative first (hydration, caffeine, analgesia); epidural blood patch for refractory.
  • Restless legs (Module 14): ferritin < 75 / TSAT < 20% → iron replacement; low-dose clonazepam or opioids in severe cases; defer dopamine agonists / gabapentinoids until postpartum.
12. Imaging, breastfeeding, when to refer
  • MRI without gadolinium preferred. CT acceptable when needed (< 0.005 Gy single head CT — well below 0.1 Gy teratogenic threshold).
  • Breastfeeding generally compatible: lamotrigine, LEV, CBZ, OXC, VPA, phenytoin, LMWH, warfarin, low-dose aspirin, most MS DMTs (recent data favour). Caution: phenobarbital, primidone (infant sedation); avoid methotrexate; DOACs limited data.
  • Always involve: obstetrics + maternal-fetal medicine + neurology + clinical pharmacy + (where relevant) MS / epilepsy / neuromuscular / stroke teams + neuroanaesthesia + neonatology.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A primigravida at 34 weeks presents with severe headache, blurred vision, and a witnessed generalised tonic-clonic seizure. BP 180/115, proteinuria 4+, hyperreflexia, brisk clonus

MRI brain (after stabilisation): bilateral posterior parieto-occipital T2/FLAIR hyperintensity consistent with PRES.

Diagnosis: Eclampsia with PRES.

Plan within minutes to hour:

  1. Magnesium sulfate: 4–6 g IV bolus over 15–20 minutes, then 1–2 g/hour infusion for 24 hours after delivery or last seizure. Watch for toxicity (loss of patellar reflex → resp depression → cardiac arrest); antidote calcium gluconate 1 g IV.
  2. Antihypertensive: labetalol 20 mg IV q10 min (max 220 mg) OR hydralazine 5–10 mg IV q20–30 min OR nifedipine immediate-release 10 mg po q20–30 min. Target SBP 140–155 / DBP 90–105 — avoid abrupt large drops.
  3. Definitive treatment is delivery — urgent obstetrics + neonatology + anaesthesia consultation; mode depends on fetal/maternal status.
  4. If recurrent seizures despite magnesium: benzodiazepine (lorazepam), then conventional second-line ASMs (levetiracetam IV, phenytoin) per status epilepticus algorithm.
  5. ICU monitoring; continue magnesium 24 h after delivery; monitor for further seizures up to 6 weeks postpartum.
  6. Counsel about future pregnancy risk (~15% recurrence pre-eclampsia, 1–2% eclampsia); low-dose aspirin from 12 weeks in next pregnancy.
Case 2 — A 32-year-old woman 3 weeks postpartum (vaginal delivery) presents with progressive holocephalic headache for 5 days + 2 focal seizures + bilateral papilloedema. No hypertension

MRI + MRV: filling defect and absent flow in superior sagittal sinus consistent with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.

Diagnosis: Postpartum cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST).

Plan:

  1. Therapeutic anticoagulation with LMWH (e.g., enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC BD) or unfractionated heparin in acute settings; transition to warfarin once stable (warfarin is compatible with breastfeeding); duration 3–12 months depending on cause and recanalisation.
  2. Antiseizure medication (levetiracetam preferred for breastfeeding; lamotrigine alternative).
  3. Manage raised ICP (head elevation; consider acetazolamide if papilloedema threatening vision; ONSF or CSF shunt rarely required).
  4. Identify provoking factors: pregnancy + postpartum + hypercoagulable workup (factor V Leiden, protein C/S, antithrombin, antiphospholipid).
  5. Counsel about future pregnancy + contraception; oestrogen-containing contraceptives generally avoided.
  6. Cross-ref Module 2.
Case 3 — A 28-year-old with epilepsy (focal seizures), seizure-free on lamotrigine 200 mg BD, wishes to conceive

She has been on lamotrigine for 4 years. Last seizure 2 years ago. Normal physical exam.

Plan — pre-conception counselling:

  1. Confirm seizure freedom and that lamotrigine is the optimal drug; otherwise continue and avoid switches close to conception.
  2. Folic acid 4–5 mg/day from ≥ 3 months pre-conception through (at least) first trimester (many guidelines through pregnancy).
  3. Document baseline lamotrigine level pre-pregnancy.
  4. Plan for frequent lamotrigine level monitoring (every 4–8 weeks) — clearance rises markedly in pregnancy, often requiring 2–3× the pre-pregnancy dose by 3rd trimester to maintain therapeutic concentration and seizure freedom.
  5. Counsel about pregnancy registry enrolment (EURAP / NAAED / local).
  6. Discuss the small (~2.5%) major congenital malformation risk on lamotrigine — emphasise that uncontrolled seizures are more dangerous to mother and fetus than the drug.
  7. Postpartum: reduce dose back to pre-pregnancy dose over 1–4 weeks (clearance returns to baseline); breastfeeding compatible with monitoring of infant for sedation / rash.
Case 4 — A 25-year-old with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy is on valproate 800 mg BD with good seizure control. She has just discovered she is 6 weeks pregnant

This is an unplanned pregnancy. She wants to continue the pregnancy.

Plan:

  1. Do NOT abruptly stop valproate — abrupt cessation risks status epilepticus and breakthrough seizures, which are more dangerous than continuing valproate.
  2. Urgent epilepsy / neurology referral within days.
  3. Discuss the substantial teratogenic risk: ~10% major congenital malformation rate (dose-dependent; especially at > 1000 mg/day) — NTDs, cardiac, facial, urogenital — and 30–40% neurodevelopmental impact (lower IQ).
  4. If a switch is considered: gradual cross-titration to lamotrigine or levetiracetam over several weeks; verify seizure freedom before further taper of valproate; document risk discussion. Sometimes the decision is to remain on valproate at the lowest effective dose (< 700 mg/day) given JME seizure control.
  5. Folic acid 4–5 mg/day immediately.
  6. Fetal medicine referral: detailed anomaly scan at 18–20 weeks; cardiac scan; alpha-fetoprotein.
  7. Multidisciplinary plan (obstetrics + neurology + clinical pharmacy + maternal-fetal medicine + neonatology).
  8. Document everything; pregnancy registry enrolment.
Case 5 — A 33-year-old with relapsing-remitting MS (EDSS 2.0) on natalizumab wishes to conceive. Last natalizumab infusion 8 weeks ago. EBV antibody-positive

Strong relapse history before natalizumab; concern about rebound on stopping.

Plan — pre-conception planning:

  1. Multidisciplinary MS clinic consultation.
  2. Discuss the natalizumab-rebound risk on cessation; some centres continue extended-interval natalizumab dosing through pregnancy (with neonatal observation for transient haematological effects), particularly when prior disease was highly active.
  3. Anti-CD20 (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, rituximab) is an alternative — long half-life means a single course can provide ongoing effect; washout 6 months pre-conception is ideal.
  4. If switching DMT: plan the transition with adequate washout and bridging therapy if needed.
  5. Optimise vitamin D pre-conception; folate.
  6. Avoid live vaccines; ensure routine vaccinations up to date.
  7. Counsel about likely reduced relapse rate during pregnancy (PRIMS Confavreux NEJM 1998) but postpartum rebound risk at ~3 months → plan post-delivery DMT resumption (recent data support breastfeeding; exclusive breastfeeding may reduce postpartum relapse rate).
  8. Cross-ref Module 8.
Case 6 — A 22-year-old at 12 weeks with severe hyperemesis gravidarum has been admitted for IV fluids with dextrose. She develops nystagmus and confusion 2 days later

Exam: horizontal nystagmus, bilateral lateral rectus weakness, ataxia, encephalopathic.

Diagnosis: Wernicke encephalopathy precipitated by IV dextrose without thiamine in hyperemesis (Caine criteria met).

Plan:

  1. Thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 2–3 days BEFORE further glucose; then 250 mg IM/IV daily × 3–5 days; then high-dose oral.
  2. Multivitamins (folate), magnesium replacement, correct electrolytes.
  3. Antiemetic (ondansetron is widely used but has some data concerns; pyridoxine + doxylamine combination is safer first-line for pregnancy nausea).
  4. MRI brain (if clinically possible): mammillary bodies, periaqueductal grey, dorsomedial thalami T2/FLAIR — supports diagnosis but treatment should not wait.
  5. Multidisciplinary: obstetrics + nutrition + neurology + maternal-fetal medicine.
  6. Counsel about Korsakoff risk (80% of untreated survivors) — largely irreversible.
  7. Prevention takeaway: any pregnancy with prolonged vomiting or IV fluids should receive thiamine routinely (Module 13).
