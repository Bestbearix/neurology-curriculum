1. Framework — three mechanistic buckets that drive treatment

Pain medicine works best when you decide first what kind of pain you are looking at. Three mechanistic categories (with a fourth "mixed" common in clinic) drive your treatment plan:

CategoryBedside hallmarksFirst-line treatments
Nociceptive — tissue injury or inflammation, normal nervous systemDull, aching, throbbing; localised; worse on use; responds to NSAIDs/paracetamol; normal sensory examParacetamol, NSAIDs, physical therapy; opioids only short-course for severe acute
Neuropathic — disease or lesion of the somatosensory systemBurning, shooting, electric, allodynia, hyperalgesia, paraesthesiae; sensory deficit in the same distributionGabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin); SNRIs (duloxetine); TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline); topical lidocaine 5% / capsaicin 8%
Nociplastic — altered nociceptive processing without clear tissue or nerve injury (formerly "central sensitisation")Diffuse, multifocal; widespread tender points; fatigue, sleep disturbance, mood, cognitive fog; normal exam apart from tendernessSNRI (duloxetine, milnacipran); TCA (amitriptyline); pregabalin; graded exercise; CBT, mindfulness; avoid opioids
MixedCombination — e.g., painful radiculopathy (nociceptive low back + neuropathic radicular leg), chronic low back pain with central sensitisationCombine approaches; opioid-sparing is the priority
The single principle: the mechanism dictates the drug class. Opioids treat acute severe nociceptive pain reasonably well; they are poorly effective and harmful in neuropathic and nociplastic pain. Reaching for opioids first in chronic pain is one of the most common and damaging errors in pain medicine.

2. Pain pathways and the modern definition

IASP 2020 revised definition of pain

"An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." Six accompanying notes emphasise that pain is always a personal experience influenced by biological, psychological and social factors; that pain and nociception are different phenomena; that verbal description is only one of several behaviours to express pain; and that a person's report of pain should be respected.

Pathway in one paragraph

Tissue stimulus → activates nociceptors (free nerve endings; small-myelinated Aδ for first/sharp pain, unmyelinated C fibres for second/dull burning pain) → dorsal root ganglion → enters spinal cord at lamina I, II (substantia gelatinosa), V → crosses within 1–2 segments → ascends in spinothalamic tract to VPL of thalamus → primary somatosensory cortex (S1), insula, anterior cingulate cortex. The same input simultaneously engages limbic and prefrontal areas — pain has an affective and cognitive dimension always.

Key modulating systems

  • Descending inhibition: periaqueductal grey (PAG) → rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) → spinal dorsal horn; mediated by NE (locus coeruleus → α2 receptors) and serotonin (raphe); released by endogenous opioids. SNRIs and TCAs work largely by enhancing this descending NE/5HT pathway.
  • Spinal gate control (Melzack & Wall): large-fibre (Aβ — touch) input inhibits transmission of C/Aδ nociceptive signals at lamina II — the basis for TENS, massage, "rubbing it better".
  • Central sensitisation: NMDA-mediated wind-up + reduced descending inhibition → amplification at the dorsal horn. Drives allodynia and chronic pain. Gabapentinoids reduce presynaptic calcium influx via α2δ; ketamine blocks NMDA; clonidine enhances descending α2 inhibition.
  • Peripheral sensitisation: inflammatory soup (prostaglandins — COX-1/2, bradykinin, histamine, substance P, CGRP) lowers nociceptor thresholds. NSAIDs work here.

3. Assessment

Structured history

Use a mnemonic such as OPQRST + impact:

  • Onset; Provoking / palliating; Quality (burning, electric → neuropathic; aching → nociceptive; widespread + fatigue → nociplastic); Region / radiation; Severity (NRS 0–10); Timing & duration.
  • Impact on sleep, mood, function, work, relationships.
  • Red flags (cauda equina, infection, malignancy, fracture, vascular catastrophe); psychosocial yellow flags (catastrophising, fear avoidance, low mood, work conflict).
  • Prior treatments (which drugs, doses, duration, effect, side effects).
  • Substance use history; opioid use; mental health history.

Examination

  • Inspection (swelling, atrophy, posture, splinting, skin/temperature/colour changes — CRPS).
  • Range of motion; tone; reflexes; strength.
  • Sensory mapping in suspected neuropathic pain — touch (Aβ), pinprick (Aδ), cold (Aδ), warmth (C), vibration (Aβ — large fibre); look for allodynia (light touch → pain) and hyperalgesia (more pain than expected to noxious stimulus).
  • Provocative tests (straight leg raise, Spurling, Tinel/Phalen, Finkelstein).
  • Generalised tender points / control points (fibromyalgia).

Validated tools

4. Nociceptive pain

  • Tissue injury or inflammation with intact nervous system. Acute postoperative, fracture, arthritis, biliary colic, MI, pancreatitis — heterogeneous and usually self-limiting with treatment of cause.
  • WHO analgesic ladder (originally cancer pain, widely applied):
    1. Step 1: paracetamol ± NSAID ± adjuvant.
    2. Step 2: weak opioid (codeine, tramadol — caveats: tramadol has SNRI activity and lowers seizure threshold; codeine highly variable metabolism — CYP2D6).
    3. Step 3: strong opioid (morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl).
    4. Adjuvants at any step: gabapentinoids, antidepressants, steroids (bony / nerve compression), bisphosphonates, anti-spasmodics, topical agents, regional nerve blocks.
  • NSAIDs cautions: GI bleed (add PPI in at-risk), renal injury (especially in elderly, dehydrated, HF, CKD), cardiovascular risk (highest with diclofenac and high-dose ibuprofen; naproxen lowest), hypertension, fluid retention. Avoid in advanced CKD and severe HF.
  • Acute postoperative pain — multimodal opioid-sparing (paracetamol + NSAID + regional block / wound infiltration + gabapentinoid limited evidence + low-dose ketamine in selected) reduces opioid requirement and persistent post-surgical pain.

Practical opioid principle in neuropathic pain: even when opioids reduce pain scores, function and quality-of-life gains are usually modest and harms accumulate over time. Combine first/second-line non-opioids with rehabilitation. Trials of opioids in chronic non-cancer pain should be time-limited and goal-directed (function, not pain score alone).

6. Painful peripheral neuropathies

  • Painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN): most common cause of painful peripheral neuropathy. Symmetric distal burning + electric pain + allodynia, worse at night. Tight glycaemic control (more effective in T1DM than T2DM for prevention); modifiable cardiovascular risk factors. First-line: duloxetine, pregabalin, gabapentin, amitriptyline. Topical capsaicin 8% in selected.
  • Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN): platinums (cisplatin — sensory ataxia + lhermitte; oxaliplatin — cold-induced acute neurotoxicity + chronic sensory neuropathy), taxanes (paclitaxel), bortezomib, vincristine, thalidomide / pomalidomide. Duloxetine is the only agent with positive RCT evidence (Smith JAMA 2013). Cryotherapy / compression gloves during infusion may reduce incidence (limited evidence).
  • Small-fibre neuropathy (SFN): burning feet, allodynia, autonomic features (dry eyes/mouth, OH, GI dysmotility, sudomotor changes — cross-ref Module 15). Conventional NCS normal. Diagnosis: skin biopsy with reduced IENFD at the distal calf (gold standard); QST. Causes: diabetes / pre-diabetes (impaired glucose tolerance is a major contributor — OGTT essential), Sjögren, sarcoidosis, vitamin B12 deficiency (Module 13), HIV, paraneoplastic, amyloid (hereditary ATTR with patisiran/vutrisiran/tafamidis — cross-ref Module 15), Fabry disease (X-linked α-galactosidase A; agalsidase enzyme replacement, migalastat in selected GLA mutations), SCN9A/10A/11A channelopathies (erythromelalgia, paroxysmal extreme pain).
  • HIV neuropathy: distal symmetric painful sensory neuropathy from HIV itself ± antiretrovirals (stavudine, didanosine — now rare).
  • Vasculitic neuropathy (multiplex, stepwise asymmetric): immunotherapy + treat underlying vasculitis.

7. Painful radiculopathy and chronic low back pain

  • Acute radiculopathy: dermatomal pain ± weakness / numbness / reflex loss; MRI for surgical planning, not for benign presentations within first 6 weeks.
  • Most resolve with conservative management (analgesia, paracetamol/NSAID, early mobilisation, physiotherapy, neuropathic pain agents if sustained radicular pain).
  • Epidural steroid injection offers short-term pain relief in selected radiculopathy but limited long-term benefit; specialist procedure with rare but serious risks (paralysis, infection, dural puncture).
  • Surgery: progressive neurological deficit, cauda equina syndrome (emergency — Module 10), or persistent disabling radicular pain despite ≥ 6 weeks of conservative management with clear MRI correlation.

Chronic low back pain

  • Globally the leading cause of years lived with disability. Mostly non-specific (~85%); identify the small number with serious cause via red flags (fever, weight loss, IV drug use, history of cancer, age > 50 or < 18, night pain, neurology, recent trauma).
  • First-line: stay active, avoid bed rest, education, physiotherapy / exercise, CBT or mindfulness, paracetamol or NSAID short course.
  • Do not image without red flags within 6 weeks.
  • Many patients have a mixed mechanistic phenotype (nociceptive + radicular neuropathic + nociplastic / central sensitisation). Identify which dominate and combine treatments.
  • Spinal cord stimulation, intrathecal drug delivery, radiofrequency facet denervation, regenerative therapies — specialist procedures with mixed evidence.

8. Herpes zoster and postherpetic neuralgia

  • Zoster (shingles): reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglia; dermatomal vesicular rash, severe neuropathic pain that often precedes the rash.
  • Treatment of acute zoster: aciclovir 800 mg po 5×/day or valaciclovir 1 g po TID × 7 days, started within 72 hours of rash; reduces severity and duration. Pain control with paracetamol, NSAID, opioid, and consider early gabapentinoid / amitriptyline. Steroids reduce acute pain but do NOT prevent PHN.
  • Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN): pain persisting > 90 days after rash onset. Risk factors: age (rare < 50; ~30% > 80), severe prodromal pain, severe rash, ophthalmic zoster, immunosuppression.
  • Treatment of PHN: gabapentin, pregabalin, TCAs, topical lidocaine 5% patch, capsaicin 8% patch; opioids only for refractory cases.
  • Recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix): 2-dose subunit vaccine; > 90% efficacy against zoster and PHN in adults ≥ 50; preferred over live vaccine (Zostavax now withdrawn in many countries). Recommend in immunocompromised (FDA expanded to ≥ 19 immunocompromised).
  • Ramsay Hunt syndrome: VZV reactivation in geniculate ganglion → facial palsy + auricular vesicles + hearing/balance symptoms; treat with valaciclovir + steroid; cross-ref Module 1 facial nerve.

9. Trigeminal neuralgia and other facial pain

  • Trigeminal neuralgia (TN): paroxysms of unilateral electric-shock or stabbing facial pain in V2 and V3 (V1 less common); triggered by light touch, brushing teeth, chewing, cold air; usually older women.
  • Burchiel classification: TN type 1 (classical — episodic, no constant pain between attacks; ICHD-3 "trigeminal neuralgia, purely paroxysmal"); TN type 2 (with concomitant continuous pain); other subtypes (post-traumatic, secondary to MS, secondary to space-occupying lesion).
  • Cause: vascular loop (superior cerebellar artery) compressing the trigeminal root entry zone in most classical TN. MRI brain with high-resolution sequences (FIESTA / CISS) to identify the vascular loop and exclude secondary causes (MS plaque — Module 8; tumour — Module 12; aneurysm). Suspect MS in young patients, bilateral TN, atypical features.
  • First-line treatment: carbamazepine 100 mg BD titrated to 600–1200 mg/day — dramatic relief in 70–90% but eventually loses efficacy in many. Oxcarbazepine better tolerated alternative. Watch for hyponatraemia (especially OXC), Stevens-Johnson (HLA-B*1502 in Han Chinese, Thai, Filipino — pre-test before CBZ); cytopenias.
  • Second-line drugs: lamotrigine, baclofen, gabapentin, pregabalin.
  • Surgical options when drugs fail or cause intolerable side effects:
    • Microvascular decompression (MVD, Jannetta) — posterior fossa craniotomy with Teflon pad between vessel and nerve root; ~90% long-term pain relief in classical TN; small but real perioperative risks; reasonable choice in fit younger patients.
    • Gamma Knife stereotactic radiosurgery — outpatient; delayed onset (weeks to months); ~70–80% pain relief; preferred in older or frail patients.
    • Percutaneous procedures at Gasserian ganglion — radiofrequency thermocoagulation, glycerol rhizolysis, balloon compression; higher recurrence; risk of facial numbness.

Other facial pain syndromes

  • Glossopharyngeal neuralgia: paroxysmal throat/ear pain triggered by swallowing, talking, yawning; rare; treat similarly to TN (carbamazepine) and consider MVD.
  • Persistent idiopathic facial pain (PIFP) (formerly atypical facial pain): continuous deep ache, often in V2 distribution, no clear trigger or structural cause; treat with antidepressant (TCA or duloxetine) + CBT.
  • Cluster headache, paroxysmal hemicrania, hemicrania continua, SUNCT/SUNA: cross-ref Module 4.
  • Temporomandibular disorders (TMD): jaw pain, clicking, restricted opening; mostly muscular; conservative care.
  • Burning mouth syndrome: idiopathic chronic oral burning; check for B12/folate/iron deficiency, candida, diabetes; clonazepam swish-and-spit, low-dose TCA, gabapentinoids.

10. Complex regional pain syndrome

  • Disproportionate pain after a minor injury or surgery, with vasomotor, sudomotor, oedema, motor, and trophic changes in a region.
  • CRPS type 1 (no defined nerve lesion — formerly RSD) vs type 2 (defined nerve injury — formerly causalgia).

Budapest diagnostic criteria (Harden 2010)

  1. Continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event.
  2. At least one symptom in three of four categories (subjective report): sensory (hyperalgesia, allodynia); vasomotor (temperature or colour asymmetry); sudomotor/oedema (sweating asymmetry, oedema); motor/trophic (decreased ROM, motor dysfunction — weakness, tremor, dystonia; trophic changes in hair, nails, skin).
  3. At least one sign at the time of evaluation in two of four categories (objective): the same four domains.
  4. No other diagnosis better explains the picture.

Management

  • Early movement and rehabilitation is the cornerstone — physiotherapy + occupational therapy + graded motor imagery + mirror box therapy.
  • Multimodal pharmacotherapy: gabapentinoids, TCAs, SNRIs; bisphosphonates (alendronate, neridronate — most evidence in early CRPS); intranasal calcitonin (mixed evidence); topical capsaicin 8%; ketamine infusion in severe refractory; intrathecal baclofen for dystonia.
  • Sympathetic nerve blocks may help — limited long-term effect.
  • Psychological — CBT, fear-avoidance work, education.
  • Vitamin C 500 mg/day for 50 days after wrist fracture reduces CRPS incidence (Lancet 1999, replicated; 2017 Cochrane equivocal — still widely used as low-risk).
  • Spinal cord stimulation in refractory cases.
The single principle in CRPS: the disorder is partly maintained by disuse and "learned non-use" cortical reorganisation. Restoring movement — even small — is the most important intervention. Pain medications enable rehabilitation; they do not cure CRPS on their own.

11. Central pain syndromes

  • Central post-stroke pain (CPSP, Dejerine-Roussy): pain (often burning, sometimes constricting) in the body region affected by a thalamic or lateral medullary stroke; allodynia, dysaesthesia. Onset variable (immediate to months post-stroke). First-line: amitriptyline, gabapentin/pregabalin, lamotrigine; second-line: duloxetine; rTMS/motor cortex stimulation in specialist centres. Cross-ref Module 2.
  • MS pain: trigeminal neuralgia from pontine plaque (often bilateral, atypical — Module 8), painful tonic spasms, Lhermitte phenomenon (electric shock down the spine on neck flexion — also seen with cervical cord compression, B12 deficiency, radiation myelopathy), central neuropathic burning. Treatment as for neuropathic pain ± management of demyelinating disease.
  • Spinal cord injury pain: at-level (segmental neuropathic) + below-level (central deafferentation pain) + nociceptive (musculoskeletal from wheelchair use, etc.) — Module 10. Gabapentinoids, TCAs, intrathecal baclofen for spasticity.
  • Syringomyelia: dissociated sensory loss with burning pain — Module 10.

12. Nociplastic pain — fibromyalgia and overlap syndromes

  • Fibromyalgia: chronic widespread pain + fatigue + sleep disturbance + cognitive symptoms ("fibro fog") + multiple somatic symptoms. Female predominance ~2–3:1. Prevalence 2–4%.
  • ACR 2016/2019 criteria: Widespread Pain Index (count of pain regions) and Symptom Severity Scale (fatigue, sleep, cognitive symptoms, somatic burden); generalised pain (≥ 4 of 5 regions, with axial); duration ≥ 3 months. Replaces 1990 tender-point criteria.
  • Pathophysiology: altered central pain processing ("nociplastic") — reduced descending inhibition, increased temporal summation, functional MRI shows amplified responses. Not a peripheral inflammatory or autoimmune disease.
  • Investigation focused: rule out inflammatory arthritis (RF, CCP, ESR/CRP), hypothyroidism, polymyalgia rheumatica, hypovitaminosis D, sleep apnea, depression. Investigations are minimal — do not pursue elaborate workup unless red flags.

Treatment

  1. Education and self-management cornerstones: validate the diagnosis; explain that the pain is real but the mechanism is central sensitisation, not tissue damage; encourage activity, sleep hygiene, pacing.
  2. Graded aerobic exercise — strongest evidence base; start very low and progress slowly; aquatic exercise often well tolerated.
  3. Cognitive behavioural therapy and mindfulness.
  4. Pharmacotherapy: duloxetine (FDA-approved), milnacipran (FDA-approved), pregabalin (FDA-approved), amitriptyline (off-label, evidence base); tramadol with caution.
  5. Avoid opioids (limited efficacy, harm potential); avoid benzodiazepines.
  6. Address comorbid mood / sleep disorders.

Overlap nociplastic syndromes

Frequently coexist with fibromyalgia and share treatment principles: chronic fatigue syndrome / ME, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis, temporomandibular disorders, chronic pelvic pain, tension-type headache (Module 4), chronic low back pain with central sensitisation, post-acute COVID-19 syndromes.

13. Cancer pain and palliative principles

  • Use the WHO analgesic ladder as a starting framework; modify with disease-specific adjuvants.
  • Bone pain: NSAIDs, paracetamol, opioids, bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid, denosumab), single-fraction palliative radiotherapy, radionuclides (strontium-89, samarium-153); manage osteoporotic fracture risk.
  • Neuropathic cancer pain (tumour invasion of plexus, radiation plexopathy, chemo-induced): combine opioids with gabapentinoid, SNRI / TCA; regional / neuraxial procedures.
  • Visceral pain: opioids + antispasmodics; consider coeliac plexus block for pancreatic / upper-abdominal malignancy.
  • Anticipatory medications at end-of-life: morphine, midazolam, hyoscine butylbromide (or glycopyrrolate), antiemetic.
  • Palliative-care input early in any cancer diagnosis with persistent pain — better symptom control, quality of life, and even survival (Temel NEJM 2010).

14. Opioid stewardship

  • Use opioids primarily for acute severe nociceptive pain, cancer pain, and end-of-life care. They are poorly effective and harmful in chronic non-cancer pain.
  • Initiation in chronic non-cancer pain — only as a time-limited trial, with explicit functional goals, after non-opioid pharmacotherapy and rehabilitation have failed. Document risk assessment (SOAPP, ORT), check prescription monitoring program.
  • Dose limits: many guidelines advise reassessing carefully at > 50 morphine milligram equivalents (MME) per day and avoiding > 90 MME/day where possible.
  • Co-prescribing risks: benzodiazepines (boxed warning — respiratory depression and death), gabapentinoids (FDA 2019 boxed warning — respiratory depression), alcohol, sedatives.
  • Overdose prevention: prescribe take-home naloxone for patient and family; counsel.
  • Tapering: slow (5–10% every 1–4 weeks; slower for long-term users); manage withdrawal symptoms; involve patient in plan; do not abruptly discontinue (rebound pain, withdrawal, mental-health crises, suicide).
  • Opioid use disorder: medication-assisted treatment with methadone or buprenorphine (cross-ref Module 13).
For any patient with chronic non-cancer pain on chronic opioids, consider a referral to a pain medicine specialist — both for opioid review and for an opioid-sparing multidisciplinary plan. Discuss the risk-benefit transparently and document. Naloxone co-prescribing is increasingly standard.

15. When to refer

  • Same day: red-flag back pain with cauda equina or new spinal cord signs (Module 10); suspected zoster ophthalmicus or Ramsay Hunt; new bilateral / atypical trigeminal neuralgia (suspect MS); CRPS with rapidly worsening function.
  • Within 1–2 weeks: refractory trigeminal neuralgia for MVD/SRS discussion; suspected small-fibre neuropathy needing skin biopsy / autonomic testing; CRPS not improving with first-line care; suspected fibromyalgia with diagnostic uncertainty.
  • Routine: chronic non-cancer pain on opioids for review and opioid-sparing plan; stable chronic neuropathic pain requiring complex regimens; functional rehabilitation for chronic pain with significant disability.
  • Always involve: multidisciplinary pain medicine, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, clinical psychology / CBT, palliative care for cancer pain.
