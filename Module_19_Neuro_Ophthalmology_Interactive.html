Why this module?

Neuro-ophthalmology is the most localising part of clinical neurology. A 30-second eye exam — visual acuity, fields, pupils, fundoscopy, motility — anchors the diagnosis in most cases. The general physician's job is to recognise the four classes of emergency (GCA, pupil-involving CN III, sudden bilateral disc swelling, pituitary apoplexy), to localise visual field defects accurately, and to refer the rest fast.

After this module you will:

  • Use the afferent/efferent + sidedness framework to localise any visual or motility problem.
  • Match visual field defects to lesion sites along the entire visual pathway.
  • Differentiate typical MS-type optic neuritis from NMOSD and MOGAD using clinical and antibody features.
  • Treat suspected GCA empirically before biopsy — and request the right workup.
  • Distinguish papilloedema from optic disc oedema of other causes and run the IIH workup.
  • Recognise Horner, Adie, Argyll-Robertson, and CN III palsy pupil patterns at the bedside.
  • Triage diplopia, isolated CN III/IV/VI palsies, INO, and dorsal midbrain syndrome.
  • Identify pupil-involving CN III palsy as a posterior communicating artery aneurysm until proven otherwise.
The single principle: three questions answer most patients — afferent or efferent? one eye or both? acute, subacute, or chronic? Then add a fourth — any pupil involvement? — and you have the diagnosis nearly cornered.

Lessons

1. Framework and visual pathway anatomy
  • Afferent = retina → optic nerve → chiasm → tract → LGN → optic radiations → occipital cortex; produces visual loss / field defects.
  • Efferent = cortical/subcortical gaze centres → MLF → CN III, IV, VI → muscles, or pupillary motor pathway; produces diplopia / nystagmus / anisocoria / gaze palsy.
  • Sidedness: monocular (in front of chiasm) vs binocular (chiasm or behind).

Pathway components:

  1. Retina → ganglion cells → 1.2 M axons per optic nerve.
  2. Optic nerve (CN II — CNS structure, oligodendrocyte myelin).
  3. Chiasm — nasal fibres cross; temporal stay ipsilateral.
  4. Optic tract — contralateral hemifield from both eyes.
  5. LGN — 6-layered thalamic relay.
  6. Radiations: parietal (inferior field representation), temporal Meyer's loop (superior field).
  7. V1 calcarine cortex — retinotopic; macula at occipital pole with MCA collateral → macular sparing in PCA stroke.

3. Transient visual loss

Monocular: amaurosis fugax → TIA pathway (Module 2); branch retinal artery occlusion → TIA workup; TVOs (seconds, posture-related, bilateral in raised ICP); retinal migraine; acute angle-closure glaucoma (painful red eye, halos, N/V).

Binocular: migraine aura (slow build, positive scintillations); vertebrobasilar TIA (with brainstem features); top-of-basilar (cortical blindness, Anton); occipital seizure; bilateral simultaneous AION (GCA).

6. Papilloedema, disc swelling differential, IIH

Papilloedema = optic disc swelling from raised ICP (bilateral). Distinguish from other disc oedema (papillitis, AION, hypertensive retinopathy, CRVO, infiltration, toxic, drusen).

Workup: MRI brain + MRV first (mass + CVST), then LP with opening pressure if imaging unremarkable.

IIH Friedman 2013 criteria: papilloedema + no localising signs (except CN VI) + normal MRI+MRV (may show empty sella, ONS enlargement, posterior globe flattening, TS stenosis) + opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O (28 children) with normal CSF + no other cause. Treat with weight loss (primary), acetazolamide 250 mg BD → 1–2 g/day (IIHTT 2014), topiramate alternative; surgery (ONSF, CSF shunt, venous sinus stenting) for fulminant or progressive vision loss. Cross-ref Module 4.

8. Diplopia and isolated CN III, IV, VI palsies

Three questions: monocular or binocular? horizontal/vertical/oblique? constant or variable (MG fatigable, painful with dissection/orbit/GCA, pupil with CN III + PCommA)?

  • CN III: ptosis + eye down-out + dilated unreactive pupil (compressive). Pupil-involving = PCommA aneurysm → urgent CTA/MRA. Pupil-sparing in vasculopathic patient (DM, HTN, > 50) = ischaemic; recovers ~3 months; image if atypical or < 50.
  • CN IV: superior oblique; longest intracranial course → most vulnerable to head trauma. Bielschowsky head tilt test (head tilted away from affected side compensates).
  • CN VI: longest extracranial course; vulnerable to raised ICP → false-localising sign in IIH. Painful CN VI → consider GCA in elderly, cavernous sinus, Tolosa-Hunt.
9. INO and gaze palsies

INO: lesion of MLF → ipsilateral failure of adduction + contralateral abducting nystagmus + preserved convergence. Bilateral INO in young → MS (Module 8); unilateral in older → stroke. "One-and-a-half syndrome": INO + ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy (abducens nucleus or PPRF lesion).

PPRF → horizontal gaze palsy. Dorsal midbrain (riMLF/INC) → Parinaud syndrome: upgaze palsy + light-near dissociation + lid retraction (Collier) + convergence-retraction nystagmus → pineal tumour, hydrocephalus, MS, stroke.

Supranuclear: PSP vertical gaze palsy + axial rigidity + early falls (Module 6); Niemann-Pick C in young patient; frontal eye fields stroke → contralateral conjugate gaze paresis ("look toward the lesion").

Chiasm: bitemporal hemianopia from pituitary macroadenoma (compresses from below — superior temporal fibres first), craniopharyngioma (above — lower fibres), meningioma. Pituitary apoplexy (Module 12): thunderclap headache + ophthalmoplegia (cavernous sinus involvement) + bitemporal hemianopia + panhypopituitarism → hydrocortisone 100 mg IV stat + urgent MRI + neurosurgery.

Cavernous sinus syndrome: CN III, IV, VI, V1 (± V2) + ipsilateral Horner. Causes: tumour, thrombosis (proptosis + chemosis + fever + IV antibiotics + anticoagulation), carotid-cavernous fistula (pulsatile proptosis + bruit), Tolosa-Hunt (painful, steroid-responsive). Superior orbital fissure spares CN II; orbital apex involves CN II.

Thyroid eye disease: lid retraction + proptosis + restrictive ophthalmoplegia (IMSLO: inferior rectus first) + optic nerve compression severe; smoking cessation, selenium, IV methylpred, teprotumumab (FDA 2020 — IGF-1R mAb) transformative for active moderate-severe disease.

11. Neuro-ophthalmic emergencies and when to refer
  • Suspected GCA — IV methylprednisolone immediately, biopsy/US within 1–2 weeks.
  • Pupil-involving CN III — urgent CTA/MRA for PCommA aneurysm.
  • Pituitary apoplexy — hydrocortisone IV stat + urgent MRI + neurosurgery (Module 12).
  • Sudden bilateral disc swelling — MRI + MRV first, then LP (IIH workup).
  • Sudden monocular visual loss in older patient — GCA workup; in younger — optic neuritis or TIA (Module 2).
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis — IV antibiotics + anticoagulation + ENT/ophthalmology/neurosurgery.
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma — IOP lowering + iridotomy.
  • Severe AQP4/MOG optic neuritis — IV methylprednisolone + plasma exchange early.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 30-year-old woman has 4 days of left eye pain on movement and progressive blurred vision. Acuity 6/24 left, 6/6 right. Left RAPD. Colour vision markedly reduced left. Disc normal on fundoscopy

MRI orbits/brain with gadolinium and fat suppression: short-segment mid-orbital optic nerve enhancement on the left + 2 small periventricular T2 lesions in the brain.

Diagnosis: Typical (MS-type) retrobulbar optic neuritis.

Plan:

  1. IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3–5 days (speeds recovery; no effect on final visual acuity per ONTT). Do not give oral prednisolone alone — increases recurrence.
  2. Anti-AQP4 and anti-MOG serology (clinical picture is typical MS-type, but exclude NMOSD and MOGAD given longer-term implications).
  3. MRI confirms ≥ 1 typical lesion → discuss MS conversion risk; ~50% over 15 years. Refer to MS clinic for DMT discussion (cross-ref Module 8 — McDonald 2017).
  4. LP for OCBs if MS workup needed.
  5. OCT (RNFL + GCL) — baseline for follow-up; thinning develops over weeks.
  6. Counsel about Uhthoff phenomenon (transient blurring with heat/exercise) and Pulfrich phenomenon (motion perception illusion).
Case 2 — A 75-year-old has 2 days of dull bitemporal headache, scalp tenderness when combing hair, jaw pain when chewing, and now sudden visual loss in the right eye to perception of light only. ESR 95, CRP 110

Examination: pallid disc swelling on the right; visual acuity 6/6 left, perception of light right. Temporal artery palpable and tender, no pulsation.

Diagnosis: Giant cell arteritis with arteritic AION; emergency.

Plan:

  1. IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3 days, then oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day immediately — do not wait for biopsy. The opposite eye is at imminent risk.
  2. Temporal artery biopsy within 1–2 weeks (yield preserved up to 2 weeks of steroid) — adequate length (≥ 1 cm); request skip lesions awareness from pathology. Temporal artery ultrasound (halo sign) is an alternative or supplement per EULAR 2018.
  3. Add aspirin 75 mg daily — reduces ischaemic events in GCA.
  4. Add tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor antagonist; GiACTA NEJM 2017) for steroid-sparing in selected patients.
  5. Bone protection (calcium + vitamin D + bisphosphonate); PCP prophylaxis; PPI; blood-glucose monitoring.
  6. Long taper over 12–24 months; monitor ESR/CRP and symptoms; relapse common.
  7. Rheumatology referral.
Case 3 — A 25-year-old obese woman has 3 months of daily headache, worse on bending and on waking, with episodes of transient blackouts of vision lasting seconds when she stands. Pulsatile tinnitus. Fundoscopy: bilateral papilloedema

Examination otherwise normal. MRI brain + MRV: partially empty sella, flattened posterior globes, optic nerve sheath dilation, mild transverse sinus stenosis bilaterally; no mass or thrombosis. LP opening pressure 32 cm H₂O in lateral decubitus; normal CSF analysis.

Diagnosis: Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (Friedman 2013 modified Dandy criteria met).

Plan:

  1. Weight loss — primary treatment; aim for 6–10% body weight reduction (often resolves IIH). Refer to dietitian; consider bariatric surgery in selected.
  2. Acetazolamide 250 mg BD titrated to 1–2 g/day (IIHTT 2014); monitor electrolytes (K), paraesthesiae, taste disturbance; pregnancy contraindication.
  3. Topiramate as alternative or adjunct (weight-loss benefit).
  4. Formal perimetry — protect vision; serial fields.
  5. Headache management (Module 4); avoid medication-overuse.
  6. Review medications that can cause secondary IIH: tetracyclines, vitamin A / retinoids, growth hormone, lithium.
  7. If fulminant or progressive vision loss: optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting (VP or LP); venous sinus stenting in selected cases with transverse sinus stenosis.
  8. Counsel: IIH is sight-threatening — adherence to follow-up is essential.
Case 4 — A 55-year-old man develops sudden severe headache, droopy right eyelid, and double vision. Examination: complete right ptosis; right eye "down and out"; right pupil dilated 7 mm, poorly reactive to light. The left pupil is 3 mm and brisk

CTA head and neck: 8 mm right posterior communicating artery aneurysm.

Diagnosis: Pupil-involving right CN III palsy due to PCommA aneurysm — emergency.

Plan:

  1. Urgent neurosurgical / neuro-interventional consultation — endovascular coiling or surgical clipping.
  2. Manage BP carefully; analgesia.
  3. ICU monitoring for SAH (cross-ref Module 4 / Module 2): nimodipine prophylaxis for vasospasm.
  4. Counsel: parasympathetic fibres ride on the surface of CN III, most vulnerable to external compression. Pupil-involving CN III palsy is PCommA aneurysm until proven otherwise — must image urgently with CTA / MRA / DSA.
  5. Compare with: pupil-sparing CN III palsy in vasculopathic patient (DM, HTN, > 50) → usually ischaemic (microvascular), recovers ~3 months; still image if any atypical features, age < 50, or no recovery.
Case 5 — A 32-year-old woman has 3 days of horizontal diplopia worse on left gaze. Examination: failure of adduction of the right eye on left gaze + abducting nystagmus of the left eye; convergence preserved. Acuity normal; no other deficits

Symptoms suggestive of MS — 3 years ago she had a 4-week episode of right leg weakness that recovered.

Diagnosis: Right internuclear ophthalmoplegia from a right MLF lesion — likely MS (Module 8).

Plan:

  1. MRI brain and spine with gadolinium — look for periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord T2/FLAIR lesions; apply McDonald 2017 criteria.
  2. LP for OCBs and IgG index.
  3. Bloods: full panel + anti-AQP4, anti-MOG, vitamin B12, TFT, HIV, syphilis serology.
  4. If MS confirmed: IV methylprednisolone for relapses; high-efficacy DMT discussion (ocrelizumab, natalizumab, ofatumumab, etc.).
  5. Counsel: bilateral INO in a young patient is highly suggestive of MS; unilateral INO in older patient → stroke. Watch for "one-and-a-half" syndrome (INO + ipsilateral horizontal gaze palsy).
  6. Refer to MS clinic and ophthalmology.
Case 6 — A 38-year-old woman 1 week after a minor neck strain develops sudden right-sided ptosis, miosis, and facial sweating reduction. Severe right-sided neck pain

Examination: right ptosis 2 mm; right pupil 3 mm, left pupil 5 mm; anisocoria worse in dark; ipsilateral facial anhidrosis. No other neurology.

Diagnosis: Right Horner syndrome — suspect right internal carotid artery dissection (third-order neuron) until proven otherwise.

Plan:

  1. Urgent CT angiography of head and neck — look for string sign, intramural haematoma, double lumen, pseudoaneurysm at the carotid.
  2. If confirmed dissection: antiplatelet (aspirin) or anticoagulation per CADISS-style decision; cross-ref Module 2 for stroke prevention.
  3. Pharmacologic confirmation if needed: apraclonidine 0.5% reverses the anisocoria (denervation supersensitivity of α1 on dilator). Cocaine 4–10% fails to dilate a Horner pupil; hydroxyamphetamine localises to third-order if fails to dilate.
  4. Stroke unit monitoring for early ischaemic events (vessel wall haematoma can occlude; watch for hemispheric or retinal ischaemia).
  5. Other Horner causes to consider if no dissection: 1st-order brainstem/cord (lateral medullary stroke, syringomyelia); 2nd-order Pancoast tumour or cervical disease (CXR/CT chest).
  6. Refer to stroke neurology.
