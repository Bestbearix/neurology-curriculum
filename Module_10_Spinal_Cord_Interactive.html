Why this module?

Spinal cord disorders include some of the most time-critical emergencies in neurology. Metastatic epidural cord compression, cauda equina syndrome, traumatic SCI, and epidural abscess all have a window measured in hours during which neurological function can still be salvaged. After that, the deficit is permanent. The cord has minimal redundancy.

After this module you will:

  • Recognise a cord lesion at the bedside — sensory level, bilateral long-tract signs, sphincter dysfunction.
  • Distinguish the classic cord syndromes by exam pattern alone.
  • Run the emergency workup for suspected compressive myelopathy.
  • Distinguish conus medullaris from cauda equina syndrome.
  • Recognise and treat autonomic dysreflexia in chronic SCI.
  • Apply the ASIA Impairment Scale.
  • Diagnose and treat reversible causes — B12, copper, dural AVF, abscess, infectious myelopathies.
The single principle that drives every clinical decision in this module: in a suspected acute compressive cord lesion, get the MRI and give dexamethasone and page spine surgery — in parallel, not sequence. Outcome is determined by neurological state at decompression. Hours matter.

Lessons

1. Recognising a cord lesion

Three clues nail it:

  1. Sensory level on the trunk (T4 nipple, T6 xiphoid, T10 umbilicus, L1 groin).
  2. Bilateral long-tract signs below the level (motor, dorsal columns, spinothalamic).
  3. Sphincter dysfunction — urinary retention or incontinence; loss of anal tone; saddle anaesthesia.

Then ask: at what level (one-level rule: image at least one level above the suspected level)? Is it compressive (the emergency)? What is the tempo?

A vertebral level is NOT the cord segment level. The cord ends at L1–L2; below that is the cauda equina. A T12 vertebral fracture damages the conus, not the thoracic cord.

4. Compressive myelopathy — the emergencies

MESCC (metastatic epidural cord compression): 5–10% of cancer patients; thoracic cord most common; progressive back pain usually precedes weakness by weeks. Pain worse with recumbency is the clue distinguishing from mechanical back pain.

Six-hour checklist for compressive myelopathy:

  1. Stat MRI whole spine (multiple-level disease in 30–50% of MESCC) with gadolinium.
  2. Dexamethasone IV (10 mg loading, then 4 mg q6h) if MESCC plausible.
  3. Neurosurgery / spine surgery referral within hours.
  4. Antibiotics (vancomycin + ceftriaxone) if epidural abscess suspected.
  5. Reverse anticoagulation if epidural haematoma suspected.
  6. Treat underlying cause (cancer, infection, autoimmune).

Patchell trial (2005): surgical decompression + RT beats RT alone for ambulation outcomes when life expectancy > 3 months and tumour not radiosensitive.

5. Vascular cord syndromes

Anterior spinal artery infarct: sudden bilateral motor + pain/temp loss, sphincter dysfunction, dorsal columns SPARED. Causes: aortic dissection or aortic repair, severe hypotension, atherosclerosis, fibrocartilaginous embolism, vasculitis. MRI may be normal early; DWI helps; pencil T2 hyperintensity over multiple segments; "owl eyes" on axial.

Dural arteriovenous fistula: middle-aged-to-older man with progressive paraparesis, urinary symptoms, exercise-induced worsening. MRI shows dilated vessels on cord surface + central T2 hyperintensity. A treatable cause of progressive myelopathy frequently missed.

6. Transverse myelitis

A syndrome — not a diagnosis. Subacute (days–weeks) inflammation of the cord. After workup, ~70% have a defined cause; ~30% remain idiopathic.

Top categories: MS (short partial lesions), NMOSD (LETM ≥ 3 segments, AQP4-IgG), MOGAD (conus, MOG-IgG), sarcoidosis (longitudinally extensive with dorsal subpial "trident" enhancement), connective tissue disease, paraneoplastic, parainfectious.

Workup: MRI whole spine + brain + LP (cells, OCB, AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG, viral PCR) + serum (AQP4, MOG, ACE, ANA, ENA, ANCA, antiphospholipid, paraneoplastic) + chest CT.

Treatment: IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 5 days; PLEX for severe / refractory (lower threshold in NMOSD); maintenance therapy depends on aetiology.

Cross-reference Module 8 for MS / NMOSD / MOGAD detail.

7. Infectious myelopathies
  • HSV / VZV — acute / necrotising; treat with IV aciclovir.
  • Enterovirus / West Nile — acute flaccid paralysis (anterior horn).
  • HIV vacuolar myelopathy — mimics B12 SCD; ART improves.
  • HTLV-1 (tropical spastic paraparesis) — slow spastic paraparesis + bladder; Caribbean, Japan, equatorial Africa.
  • Neurosyphilis (tabes dorsalis) — lancinating pains, ataxia, Argyll Robertson pupils.
  • Tuberculosis (Pott) — vertebral collapse, gibbus, anterior compression.
  • Schistosomiasis, Lyme, brucellosis, fungal infections — pathogen-specific.
8. Metabolic, toxic & nutritional myelopathies

B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration): dorsal columns + lateral corticospinal tracts; symmetric vibration/proprioception loss + spasticity; macrocytosis often absent. Check B12, MMA, homocysteine. Treat IM or oral high-dose. Watch for nitrous oxide abuse (whippets) — inactivates B12 within hours and can cause myelopathy in a young person with normal serum B12.

Copper deficiency: mimics B12 SCD; bariatric surgery and zinc excess (denture cream!) are common causes. Check copper, ceruloplasmin, zinc.

Other: heroin, intrathecal chemotherapy, decompression sickness, radiation myelopathy (early transient vs late delayed).

9. Degenerative & hereditary myelopathies

Spondylotic myelopathy — most common cause of myelopathy in adults > 55. Cervical > thoracic. Mixed UMN (cord compromise) + LMN (root compromise) — "myeloradiculopathy." Hand clumsiness, gait stiffness, Hoffman, Lhermitte. MRI: stenosis + cord T2 hyperintensity (myelomalacia). Treat with decompressive surgery if moderate-to-severe or progressive.

Hereditary spastic paraparesis (HSP) — pure or complicated; SPAST (SPG4) most common. Family history; genetic testing.

Adrenomyeloneuropathy (X-linked) — ABCD1; young men with spastic paraparesis + adrenal insufficiency + peripheral neuropathy; elevated VLCFAs.

Friedreich ataxia — GAA expansion in FXN; childhood/adolescent; areflexia + dorsal column loss + cerebellar ataxia + cardiomyopathy. Omaveloxolone (SKYCLARYS) is the first FDA-approved (2023) DMT.

10. Syringomyelia

Fluid-filled cavity, usually central cervical cord. Classic: dissociated sensory loss in a "cape" distribution (pain/temp lost, touch and proprioception preserved); LMN weakness in arms; UMN signs in legs later.

Often associated with Chiari I malformation. Post-traumatic, post-myelitic, or tumour-associated.

MRI confirms. Treat: suboccipital decompression for Chiari-associated; shunt for refractory.

11. Traumatic SCI & ASIA scale

Acute care: ABCDE, C-spine immobilisation, MAP ≥ 85 for 5–7 days, imaging, early decompression (< 24 h) for incomplete cervical SCI with persistent compression.

Methylprednisolone: controversial; AANS/CNS does NOT recommend routine use; AOSpine 2017 allows it as an option within 8 h of blunt SCI; many centres no longer use it (NASCIS trials' marginal benefit at considerable infection / GI / mortality cost).

ASIA Impairment Scale: A (complete) → E (normal). The calculator tab takes you through grading.

Spinal shock vs neurogenic shock: spinal shock = transient flaccid/areflexic phase; neurogenic shock = hypotension + bradycardia from loss of sympathetic outflow (lesion above T6).

12. Autonomic dysreflexia

Chronic SCI above T6 + noxious stimulus below the lesion → unregulated sympathetic outflow → paroxysmal severe hypertension + pounding headache + flushing above level + pallor and pilo-erection below + reflex bradycardia.

Common triggers: blocked urinary catheter, faecal impaction, pressure sore, ingrown toenail, UTI.

Management:

  1. Sit the patient up (orthostatic drop helps).
  2. Loosen tight clothing.
  3. Find and remove the trigger immediately (unblock catheter, manual disimpaction).
  4. Antihypertensive (nifedipine bite-and-swallow, nitroglycerin, captopril) only if BP severely elevated and trigger not quickly removed.
A true neurological emergency. Untreated → stroke, seizure, MI, death. Don't give long-acting antihypertensives reflexively — fix the trigger first.

Both are surgical emergencies when compressive. Time to surgery within 24–48 h of bowel/bladder symptoms is the key prognostic factor.

14. Neurogenic bladder & bowel

Suprasacral cord lesion: spastic / UMN bladder — detrusor hyperreflexia ± dyssynergia, urgency, incontinence, incomplete emptying.

Conus/cauda lesion: flaccid / LMN bladder — detrusor areflexia, overflow incontinence, retention.

Management: clean intermittent self-catheterisation (preferred), antimuscarinics or mirabegron (β3) for overactive detrusor, intradetrusor botulinum toxin for refractory, α-blocker for sphincter dyssynergia, urologic follow-up.

Bowel: regular routine, fibre, suppositories, manual evacuation; faecal impaction is a major autonomic dysreflexia trigger.

15. When to refer
Same-day emergency referral:
  • Suspected compressive cord lesion (MESCC, abscess, haematoma).
  • Cauda equina syndrome.
  • Conus medullaris syndrome.
  • Traumatic SCI.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia in chronic high-cord SCI.
Urgent neurology referral: transverse myelitis (start steroids + workup); spinal cord stroke; progressive spondylotic myelopathy; B12/copper deficiency that does not improve with replacement; suspected hereditary myelopathy for genetic testing.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 64-year-old man with breast-cancer history and 2 weeks of mid-thoracic back pain, now bilateral leg weakness over 6 hours

Pain was constant, worse at night (woke him up). Now power 3/5 bilaterally, T8 sensory level on the trunk, urinary retention. Reflexes initially flaccid (spinal shock); plantars equivocal.

Diagnosis: Metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC) at thoracic level. Time-critical.

Plan: Stat MRI whole spine with gadolinium (not just the thoracic level — multiple-level disease in 30–50%). Dexamethasone 10 mg IV loading, then 4 mg q6h. Neurosurgery / spine surgery referral within the hour. Workup primary site. If life expectancy > 3 months and tumour not radiosensitive → surgical decompression + post-op RT (Patchell trial). Otherwise → RT alone. Treat the underlying cancer. Catheterise bladder. DVT prophylaxis.

Case 2 — A 28-year-old IV drug user with 4 days of fever, severe back pain, and progressive paraparesis

Temp 38.7. T10 sensory level. Paraparesis 2/5. Reflexes initially absent (spinal shock). New urinary retention. WBC 18,000; CRP 240. Recent dental abscess treated with oral antibiotics.

Diagnosis: Spinal epidural abscess.

Plan: Blood cultures × 2. Empirical IV vancomycin + ceftriaxone immediately (do not wait for cultures). Stat MRI whole spine with gadolinium. Neurosurgical / spine surgery consultation for emergent drainage. Echocardiogram to look for endocarditis. Notify ID. Cord salvage rate is far better with surgical drainage + antibiotics started within 24 h of neurological deficit than antibiotics alone.

Case 3 — A 35-year-old vegan with 6 months of progressive gait unsteadiness and tingling in feet

Wide-based unsteady gait; positive Romberg (worse with eyes closed); reduced vibration and proprioception to the knees; mildly brisk reflexes; upgoing plantars. Macrocytic anaemia mild. Serum B12 low; methylmalonic acid markedly elevated. Also occasionally uses recreational nitrous oxide (whippets) for the past 8 months.

Diagnosis: Subacute combined degeneration from B12 deficiency, amplified by chronic N₂O exposure.

Plan: Stop N₂O immediately and counsel about it. IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg daily × 1 week, then weekly × 4 weeks, then monthly. Folate supplementation. Dietary advice / supplements (vegan diet). Reassess at 4–6 weeks; ataxia and proprioceptive loss may improve partially but may not fully resolve if treatment is delayed.

Case 4 — A 68-year-old man with chronic neck pain and 12 months of hand clumsiness and stiff gait

Difficulty buttoning shirts; recent falls; urinary urgency. Exam: spastic gait, Hoffman positive bilaterally, hyperreflexia in arms and legs, clonus at ankles, positive Babinski. Cervical spine MRI: multi-level spinal stenosis worst at C4-C6 with cord T2 hyperintensity.

Diagnosis: Cervical spondylotic myelopathy.

Plan: Refer to spine surgery for decompression (cervical laminoplasty or laminectomy with fusion, or anterior cervical discectomy and fusion depending on pathology). Conservative measures for mild disease (PT, activity modification, soft collar in select cases). Surgical decompression improves outcomes for moderate-to-severe myelopathy.

Case 5 — A 56-year-old paraplegic from chronic T4 SCI is brought in with pounding headache, BP 220/130, sweating above the chest

Indwelling Foley catheter. Has not had a bowel movement for 5 days. Skin flushed above the chest, pale below; pilo-erection on legs. HR 50.

Diagnosis: Autonomic dysreflexia, trigger likely blocked catheter and/or faecal impaction.

Plan:

  1. Sit the patient up.
  2. Loosen clothing.
  3. Examine the urinary catheter — flush, change, or place new one. Manual disimpaction if rectal exam confirms impaction.
  4. If BP remains severely elevated and trigger not yet removed: nifedipine immediate-release 10 mg or nitroglycerin sublingual.
BP usually normalises within minutes once trigger is removed. Educate patient and family about recognition and prevention.

Don't reach for IV labetalol first. The fix is mechanical — find and remove the trigger.
Case 6 — A 45-year-old man with 2 days of severe lumbar back pain, now numb perineum and unable to urinate

Picked up his child; sudden severe lumbar pain radiating down the right leg, then progressive numbness across both buttocks and inner thighs. Tried to urinate — could not. Bladder scan: 800 mL residual. Exam: absent ankle jerks, weak ankle dorsiflexion bilaterally R > L, sensory loss in saddle and L5/S1 dermatomes, anal tone reduced.

Diagnosis: Cauda equina syndrome from large central lumbar disc herniation until proven otherwise.

Plan: Stat MRI lumbosacral spine. Urinary catheter. Stat spine surgery referral. Emergency decompression within 24–48 h of bowel/bladder onset — the strongest predictor of recovery. Document neurological exam precisely (the legal threshold for "complete CES" vs "incomplete CES" influences urgency and outcome assessment).

Cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency. Don't wait for the elective spine clinic — the patient needs an MRI today and an operation tomorrow at the latest.
