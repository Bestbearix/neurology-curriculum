1. Framework — five questions in 30 seconds

Weakness is the symptom that triggers more neurology referrals than any other. A structured approach turns it from a vague complaint into a localisable, treatable problem. Ask five questions in order and you have nearly cornered the diagnosis:

  1. Is it really weakness — or fatigue, pain inhibition, ataxia, apraxia, or functional?
  2. Tempo — hyperacute (seconds–minutes), acute (hours–days), subacute (days–weeks), chronic (weeks–months), episodic / fatigable?
  3. Pattern — focal (one limb / hemiparesis / monoparesis), symmetric (proximal vs distal), bilateral with sensory level, cranial / bulbar prominent, fatigable, length-dependent?
  4. Level — UMN, LMN (anterior horn / nerve / plexus / root), NMJ, or muscle?
  5. Associated features — sensory, autonomic, pain, sphincter, bulbar, respiratory, systemic, drugs, family history?
The single principle: tempo + pattern + level usually localises 95% of weakness. Run the five-question loop in < 30 seconds, then ask one more question on every visit — "anything that doesn't fit?" — to catch the rare emergency (acute spinal cord compression, GBS, MG crisis, dissection-stroke, hypokalaemic paralysis, botulism, polio-like syndrome).

2. Rule out emergencies BEFORE everything else

The "do not miss" checklist for new weakness — run it on every patient before you sit down to history:
  • Acute spinal cord compression (MESCC, abscess, haematoma, fracture) — sensory level + bilateral leg weakness + sphincter dysfunction → urgent MRI whole spine + dexamethasone if malignancy + neurosurgery (Module 10).
  • Stroke (cortical, brainstem, lacunar) — sudden hemiparesis or crossed signs; FAST screen; thrombolysis / thrombectomy window (Module 2).
  • Guillain-Barré syndrome — ascending weakness + areflexia + respiratory involvement; 20/30/40 rule (FVC < 20 mL/kg, NIF less negative than −30, MEP < 40); ICU with telemetry for autonomic instability; IVIG / PLEX (Module 9, 15, 22).
  • Myasthenic crisis — bulbar / respiratory failure; avoid known precipitants (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, β-blockers, magnesium); PLEX / IVIG (Module 9, 20).
  • Botulism — descending paralysis + dilated pupils + dry mouth + bulbar; BIG-IV for infants, equine antitoxin for adults (Module 9, 16).
  • Tick paralysis — ascending paralysis with tick attached; remove the tick.
  • Hypokalaemic periodic paralysis (incl. thyrotoxic in East Asian men) — episodic; cautious K replacement + propranolol if thyrotoxic (Module 9, 23).
  • Carotid / vertebral dissection — neck pain + Horner + cranial nerves + stroke (Module 2, 19).
  • Brainstem stroke — crossed signs (ipsilateral cranial nerve + contralateral hemiparesis); urgent imaging (Module 2).
  • Wernicke encephalopathy — confusion + nystagmus + ataxia + nutritional risk → thiamine before glucose (Modules 13, 20).
  • Hypoglycaemia mimicking stroke / focal weakness → glucose stat.
  • Eclampsia / PRES in pregnancy — magnesium + antihypertensive + delivery (Module 20).
  • NMS / serotonin syndrome / MH hyperthermia with rigidity → Module 13.

6. Upper motor neuron lesions

  • Cortical: hemiparesis with face/arm or arm/leg predominance; aphasia (dominant); neglect (non-dominant) — stroke, tumour, abscess, MS plaque (Module 2, 8, 11, 12).
  • Subcortical / internal capsule: dense pure motor hemiparesis (lacunar stroke); pure motor lacunar syndrome (Module 2).
  • Brainstem: crossed signs — ipsilateral cranial nerve + contralateral hemiparesis; INO; gaze palsies (Module 2, 19).
  • Spinal cord: bilateral UMN weakness below the level + sensory level + sphincter (Module 10).
  • ALS UMN signs: with LMN signs (without sensory) suggests ALS (Module 9).
  • Hereditary spastic paraplegia: chronic progressive spasticity + minimal weakness; family history.
  • Post-stroke spasticity: emerges over weeks (Module 25 spasticity ladder — physiotherapy → oral antispastics → BoNT-A → intrathecal baclofen).

8. Neuromuscular junction

  • Myasthenia gravis (MG) (Module 9): fatigable bulbar + ocular + proximal limb; AChR antibodies (or MuSK or seronegative); decremental response on RNS; pyridostigmine + steroid + steroid-sparing (azathioprine, MMF, rituximab); thymectomy in selected; modern biologics: eculizumab, efgartigimod (FcRn 2021), rozanolixizumab (2023), zilucoplan (2023). Crisis: PLEX / IVIG.
  • LEMS (Module 12): proximal lower limb weakness improving with brief exercise; dry mouth; reduced reflexes that improve after exercise; incremental response on RNS > 60%; VGCC antibodies; 60% have SCLC (paraneoplastic); 3,4-diaminopyridine + treat tumour.
  • Botulism (Module 9, 16): descending paralysis + dilated unreactive pupils + dry mouth + bulbar; BIG-IV for infants; equine antitoxin for adults.
  • Congenital myasthenic syndromes (Module 16): paediatric ptosis + fatigable weakness; genetic testing; targeted therapy by subtype.
  • Drug-induced NMJ block: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, magnesium, β-blockers can precipitate MG crisis; succinylcholine in burns / SCI / GBS → hyperkalaemia (Module 9, 20).

10. Functional weakness

Cross-ref Module 21. Use positive bedside signs to make a rule-in diagnosis:

  • Hoover's sign: weak hip extension paretic side normalises with contralateral hip flexion against resistance (sensitivity ~63%, specificity ~100%; Stone JNNP 2010).
  • Hip abductor sign (Sonoo).
  • Drag / monoplegic gait without circumduction.
  • Give-way weakness with sudden loss of resistance.
  • Inconsistent power — strong on automatic tasks, weak on formal testing.
  • Spinal Injuries Center sign: supine leg won't lift; sitting it lifts effortlessly.
  • Communicate diagnosis using Stone framework (Module 21); avoid dismissive phrases; demonstrate the positive sign to the patient; refer to FND-specific physiotherapy.
  • Coexistence with neurological disease is common (~10–20% of PNES patients have epilepsy; functional movements coexist with PD).

11. Systemic / metabolic causes of weakness

  • Electrolyte: hypokalaemia (paralysis at < 2.5; rhabdomyolysis); hyperkalaemia (cardiac > muscle); hypocalcaemia (tetany); hypomagnesaemia (cramps, weakness); hypophosphataemia (severe → diaphragm paralysis in refeeding).
  • Endocrine: thyroid (myxoedema or thyrotoxic); Cushing; hyperPTH; hypoadrenal; pituitary; diabetes (Module 23).
  • Nutritional: B12 (subacute combined degeneration — Module 13); B1 (Wernicke / dry beriberi); B6 (sensory neuronopathy); copper (myeloneuropathy mimics B12); vitamin D (proximal myopathy); vitamin E (spinocerebellar) (Module 13).
  • Toxic / drug: alcohol (Module 13); statin; steroid; aminoglycoside; isoniazid (B6 deficiency); vincristine; cisplatin / oxaliplatin; metronidazole; bortezomib; nitrous oxide abuse (B12 functional deficiency — Module 13); methanol; CO; heavy metals (lead → motor neuropathy / wrist drop; arsenic; mercury) (Module 13).
  • Infection (Module 11): polio-like syndromes (enterovirus D68 acute flaccid myelitis); West Nile; rabies; tetanus (rigidity not paralysis); diphtheria neuropathy; HIV; Lyme; leprosy.
  • Paraneoplastic (Module 12): LEMS; anti-Hu sensory ganglionopathy; dermatomyositis; SCLC-associated.
  • Rheumatological (Module 23): SLE; Sjögren neuropathy; vasculitides; RA atlantoaxial subluxation; sarcoid.
  • Renal failure: uraemic neuropathy; uraemic myopathy.
  • Hepatic failure: hepatic myelopathy (rare).

13. Decision points — admit vs same-day clinic vs outpatient

Admit immediately:
  • Stroke / TIA (within window for reperfusion).
  • Acute spinal cord compression suspected.
  • GBS with respiratory compromise (FVC < 20 mL/kg, NIF less negative than −30, MEP < 40) or autonomic instability.
  • Myasthenic crisis (bulbar / respiratory failure).
  • Botulism suspected.
  • Rhabdomyolysis with renal compromise.
  • Hypokalaemic periodic paralysis with severe weakness.
  • Eclampsia / PRES.
  • NMS / serotonin syndrome / MH.
  • Hyperthermia with rigidity.
  • New severe / progressive weakness with unclear cause.
Same-day or 24-hour clinic:
  • Subacute progressive weakness without respiratory compromise.
  • New radicular pain + motor weakness in a stable patient.
  • Suspected MG without crisis (need RNS + antibody testing).
  • Suspected dermatomyositis / polymyositis with rapid progression.
  • Suspected vasculitic neuropathy (mononeuritis multiplex).
  • Coexisting systemic illness (Module 23) suggesting urgent workup.
Outpatient:
  • Chronic progressive weakness (CIDP, MG with stable disease, muscular dystrophy, ALS — coordinated multidisciplinary clinics — Module 9, 25).
  • Mild radiculopathy without weakness.
  • Carpal tunnel; meralgia paresthetica; common mononeuropathies.
  • Functional weakness (Module 21) — neurology + FND clinic.
  • Statin myalgia without CK rise or weakness.

14. When to refer

  • Neurosurgery: spinal cord compression, MESCC, herniation, refractory radiculopathy.
  • Neurology: any new unexplained weakness; MG / LEMS workup; CIDP; ALS; suspected vasculitis; functional weakness; channelopathy.
  • Rheumatology / immunology: inflammatory myopathy, vasculitis (Module 23).
  • Cardiology: muscular dystrophy with cardiac involvement; myotonic dystrophy; LGMD; mitochondrial.
  • Endocrinology: thyroid / Cushing / hyperPTH / vitamin D myopathy.
  • Rehabilitation (Module 25): all moderate-severe weakness for multidisciplinary care.
  • Genetic / metabolic: suspected hereditary neuropathy, channelopathy, dystrophy, mitochondrial.
  • Palliative care: ALS, advanced muscular dystrophy, progressive incurable disease (Module 25).
