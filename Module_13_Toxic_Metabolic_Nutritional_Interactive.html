Why this module?

Toxic, metabolic, and nutritional disorders mimic almost everything in neurology — and many are completely reversible if recognised early. The general physician sees them constantly: alcohol withdrawal, hepatic encephalopathy, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, drug toxicity, and missed Wernicke encephalopathy are everyday hospital problems. Missing them costs lives and permanent disability.

After this module you will:

  • Build a structured approach to acute encephalopathy and identify reversible causes early.
  • Recognise and manage alcohol withdrawal and Wernicke-Korsakoff before damage becomes permanent.
  • Diagnose and treat hepatic, uraemic, and electrolyte-related encephalopathies.
  • Correct sodium safely and recognise central pontine myelinolysis.
  • Differentiate NMS, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, anticholinergic, and sympathomimetic syndromes — and treat each correctly.
  • Recognise vitamin deficiencies (B1, B12, B6, niacin, E, copper) and metal poisoning.
  • Identify Wilson disease in any young patient with combined neuropsychiatric + hepatic features.
  • Manage opioid overdose and connect patients to addiction services.
The single principle that prevents disasters: in any acute encephalopathy, give thiamine 500 mg IV BEFORE glucose if Wernicke is possible (alcohol use, malnutrition, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery, prolonged TPN, GI malignancy). Wernicke encephalopathy is a clinical diagnosis, the classic triad is present in only ~16%, and the cost of missing it is Korsakoff psychosis — permanent.

Lessons

1. Bedside approach to acute encephalopathy

Toxic/metabolic encephalopathies share features that distinguish them from structural disease:

  • Fluctuating attention and cognition (defining feature of delirium).
  • Symmetric motor signs — paratonia, multifocal myoclonus, asterixis, tremor. Focal deficits suggest structural until proven otherwise.
  • Symmetric pupil/oculomotor signs; small reactive pupils typical.
  • Preserved brainstem reflexes until very deep coma.
  • Diffuse EEG slowing ± triphasic waves.

Empirical resuscitation cocktail: O₂, IV access, glucose check (50 mL of 50% dextrose if low or unknown), thiamine 500 mg IV before glucose if Wernicke possible, naloxone 0.4–2 mg IV if opioid possible; flumazenil generally avoided (precipitates seizures in tolerant patients).
2. Hepatic encephalopathy

Multifactorial — ammonia + inflammation + GABAergic tone. West Haven grading 0–IV. Precipitants — HEPATICS: Hypovolaemia, Electrolytes, Protein load (GI bleed), Alkalosis, TIPS, Infection (SBP), Constipation, Sedatives.

  1. Identify and treat precipitant — SBP, GI bleed, hypokalaemia, dehydration, sedatives, constipation.
  2. Lactulose 30–45 mL po q1–2h until soft stools, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day.
  3. Rifaximin 550 mg po BID for recurrence prevention.
  4. Avoid protein-restriction long-term (sarcopenia worsens outcome).
  5. Liver transplant for refractory disease.
Ammonia caveat: normal arterial ammonia largely rules out HE; venous ammonia unreliable. Don't titrate to ammonia — titrate to mental status and bowel movements.
3. Hyponatraemia and central pontine myelinolysis
  • Acute symptomatic severe (Na < 125 mmol/L + seizures, coma): 100 mL of 3% saline IV bolus over 10 min, repeat × 2 if symptoms persist (target rise of 4–6 mmol/L to abolish symptoms).
  • Correction limits: ≤ 8 mmol/L in 24 h (≤ 6 in alcoholics / malnourished / hypokalaemic).
  • If overcorrection (> 10 mmol/L/24 h), re-lower with D5W ± desmopressin.
  • CPM/ODS presents 2–6 days later: pseudobulbar palsy, quadriparesis, "locked-in", encephalopathy. MRI: pons hyperintense. Once developed, supportive care only.
Most CPM cases are iatrogenic. The single most important preventive step is slow, monitored Na correction. Check Na every 2–4 hours during active correction.
4. Alcohol withdrawal and Wernicke-Korsakoff
Time from last drinkSyndrome
6–24 hTremor, anxiety, insomnia, autonomic activation

12–24 hAlcoholic hallucinosis — visual; sensorium clear

Management:

  • CIWA-Ar–guided diazepam 10–20 mg po (or lorazepam 1–2 mg in hepatic disease).
  • Thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 2–3 days BEFORE glucose, then 250 mg IM/IV daily × 3–5 days, then oral. Multivitamins, magnesium, glucose as needed.
  • Refractory withdrawal / DTs: phenobarbital, propofol, dexmedetomidine in ICU.
  • Long-term: naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram (if motivated and liver intact).
Wernicke encephalopathy: clinical diagnosis, medical emergency.
  • Caine criteria (≥ 2 of 4): nutritional deficiency, oculomotor abnormalities, cerebellar signs, altered mental state.
  • Classic triad (encephalopathy + ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus + ataxia) in only ~16%.
  • Thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 2–3 days (RCP regimen), then 250 mg IV/IM daily × 3–5 days, then high-dose oral.
  • Korsakoff syndrome: residual anterograde amnesia + confabulation in 80% of untreated survivors — largely irreversible.
  • MRI: symmetric T2/FLAIR in mammillary bodies, periaqueductal grey, dorsomedial thalami.
  • Causes beyond alcohol: hyperemesis gravidarum, bariatric surgery, prolonged TPN without thiamine, anorexia, malabsorption, dialysis, refeeding syndrome, GI malignancy.
5. Other chronic alcohol-related neurology
  • Alcoholic cerebellar degeneration — gait > truncal > limb; superior vermian atrophy.
  • Alcoholic polyneuropathy — length-dependent, painful; nutritional + ethanol toxicity.
  • Marchiafava-Bignami — corpus callosum demyelination; malnourished alcoholics.
  • Hepatocerebral degeneration — parkinsonism + chorea + dementia.
  • CPM/ODS — after rapid Na correction (above).
  • Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder — no safe threshold in pregnancy.
  • Nutritional optic neuropathy — bilateral central scotoma; treat with thiamine + B12 + folate + abstinence.

7. Drug-related neurological syndromes
  • Lithium toxicity: tremor + ataxia + dysarthria + confusion + seizures; level > 1.5 mmol/L or chronic with renal impairment. Stop, IV fluids, haemodialysis if severe.
  • Valproate-induced hyperammonaemic encephalopathy: stop VPA; L-carnitine 100 mg/kg IV → 50 mg/kg q6h.
  • Phenytoin toxicity: nystagmus → ataxia → dysarthria → drowsiness ladder.
  • N₂O ("whippets") abuse: functional B12 deficiency → subacute combined degeneration (Module 10). Parenteral B12 + counselling.
  • Methanol: anion-gap acidosis + osmolar gap + optic neuropathy + putaminal necrosis. Fomepizole (or ethanol), folate, dialysis.
  • Ethylene glycol: AG acidosis + osmolar gap + oxalate crystals + renal failure + cranial neuropathies (delayed). Fomepizole, pyridoxine, thiamine, dialysis.
  • Carbon monoxide: 100% O₂; HBO for severe / pregnancy / persistent symptoms. Delayed neurological sequelae weeks later (parkinsonism, cognitive decline, bilateral globus pallidus T2 hyperintensity).
  • Chemotherapy: vincristine/platinum/taxane neuropathy; ifosfamide encephalopathy (methylene blue); methotrexate myelopathy/leukoencephalopathy; immune checkpoint inhibitors (hypophysitis, encephalitis, myositis-myocarditis-myasthenia overlap).

10. Wilson disease

AR; ATP7B mutation; biliary copper excretion impaired. Presents 5–35 years (rarely later). Three presentations: hepatic (incl. acute liver failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis), neurological (dysarthria, wing-beating tremor, dystonia, parkinsonism, ataxia, risus sardonicus), psychiatric (personality change, depression, psychosis).

  • Kayser-Fleischer rings: slit-lamp; > 95% with neurological Wilson.
  • Diagnosis: low ceruloplasmin (< 0.2 g/L) + elevated 24-h urinary copper (> 100 µg/24 h); liver biopsy copper (> 250 µg/g dry weight) + ATP7B mutation analysis. Free serum copper high.
  • MRI: "face of the giant panda" sign in midbrain; basal ganglia and thalamic T2 changes.
  • Treatment: chelators (penicillamine — may transiently worsen neurological symptoms; trientine better tolerated); zinc (induces metallothionein — maintenance + presymptomatic relatives); low-copper diet; liver transplant for fulminant/advanced cirrhosis.
  • Screen all first-degree relatives.
11. Mitochondrial and inherited metabolic (adult presentations)
  • MELAS: stroke-like episodes (often posterior, not respecting vascular territories), seizures, sensorineural hearing loss, migraine, short stature, ragged-red fibres; mtDNA m.3243A>G. Avoid valproate (worsens). L-arginine for acute episodes.
  • MERRF: myoclonic epilepsy + ataxia + myopathy; m.8344A>G.
  • Kearns-Sayre / CPEO: ptosis + PEO + retinopathy + heart block (KSS); large mtDNA deletions.
  • LHON: bilateral subacute optic neuropathy in young men; m.11778/14484/3460; idebenone may help m.14484.
  • Adult leukodystrophies: AMN (X-linked, VLCFA), MLD (ARSA), Krabbe (GALC), CADASIL (Module 2), Alexander, vanishing white matter.
  • Acute intermittent porphyria: abdominal pain + autonomic + neuropathy + psychiatric + hyponatraemia. Triggers: many drugs, fasting, alcohol. Urinary porphobilinogen ↑ during attack. Treat: stop triggers, IV glucose, IV haemin, pain control (avoid barbiturates).
12. When to refer
  • Same day: unexplained acute encephalopathy; suspected NMS / SS / MH; methanol / ethylene glycol / CO poisoning; Na < 120 with seizures or coma; severe withdrawal / DTs.
  • Within 1 week: suspected Wilson disease; mitochondrial disease workup; toxic neuropathy; suspected porphyria.
  • Routine: chronic alcohol-related neurology after stabilisation; vitamin replacement follow-up; chelation; addiction medicine.
  • Always: poisons centre for any non-routine toxic exposure; hepatology for Wilson; clinical genetics for inherited metabolic disorders.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 45-year-old man with chronic alcohol use, found confused with horizontal nystagmus and unable to stand

Last drink unclear; girlfriend reports poor diet and weight loss. Exam: encephalopathy, bilateral lateral-rectus weakness (CN VI), gaze-evoked horizontal nystagmus, broad-based gait, intact strength. Glucose 4.5 mmol/L. ED clinician about to give "B&C cocktail" (glucose + multivitamin).

Diagnosis: Wernicke encephalopathy. Caine criteria met (nutritional deficit + oculomotor + cerebellar + altered mental state — all four).

Plan:

  1. Thiamine 500 mg IV TID for 2–3 days (RCP regimen) — GIVE BEFORE any glucose load.
  2. Then thiamine 250 mg IV/IM daily × 3–5 days → high-dose oral thiamine 100 mg TID long-term.
  3. Multivitamins (folate), magnesium replacement (often co-deficient), correct electrolytes.
  4. CIWA-Ar–guided benzodiazepine for emerging alcohol withdrawal.
  5. MRI brain (mammillary body, periaqueductal grey, thalamic T2/FLAIR hyperintensity) — supportive but not required for diagnosis.
  6. Counsel: untreated Wernicke leaves residual Korsakoff (anterograde amnesia + confabulation) in 80%. Refer to addiction medicine; consider acamprosate or naltrexone after stabilisation.
Giving glucose without thiamine in a thiamine-deficient patient precipitates Wernicke encephalopathy. Always thiamine first.
Case 2 — A 70-year-old woman with cirrhosis develops drowsiness over 24 h after a small GI bleed

Lethargic, asterixis, slurred speech, mild flapping tremor. Recently treated with diuretics and a new sedative for "insomnia". Na 130, K 3.0, INR 1.8, Hb dropped by 2 g/dL after a small upper GI bleed.

Diagnosis: Hepatic encephalopathy (West Haven II), precipitated by GI bleed + hypokalaemia + sedative + alkalosis.

Plan:

  1. Identify and treat each precipitant: stop the sedative, replace K, treat GI bleed, exclude SBP (diagnostic paracentesis if ascites), check for constipation.
  2. Lactulose 30–45 mL po q1–2h until soft stools, then titrate to 2–3 soft stools daily.
  3. Add rifaximin 550 mg po BID for recurrence prevention (this is her second episode).
  4. Avoid further sedatives. Avoid long-term protein restriction.
  5. Don't titrate to ammonia — titrate to mental status and bowel movements.
  6. Liver transplant evaluation if Child-Pugh advanced / refractory.
Case 3 — A 30-year-old with depression has been started on tramadol on top of fluoxetine and now has confusion, hyperreflexia with sustained ankle clonus, dilated pupils, and a temperature of 39.5 °C

Onset over 6 hours. Diaphoretic, agitated, BP 170/100, HR 130. Inducible ankle clonus, ocular clonus visible. Mild GI symptoms.

Diagnosis: Serotonin syndrome (Hunter criteria — spontaneous or inducible clonus + agitation/diaphoresis or ocular clonus + tremor/hyperreflexia).

Plan:

  1. Stop tramadol, fluoxetine, and any other serotonergic agent (linezolid, MAOI, lithium, triptans, MDMA, St John's wort).
  2. Aggressive external cooling; IV fluids; benzodiazepines (lorazepam or diazepam) titrated to control agitation, clonus, and autonomic surge.
  3. Cyproheptadine 12 mg po (or via NG) loading, then 2 mg q2h up to 32 mg/day (5-HT2A antagonist).
  4. Avoid bromocriptine (worsens by stimulating serotonin).
  5. Avoid antipyretics — fever is from muscle activity, not central; benzodiazepines treat it indirectly.
  6. Severe cases: ICU, paralytic + intubation; consider non-depolarising NMB if rigidity is causing rhabdomyolysis / hyperthermia.
  7. Counsel about future medication safety; document allergy/serotonergic combination interaction.
Case 4 — A 78-year-old with Parkinson disease is admitted for pneumonia; an evening RN gives metoclopramide for nausea. 5 days later he is rigid, hyperthermic, and confused

Lead-pipe rigidity throughout, hyperthermia 40.5 °C, bradyreflexic, autonomic instability with labile BP, encephalopathy. CK 8500 U/L, WBC 18,000.

Diagnosis: Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) from metoclopramide + possible interruption of his PD medication.

Plan:

  1. Stop metoclopramide. Restart and optimise PD medication.
  2. Aggressive external cooling; IV fluids (large volume, watch CK and renal function — rhabdomyolysis common).
  3. Dantrolene 1–2.5 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg/kg/day) for severe rigidity and hyperthermia.
  4. Bromocriptine 2.5–5 mg po TID or amantadine for dopaminergic support.
  5. ICU; benzodiazepines for agitation; avoid further dopamine antagonists.
  6. ECT considered in refractory cases.
  7. Counsel: future avoidance of metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, typical antipsychotics in PD; cross-ref Module 6.
Case 5 — A 24-year-old man with a vegan diet has used nitrous oxide ("whippets") recreationally for 6 months; now has progressive bilateral leg paraesthesiae, gait ataxia, and a wide-based gait

Exam: reduced vibration and proprioception to mid-thigh, Romberg positive, mildly brisk knee reflexes with absent ankle reflexes (combined upper-and-lower-motor-neuron pattern), normal mentation. B12 250 pg/mL, MCV 102, homocysteine markedly elevated, methylmalonic acid elevated.

Diagnosis: Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord from functional B12 deficiency (N₂O oxidises cobalamin) + dietary contribution.

Plan:

  1. Hydroxocobalamin 1000 µg IM daily × 5 days, then weekly × 4, then monthly for life (or until N₂O abuse stopped and dietary issue corrected).
  2. Stop N₂O abuse and counsel about ongoing risk; refer to addiction medicine.
  3. Folate replacement only AFTER B12 started (folate alone in B12 deficiency can precipitate myelopathy).
  4. Dietary counselling: B12 fortified foods, supplements; vegans require oral B12 supplementation.
  5. MRI spinal cord: T2 hyperintensity of posterior columns ("inverted V" sign).
  6. Cross-ref Module 10 — recovery depends on duration of deficiency; partial improvement possible over months.
Case 6 — A 22-year-old presents with subacute behavioural change, dysarthria, and a tremor that worsens when the arms are held outstretched

6-month history. Recent psychiatric assessment for "depression". Exam: prominent wing-beating proximal-arm tremor, dysarthria, drooling, mild parkinsonism, mild dystonic posturing. Family: father died of liver failure in his 30s. Slit-lamp: bilateral Kayser-Fleischer rings.

Diagnosis: Wilson disease (combined neuropsychiatric + family history of liver disease + KF rings).

Plan:

  1. Confirm: ceruloplasmin (low, < 0.2 g/L), 24-h urinary copper (> 100 µg/24 h), serum free copper, LFTs, AST:ALT ratio (often > 2 in Wilson), liver biopsy with tissue copper if needed, ATP7B mutation analysis.
  2. MRI brain: look for "face of the giant panda" midbrain sign, basal ganglia and thalamic T2 changes.
  3. Treatment: trientine (preferred over penicillamine for neurological presentations to avoid initial worsening) + zinc; low-copper diet (avoid liver, shellfish, nuts, chocolate, mushrooms).
  4. Counsel about the gradual response (months); ongoing monitoring of 24-h urinary copper, ceruloplasmin, LFTs.
  5. Liver transplant for fulminant or advanced cirrhosis.
  6. Screen all first-degree relatives (clinical, biochemistry, mutation analysis).
  7. Multidisciplinary: hepatology + clinical genetics + neurology + psychiatry.
