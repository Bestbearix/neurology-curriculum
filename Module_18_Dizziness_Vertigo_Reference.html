1. Framework — replace "type of dizziness" with timing & triggers

The traditional teaching — "ask the patient what they mean by dizzy" — is unreliable. Patients describe the same experience in different ways at different times; the symptom quality (spinning vs lightheaded vs unsteady) does not separate causes. The modern bedside approach (TiTrATE, Newman-Toker 2015) instead asks when and what triggers the dizziness, and then uses targeted examination to confirm.

Vestibular syndromeTimingTriggersExamples
Acute Vestibular Syndrome (AVS)Continuous, days–weeks, dawning over hoursSpontaneous (no trigger needed)Vestibular neuritis; labyrinthitis; posterior-circulation stroke (cerebellar or lateral medullary); MS
Episodic Vestibular Syndrome — Triggered (t-EVS)Seconds to minutes, recurrentSpecific trigger — head position change, Valsalva, loud soundBPPV (commonest); orthostatic hypotension (Module 15); SCDS
Episodic Vestibular Syndrome — Spontaneous (s-EVS)Minutes to hours, recurrentNo identifiable triggerVestibular migraine; Ménière disease; TIA; panic / arrhythmia
Chronic Vestibular SyndromeMonths, often constantWorse upright, with motion, in busy visual environmentsPPPD; bilateral vestibulopathy; chronic central disorders
The single principle: stop asking "what kind of dizzy?" and start asking "how long does each episode last and what makes it happen?" The answer puts the patient in one of four syndrome boxes — and each box has a defined differential and a specific examination.

2. Vestibular anatomy and physiology

Peripheral apparatus

  • Three semicircular canals on each side (anterior/superior, posterior, horizontal/lateral) — sense angular acceleration. Activated by endolymph flow deflecting the cupula in the ampulla.
  • Otolith organs — utricle (horizontal acceleration + head tilt relative to gravity) and saccule (vertical acceleration).
  • Both produce afferent signals via the vestibular nerve (superior division innervates anterior + horizontal canals and utricle; inferior division innervates posterior canal and saccule) → enters brainstem at pontomedullary junction → vestibular nuclei.

Central pathways and outputs

  • Vestibular nuclei → cerebellar flocculus and nodulus (gain adjustment), thalamus → parieto-insular vestibular cortex (PIVC), oculomotor nuclei (vestibulo-ocular reflex, VOR), spinal cord (vestibulospinal reflex — posture).
  • Vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR): a head movement → opposite-direction eye movement that keeps gaze stable. Latency ~10 ms. A unilateral peripheral lesion → corrective saccade on the affected side during head-impulse test. Central lesions usually spare the VOR.
  • Nystagmus is named by its fast phase. Peripheral vestibular lesions cause horizontal-torsional nystagmus that obeys Alexander's law (worse when looking in the direction of the fast phase) and is suppressed by visual fixation.

3. TiTrATE — the modern bedside approach

Step 1 — Timing and Triggers

  • Onset — sudden or gradual? Duration of each episode? Constant or episodic?
  • What provokes it (head movement, standing, sound, stress, busy visual environments, nothing)?
  • Associated features — hearing change, tinnitus, aural fullness, headache, photophobia/phonophobia, visual symptoms, dysarthria, dysphagia, diplopia, limb ataxia, weakness, sensory loss.
  • Vascular risk factors, prior episodes, medications (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, salicylates → ototoxicity; antihypertensives, sedatives → falls).

Step 2 — Place into a syndrome (see Section 1 table)

Once you have one of the four vestibular syndromes, the differential is short and the bedside examination is targeted (see next section).

4. Targeted bedside examination

For acute vestibular syndrome (continuous dizziness lasting days)

HINTS exam — performed in AVS patients (continuous spontaneous dizziness with nystagmus). When done correctly, HINTS+ is more sensitive than early MRI DWI for posterior-circulation stroke (sensitivity ~99% vs ~80%; Kattah Stroke 2009; Newman-Toker NEJM 2018).
TestPeripheral findingCentral / stroke finding
Head ImpulseAbnormal (corrective saccade on affected side)Normal (intact) — counter-intuitive
NystagmusUnidirectional horizontal-torsional, fast phase in one direction regardless of gazeBidirectional / direction-changing on gaze, vertical, or torsional
Test of Skew (alternate cover)No vertical skew deviationVertical skew deviation present

HINTS+ adds hearing — new unilateral sensorineural hearing loss in AVS suggests AICA stroke (anterior inferior cerebellar artery infarcts often involve the internal auditory artery + lateral pons + cerebellar peduncle).

Common pitfalls: HINTS is only valid in continuous, ongoing dizziness with nystagmus. It is NOT useful in patients who are not actively dizzy at exam, or in episodic dizziness (e.g., BPPV). Confusion arises when clinicians do HINTS on patients without AVS. Also, performing head impulse poorly (small low-velocity head turns) gives false-negative "intact VOR" in peripheral lesions.

For triggered episodic dizziness (BPPV)

  • Dix-Hallpike test — diagnostic for posterior canal BPPV. Patient seated, head turned 45° to side being tested; rapidly lower to head-hanging position 20–30° below horizontal. Watch eyes for ≥ 30 seconds. Positive: upbeat-torsional nystagmus with 5–20 second latency, lasting < 60 seconds, with reversal on sitting up, and fatigues on repeat.
  • Supine roll test (Pagnini-McClure) — for horizontal canal BPPV. Supine, rapidly turn head 90° each side. Geotropic (toward ground) or apogeotropic (away from ground) horizontal nystagmus.

Bedside vestibular and gait exam

  • Spontaneous nystagmus (with and without fixation — Frenzel glasses or simply +20 dioptre lenses; smartphone close-up flashlight can help).
  • Smooth pursuit, saccades, gaze-evoked nystagmus.
  • Head impulse test.
  • Dynamic visual acuity (read Snellen → shake head → ≥ 2 line drop suggests VOR loss).
  • Head-shaking nystagmus (vigorous head shake at 1–2 Hz then observe — vigorous post-shake nystagmus suggests vestibular asymmetry).
  • Romberg, tandem gait, Fukuda step test.
  • Cerebellar exam (heel-to-shin, finger-to-nose), cranial nerves, motor/sensory/reflex full exam to detect brainstem signs.
  • Orthostatic vitals (Module 15) — common cause of "lightheadedness" mistaken for vertigo.

5. Acute vestibular syndrome — vestibular neuritis vs stroke

Vestibular neuritis (VN)

  • Continuous vertigo, nausea/vomiting, gait imbalance for days, peaking within 24 hours, slowly resolving over 1–6 weeks; thought to be post-viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve (HSV-1 reactivation in some).
  • Often involves only the superior division (sparing posterior canal and saccule). Hearing usually preserved (vs labyrinthitis where hearing is also lost).
  • Exam: horizontal-torsional nystagmus with fast phase away from affected side, suppressed by fixation, obeys Alexander's law; abnormal head impulse on affected side; normal HINTS+ otherwise.
  • Treatment: short course (≤ 3 days) of antihistamines (cyclizine, meclizine, dimenhydrinate) or benzodiazepines for symptom relief in the acute phase. Prolonged use delays central compensation — stop within 3 days. Antiemetics (ondansetron, prochlorperazine — caveat extrapyramidal effects, Module 6).
  • Corticosteroids — methylprednisolone 100 mg/day tapered over 22 days within 3 days of onset improves caloric recovery (Strupp NEJM 2004); modest evidence; routine use varies by guideline.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation therapy — most important intervention long-term; gaze stabilisation, habituation, balance exercises; start as soon as symptoms allow.

Labyrinthitis

  • Vestibular neuritis + cochlear involvement → vertigo + sensorineural hearing loss + tinnitus.
  • Distinguish from AICA stroke (acute hearing loss with vertigo and central HINTS findings — image urgently).
  • Treatment as for VN; for suspected viral labyrinthitis, oral aciclovir is reasonable; for suppurative labyrinthitis (from otitis media or meningitis), IV antibiotics + ENT.
Posterior-circulation stroke in AVS:
  • ~25% of patients presenting to the ED with AVS have a posterior-circulation stroke — typically cerebellar (PICA territory) or lateral medullary (Wallenberg, PICA or vertebral).
  • Lateral medullary syndrome: vertigo + ipsilateral facial sensory loss + Horner + ipsilateral cerebellar signs + contralateral body pain/temperature loss + dysphagia + hoarseness (cross-ref Module 2).
  • AICA: vertigo + sudden unilateral SNHL + facial weakness + cerebellar signs + ipsilateral Horner.
  • Early MRI DWI misses ~10–20% of small brainstem/cerebellar strokes in the first 24–48 hours — HINTS+ is more sensitive than early MRI in trained hands.
  • Image with MRI/MRA (CT often misses posterior fossa strokes; CT angiography is helpful for vertebral / basilar stenosis or dissection).

6. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

  • Most common cause of vertigo overall. Lifetime prevalence ~2.4%.
  • Otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) detach from utricle and enter a semicircular canal — most commonly the posterior canal (~85%), less often horizontal canal, rarely anterior.
  • Two mechanisms: canalithiasis (free-floating debris — common, shorter nystagmus duration) and cupulolithiasis (debris adherent to cupula — longer nystagmus).
  • Triggers: rolling over in bed, looking up, bending forward, lying down. Brief episodes (seconds to < 1 minute).
  • Risk factors: age, female sex, head trauma, recent inner-ear disease, prolonged supine positioning.

Diagnosis (Bárány Society 2015 criteria)

  • Posterior canal BPPV: positive Dix-Hallpike — upbeat-torsional nystagmus (top of eye torts toward affected/down-side ear) with 5–20 s latency, lasting < 60 s, reverses on sitting up, fatigues on repeat.
  • Horizontal canal BPPV: supine roll test — geotropic (commoner — canalithiasis; affected side gives more intense nystagmus) or apogeotropic (cupulolithiasis or canalithiasis in anterior arm; less intense nystagmus on affected side).

Treatment — repositioning manoeuvres

BPPV typeManoeuvre
Posterior canal (commonest)Epley manoeuvre — Dix-Hallpike position → rotate head 90° to opposite side → roll body onto that side (head ends nose-down) → sit up; hold each position 30–60 s. 80–90% effective after 1–2 attempts. Semont liberatory manoeuvre is an alternative.
Horizontal canal — geotropicBBQ roll (Lempert) — 360° roll in 90° increments toward unaffected side. Gufoni manoeuvre is an alternative; lie on the unaffected side then rapidly rotate head toward floor for 1 minute.
Horizontal canal — apogeotropicFirst convert to geotropic (Gufoni for apogeotropic, or head-shake), then BBQ roll. Forced prolonged position (sleep on affected side, then nudge crystals back into utricle).
Anterior canal (rare)Deep head-hanging or reverse Epley (less standardised).
  • Recurrence: ~30% at 1 year, ~50% at 5 years. Educate patient that recurrence is normal and re-treatment is effective; can teach home-modified Epley.
  • Brandt-Daroff exercises — habituation for persistent dizziness after canalith repositioning; less effective than Epley for active BPPV but useful for residual disequilibrium.
  • Avoid vestibular suppressants (antihistamines, benzodiazepines) — they do not treat BPPV and delay compensation.
After successful Epley, mild lingering disequilibrium ("brain fog") is common for hours to days; reassure and recommend gradual return to activity. Recurrent or atypical BPPV warrants ENT/neuro-otology referral — secondary causes include head injury, vestibular neuritis, Ménière, vascular insults to inner ear.

7. Ménière disease

  • Idiopathic endolymphatic hydrops — episodic vertigo + fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss + tinnitus + aural fullness in the affected ear. Onset 40s–60s, gradually progressive over years.
  • Vertigo episodes typically last 20 minutes to 12 hours (often 2–4 h); accompanied by severe nausea/vomiting.

Bárány Society 2015 criteria — definite Ménière disease

  1. ≥ 2 spontaneous episodes of vertigo, each lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours.
  2. Audiometrically documented low- to medium-frequency SNHL in the affected ear on at least one occasion before, during, or after a vertigo episode.
  3. Fluctuating aural symptoms (hearing, tinnitus, fullness) in the affected ear.
  4. Not better accounted for by another vestibular diagnosis.

Management

  • Lifestyle: low-salt diet (< 2 g/day), reduced caffeine and alcohol, adequate sleep, stress reduction.
  • Diuretics: hydrochlorothiazide (often combined with triamterene) — modest evidence; widely used first-line.
  • Acute attacks: vestibular suppressant + antiemetic; rest; treat dehydration.
  • Betahistine 16–48 mg/day — widely used in Europe and Asia, weak RCT evidence (mainly historical Cochrane mixed); not approved in US.
  • If frequent disabling vertigo despite conservative management: intratympanic gentamicin (chemical labyrinthectomy — controls vertigo at cost of further hearing loss) or intratympanic steroid (preserves hearing better; growing evidence).
  • Endolymphatic sac decompression / shunt surgery — controversial.
  • Surgical labyrinthectomy or vestibular neurectomy — for refractory unilateral disease with non-serviceable hearing.
  • Hearing aids for hearing loss; cochlear implant for severe bilateral cases.

8. Vestibular migraine

  • One of the commonest causes of recurrent spontaneous vertigo (likely under-diagnosed).
  • Vertigo episodes lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours, with migraine features (headache, photophobia/phonophobia, visual aura) at least 50% of the time.

Bárány Society / IHS 2012 criteria — vestibular migraine

  1. ≥ 5 episodes of vestibular symptoms (vertigo, head-motion intolerance, positional vertigo, visually induced vertigo) lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours.
  2. Current or past history of migraine (with or without aura) per ICHD-3.
  3. ≥ 1 migraine feature with ≥ 50% of vestibular episodes: headache (unilateral, pulsating, moderate-severe, aggravated by routine activity); photophobia and phonophobia; visual aura.
  4. Not better accounted for by another vestibular or ICHD-3 diagnosis.

Management

  • Same as migraine (cross-ref Module 4): trigger identification, sleep, hydration, regular meals.
  • Acute: triptans (less robust evidence for vertigo than headache), antiemetics, vestibular suppressant short course for severe attacks.
  • Prevention: same agents as migraine — topiramate, propranolol or metoprolol, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, flunarizine, valproate; emerging CGRP mAb data; lifestyle.
  • Vestibular rehabilitation for residual imbalance between attacks.

9. Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD)

  • The most common cause of chronic vestibular symptoms in adults (Bárány Society 2017).
  • Persistent (≥ 3 months) sensation of non-spinning dizziness, unsteadiness, or non-vertiginous vertigo, present most days, that is exacerbated by:
    • Upright posture.
    • Active or passive motion (independent of direction or position).
    • Exposure to moving visual stimuli or complex visual patterns (supermarket aisles, scrolling screens).
  • Often triggered by an acute vestibular event (vestibular neuritis, BPPV, vestibular migraine, panic attack, post-concussion); the pre-existing event has resolved, but the patient remains dizzy.
  • Driven by maladaptive postural control and visual dependence; not psychogenic — it is a functional disorder of the vestibular system.

Management

  • Vestibular rehabilitation — gradual exposure, habituation; the foundation.
  • SSRI or SNRI (sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine) — best pharmacological evidence; start low, increase slowly.
  • CBT targeted at fear-avoidance and visual dependence.
  • Avoid prolonged vestibular suppressants (delay habituation).
  • Educate patient — PPPD is a recognised, treatable disorder, not "all in your head".

10. Bilateral vestibulopathy & ototoxicity

  • Loss of vestibular function bilaterally → oscillopsia (bouncing visual scene) with head movement, walking; profound imbalance especially in the dark; no vertigo.
  • Bedside: bilaterally abnormal head impulse, abnormal dynamic visual acuity (drops ≥ 2 Snellen lines).
  • Causes: aminoglycoside ototoxicity (gentamicin especially — even normal doses can cause irreversible vestibulotoxicity in susceptible patients); cisplatin; idiopathic bilateral vestibulopathy; autoimmune (Cogan syndrome — sudden bilateral vestibulocochlear deficits + interstitial keratitis); meningitis; superficial siderosis.
  • Treatment: stop ototoxic drug; vestibular rehabilitation; counsel — outcome variable, complete recovery rare.

11. Superior canal dehiscence syndrome & perilymph fistula

  • Superior canal dehiscence syndrome (SCDS, Minor syndrome): thin or absent bone over superior canal → "third window" allowing sound and pressure to deflect the cupula.
  • Symptoms: vertigo and oscillopsia triggered by loud sound (Tullio phenomenon) or by Valsalva / pressure change in ear or middle ear (Hennebert sign); autophony (hearing own voice loudly); hearing own footsteps, pulse, eye movements; conductive hearing loss with normal middle ear.
  • Diagnosis: characteristic history + high-resolution CT temporal bone (Pöschl plane); VEMP (vestibular evoked myogenic potentials) with low thresholds.
  • Treatment: avoid triggers; surgical canal plugging or resurfacing for disabling cases.
  • Perilymph fistula: communication between perilymph space and middle ear → fluctuating vertigo and hearing loss after barotrauma, head injury, or stapes surgery; bed rest, surgical repair for selected.

12. Central vertigo — brainstem and cerebellar causes

  • Central vertigo usually has accompanying brainstem or cerebellar signs (dysarthria, diplopia, ataxia, dysphagia, sensory or motor deficits, Horner) and central nystagmus features.
  • Causes:
    • Stroke — PICA / lateral medullary (Wallenberg), AICA, basilar (Module 2).
    • Cerebellar haemorrhage (sudden severe occipital headache + vomiting + ataxia + reduced consciousness — neurosurgical emergency).
    • Vertebral artery dissection (post-trauma, post-manipulation; severe neck/head pain; cross-ref Module 2).
    • Multiple sclerosis (brainstem plaques causing nystagmus, INO, vertigo — Module 8).
    • Tumour (cerebellopontine angle — vestibular schwannoma in Module 12; medulloblastoma in children — Module 16).
    • Chiari I malformation (cough-induced headache and vertigo — Module 16).
    • Episodic ataxia type 2 (CACNA1A, acetazolamide-responsive); spinocerebellar ataxias (Module 6).
    • Wernicke encephalopathy (cross-ref Module 13 — give thiamine before glucose).
    • Drugs — anticonvulsants (especially phenytoin, carbamazepine — Module 13), antipsychotics, alcohol, lithium.

13. Disequilibrium, gait disorders, and presyncope

  • Disequilibrium without vertigo: think bilateral vestibulopathy, peripheral neuropathy (Module 9), cerebellar disease (Module 6), spasticity, parkinsonism, NPH (Module 7), severe visual impairment, deconditioning, multisensory deficit of the elderly.
  • Approach: thorough neurological exam + targeted workup (B12 — Module 13; HbA1c; TFTs; gait analysis; consider DAT scan for parkinsonism, MRI for NPH).
  • Presyncope (lightheaded as if about to faint) — think orthostatic hypotension, cardiac (arrhythmia, AS), reflex syncope, drugs; cross-ref Module 15. Not vestibular.
  • Falls in older adults — multifactorial: medications, vision, vestibular function, neuropathy, cognition, environment. Multidisciplinary falls clinic; deprescribing; exercise (Otago programme); home safety.

14. When to refer

  • Same day: suspected posterior-circulation stroke (AVS with central HINTS findings, inability to walk unaided, sudden hearing loss with vertigo); cerebellar haemorrhage; sudden severe vertigo with cranial nerve or motor/sensory deficits; sudden hearing loss (otology emergency — within 72 h for steroids); suspected vertebral artery dissection.
  • Within 1 week: refractory BPPV after 2 manoeuvre attempts; suspected Ménière disease for audiology and ENT review; suspected vestibular migraine; suspected SCDS for imaging; chronic dizziness with red flags.
  • Routine: PPPD for vestibular rehabilitation + SSRI initiation; established VM on prophylaxis; bilateral vestibulopathy for vestibular rehab.
  • Always involve: ENT / neuro-otology, audiology, vestibular physiotherapy, neurology when central features or atypical presentations, falls clinic for elderly with recurrent imbalance.
