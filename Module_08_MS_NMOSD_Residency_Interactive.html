Three diseases that look alike and are treated differently

The general-physician Module 8 teaches recognition and referral. This version assumes you apply current criteria, know why an MS drug can harm an NMOSD patient, interpret antibody titres critically, and run the autoimmune-encephalitis workup.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Diagnose MS using the 2024 McDonald criteria — and articulate the overdiagnosis critique.
  2. Select and monitor disease-modifying therapy by efficacy tier and risk.
  3. Recognise NMOSD and prescribe from the approved biologic classes.
  4. Apply the 2023 MOGAD criteria and resist over-reading low-positive MOG-IgG.
  5. Identify the major antibody syndromes and treat autoimmune encephalitis early.
  6. Work up paraneoplastic disease, CNS vasculitis, and neurosarcoidosis.
Educational use only. Verify criteria, drugs, and monitoring against current guidelines and product labeling at the point of care.

MS diagnosis & the 2024 McDonald criteria

Demonstrate dissemination in space (DIS) and time (DIT), excluding better explanations. Phenotypes: relapsing-remitting, secondary progressive, primary progressive.

2024 revisions. (1) Optic nerve added as a fifth DIS site (with periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord); (2) DIT no longer required in selected circumstances; (3) new paraclinical markers — central vein sign, paramagnetic rim lesions, and the CSF kappa free light chain index as an alternative to CSF-specific oligoclonal bands.
Real-world effect. In a 306-patient multicentre Korean cohort, the 2024 criteria diagnosed MS earlier and in more patients than 2017 — mainly via the optic-nerve DIS site (~6.7% additional).
Overdiagnosis caveat. Critiques warn that relaxing DIT and broadening substitutions reduce specificity and invite confirmation bias — especially if applied to nonspecific symptoms or incidental white-matter findings/RIS. The criteria assume a typical demyelinating presentation.
Red flags against MS: normal MRI, LETM, severe bilateral optic neuritis, area postrema syndrome, meningeal enhancement, prominent systemic features, progressive myelopathy without dissemination.

MRI standards

Follow standardised protocols (German neuroradiology consensus): consistent sequences/geometry, appropriate gadolinium, reproducible intervals so new/enlarging T2 lesions are reliably detected. Typical lesions: ovoid, periventricular (Dawson fingers), juxtacortical/cortical, infratentorial, short-segment cord.

MS therapy

Acute relapse

High-dose corticosteroids speed recovery (no long-term disability change); plasma exchange for severe steroid-refractory attacks. Exclude pseudo-relapse (infection/fever, Uhthoff) first.

Before any DMT: confirm it's not a mimic, check antibodies if atypical, screen HBV/TB/JCV, update vaccines, plan pregnancy.

NMOSD & MOGAD

NMOSD (AQP4-IgG astrocytopathy)

Core syndromes: severe/bilateral optic neuritis, LETM (≥3 segments), area postrema syndrome (intractable hiccups/nausea/vomiting), acute brainstem, diencephalic, cerebral. Disability is attack-related → prevention is everything. Test AQP4-IgG by cell-based assay.

Approved biologics. Complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab), anti-IL-6R (satralizumab, tocilizumab), anti-CD19 (inebilizumab), plus rituximab and older agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate) — relapse-risk reductions on the order of 60–90% in trial programmes.
Two hard rules: complement inhibitors require meningococcal vaccination; and MS drugs (interferon-β, natalizumab) can severely worsen NMOSD — get antibody status first.

MOGAD

Bilateral optic neuritis with marked disc oedema, myelitis (often longitudinally extensive, conus), ADEM-like presentations, cortical encephalitis with seizures. Steroid-responsive but relapses on withdrawal.

MOG-IgG positivity ≠ MOGAD. Applying the 2023 international criteria at a referral centre, only ~68% of patients labelled MOGAD actually met criteria — misapplication from testing without a compatible phenotype and over-reading low-positive titres. Require a compatible syndrome + clear-positive cell-based assay (or supporting features if low-positive).

ADEM

Monophasic post-infectious demyelination of childhood; encephalopathy + multifocal large poorly demarcated lesions is the discriminator from a first MS attack. Many are MOG-IgG positive. Steroids first; IVIG/PLEX if refractory.

Autoimmune encephalitis & antibody syndromes

Recognise the syndrome → test serum AND CSF (CSF essential for NMDA-R) → image/EEG → hunt for a tumour → treat early and empirically.

NMDA-R treatment (provided cohort). First-line = steroids + IVIG or plasma exchange plus tumour removal; escalate to rituximab/cyclophosphamide in non-responders. Early treatment and tumour removal predict better outcome; recovery continues over many months.
Post-HSV autoimmune encephalitis: new movement disorder/psychiatric decline weeks after HSE with negative repeat HSV PCR → test NMDA-R and give immunotherapy, not more acyclovir.
Normal MRI doesn't exclude it — MRI is frequently normal in NMDA-R encephalitis; EEG may show extreme delta brush.

Paraneoplastic, CNS vasculitis & neurosarcoidosis

PNS-CARE (2021). Combines phenotype risk + antibody risk + cancer presence into a score; ≥6 = probable/definite paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome. Validated well in the provided cohort, though some convincing cases scored ≤5 — judgement still required. Classic phenotypes: rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome, limbic encephalitis, opsoclonus-myoclonus, sensory neuronopathy.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are a growing cause of antibody-positive neurologic complications — recognise, stop the drug, immunosuppress.

Primary CNS vasculitis: headache, cognitive change, multi-territory strokes, seizures; angiography may be normal in small-vessel disease — biopsy is the gold standard. Distinguish from RCVS (thunderclap, reversible string-of-beads) — opposite treatments.

Neurosarcoidosis: cranial neuropathies (especially facial), basal leptomeningeal enhancement, hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction, myelopathy. Steroids → methotrexate/mycophenolate → infliximab if refractory.

The neuroimmunology habit: Is there an antibody? Is there a tumour? Is there a systemic disease?

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A 34-year-old woman has severe bilateral optic neuritis and a cord lesion spanning T4–T8, with intractable hiccups for a week beforehand.
Diagnosis, test, and the therapy trap?

NMOSD — LETM + severe bilateral optic neuritis + area postrema syndrome. Send AQP4-IgG by cell-based assay. Treat the attack (steroids ± plasma exchange) and start a preventive biologic (eculizumab/ravulizumab, satralizumab, or inebilizumab; rituximab an option). Trap: do not start interferon-β or natalizumab — MS drugs can worsen NMOSD.

Case 2. A 24-year-old develops psychiatric symptoms, then seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, and autonomic instability. MRI brain is normal.
Diagnosis and management?

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis — a normal MRI is common. Send CSF NMDA-R antibodies (CSF essential), EEG (extreme delta brush), and screen for ovarian teratoma. Give first-line immunotherapy (steroids + IVIG or PLEX) with tumour removal; escalate to rituximab/cyclophosphamide if no response. Recovery is often slow but substantial.

Case 3. A patient with chronic headache and incidental scattered white-matter T2 lesions is referred with "possible MS." No prior clinical attack; optic nerves normal.
Do the 2024 McDonald criteria apply?

No — this is where the criteria are misused. The 2024 McDonald criteria are validated for patients with a typical demyelinating presentation. Applying them to nonspecific symptoms or incidental findings risks overdiagnosis. Consider radiologically isolated syndrome, pursue mimics (migraine, vascular, systemic inflammatory), and follow clinically/radiologically rather than labelling MS.
