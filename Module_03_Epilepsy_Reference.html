1 · Definitions — what is a seizure and what is epilepsy?

A seizure is a transient clinical event caused by abnormally excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. Not every seizure means epilepsy.

Epilepsy (ILAE 2014, operational definition) requires any one of:

  1. ≥ 2 unprovoked seizures > 24 h apart, or
  2. 1 unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk ≥ 60% over 10 years (e.g. clear epileptiform EEG, structural lesion on imaging, focal onset, nocturnal onset), or
  3. Diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.

Acute symptomatic (provoked) seizures occur in the setting of an immediate brain insult (stroke, head injury, CNS infection, metabolic derangement, drug exposure) and usually do not recur once the cause resolves. Recurrence risk after a single unprovoked seizure is ~21–45% in the first 2 years, with the strongest predictors being an abnormal MRI, an epileptiform EEG, a nocturnal first event, and a prior brain insult.

Key clinical distinction: a provoked seizure does not automatically equal epilepsy. Correct the provocation; AEDs are usually not indicated long-term.

2 · Classifying the seizure — ILAE 2017

The current scheme classifies seizures by three features in order: (1) onset, (2) awareness, (3) motor features. The older terms (simple partial, complex partial, secondarily generalised) have been retired.

OnsetAwarenessExamples
FocalFocal aware (was "simple partial")Jacksonian motor march, déjà vu, epigastric rising sensation, olfactory hallucination.
FocalFocal impaired awareness (was "complex partial")Behavioural arrest, lip smacking, manual automatisms, postictal confusion. 70–80% temporal lobe origin.
FocalEither, evolves to bilateralFocal to bilateral tonic-clonic (was "secondarily generalised").

Unknown onsetInsufficient informationOften tonic-clonic but no witnessed onset.

Practical features to clarify in the history

  • Aura — the aura is a focal seizure. The character of the aura localises:
    • Epigastric rising / déjà vu / olfactory → mesial temporal.
    • Fear, rising sensation → temporal/amygdala.
    • Visual (coloured circles, scintillations) → occipital.
    • Somatosensory tingling march → contralateral postcentral gyrus.
    • Forced version of head/eyes → contralateral frontal eye field.
  • Automatisms = coordinated repetitive purposeless movements (lip-smacking, fumbling, picking) — classic of temporal-lobe seizures.
  • Postictal Todd's paralysis — focal weakness for minutes–hours after the seizure, lateralising to the contralateral cortex.
  • Tongue biting on the lateral tongue strongly suggests epileptic GTC; central tongue biting is more often psychogenic.
  • Postictal confusion distinguishes seizure from syncope (recovery usually under a minute) and from psychogenic non-epileptic events.

Generalised tonic-clonic seizure — the classic picture

Abrupt LOC + tonic phase (rigid extension, expiratory cry, cyanosis, ~10–30 s) → clonic phase (rhythmic synchronous jerking, ~1–2 min) → postictal lethargy and confusion. Urinary incontinence common, tongue biting lateral. Compare with non-epileptic events below.

3 · Epilepsy syndromes to recognise

An epilepsy syndrome integrates seizure types, age of onset, EEG pattern, imaging, comorbidities, prognosis, and AED responsiveness. Identifying a syndrome guides drug choice.

Idiopathic generalised epilepsies (IGE)

SyndromeAge onsetSeizure typesEEG / Notes
Childhood absence epilepsy4–12 yAbsence; ~30–50% develop GTCStereotyped 3-Hz generalised spike-wave. Often remits by adolescence. Ethosuximide or valproate; lamotrigine if GTC.
Juvenile absence epilepsy10–13 yAbsence + GTC + sometimes myoclonusLess likely to remit; needs lifelong AED.
Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME)8 y – mid-20sMorning myoclonic jerks, GTC on awakening, rare absences4–6 Hz polyspike-and-wave. Lifelong AED. Avoid sodium-channel blockers (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin) — they worsen myoclonus.
GTC alone (formerly "on awakening")AdolescenceGTC onlyTriggered by sleep deprivation, alcohol, photic stimulation.

Focal epilepsies

SyndromeFeatures
Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (MTLE)The most common adult focal epilepsy. Often history of febrile seizures or hippocampal sclerosis on MRI. Aura of epigastric rising, déjà vu, fear; oral automatisms; postictal confusion. Up to 80% become seizure-free after temporal lobectomy if drug-resistant.
Frontal lobe epilepsyBrief, abrupt, nocturnal cluster seizures with bizarre motor features (bicycling, pelvic thrust, vocalisations). Scalp EEG often non-diagnostic. Easily confused with non-epileptic spells.
Occipital lobe epilepsyVisual aura (coloured circles, lights), can spread to clonic activity.
Benign Rolandic (centrotemporal spikes)Children 4–13 y; nocturnal hemifacial twitching, drooling, speech arrest. Self-limited by adolescence. Often no treatment needed.

Combined / epileptic encephalopathies (recognise to refer)

Lennox-Gastaut syndrome: intellectual disability, multiple seizure types including atonic drops, slow spike-wave on EEG. Often refractory. Dravet syndrome: SCN1A mutation, fever-sensitive early-life seizures evolving into intractable epilepsy. West syndrome (infantile spasms): clusters of flexor spasms, hypsarrhythmia on EEG, developmental regression — neurological emergency, treat with ACTH or vigabatrin.

4 · Differentiating seizures from mimics

DiagnosisDistinguishing features
SyncopeBrief LOC (seconds), preceded by lightheadedness/visual greying, pallor, rapid recovery. Brief asynchronous myoclonic jerks (convulsive syncope) common and misleading. Postictal confusion absent.
Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES)Eyes closed during event, side-to-side head movement, asynchronous limb thrashing, waxing-and-waning, longer duration (often > 2 min), pelvic thrusting, preserved awareness despite "GTC-like" movements, frequent emotional trigger, recall of event. Diagnosis confirmed by video-EEG.
TIANegative symptoms (loss of function) rather than positive; usually no LOC; rapid maximal deficit. Limb-shaking TIA from severe carotid stenosis is a rare exception.
Migraine with auraSlow spreading positive visual phenomena over minutes (scintillations crossing the field) → followed by headache. Seizure auras are stereotyped, faster, and brief.
Movement disorder paroxysmDystonia, paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia — preserved awareness, characteristic triggers.
Sleep eventsRBD, parasomnias, periodic limb movements.
Transient global amnesiaPure anterograde amnesia for hours, no LOC, no automatisms.
Tongue biting and shoulder dislocation have positive predictive value for GTC. Eyes closed during the event has high positive predictive value for PNES.

5 · The first seizure workup

  1. History from patient and witness. Most diagnostic.
  2. Examine for focal deficits, signs of head injury, meningism, skin stigmata (neurocutaneous syndromes), source of provocation (infection, intoxication).
  3. Labs: CBC, electrolytes (sodium, calcium, magnesium), glucose, renal/liver function, urine toxicology. Pregnancy test in any woman of childbearing age. Consider prolactin within 10–20 min of a tonic-clonic event (markedly elevated supports GTC vs PNES, but cannot rule out either).
  4. ECG — exclude long-QT, Brugada, AV block as causes of convulsive syncope.
  5. Imaging: non-contrast CT in the ED to exclude acute haemorrhage, tumour, abscess. Outpatient MRI brain with epilepsy protocol is the standard imaging.
  6. EEG within 24–48 h if possible (yield is higher early; sleep-deprived EEG raises sensitivity).
  7. Lumbar puncture if CNS infection or SAH suspected (after CT).

When to start an AED after a single seizure

Start an AED if the recurrence risk is > 60% over 10 years (i.e. ILAE criterion for epilepsy met after a single event). High-risk features:

  • Epileptiform EEG.
  • Structural lesion on MRI (stroke, tumour, hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia).
  • Nocturnal seizure.
  • Focal seizure.
  • Recognised epilepsy syndrome (e.g. JME morning myoclonus).

Counsel about driving restrictions (jurisdiction-specific), occupational risk, swimming/bathing safety, and seizure first-aid. In most jurisdictions, driving is forbidden for 6–12 months after a seizure with impaired awareness.

6 · Provoked / acute symptomatic seizures

Treat the cause; AEDs short-term or not at all.

ProvocationApproach
Hypoglycaemia (< 36 mg/dL)IV dextrose with thiamine. No AED needed.
Hyperglycaemia (> 450 with ketoacidosis) or non-ketotic hyperosmolarCorrect the metabolic state. Non-ketotic hyperglycaemia famously causes focal seizures resistant to phenytoin.
Hyponatraemia (< 115)Cautious correction.
Alcohol withdrawalBenzodiazepines, thiamine. No long-term AED.
EclampsiaMagnesium sulfate, BP control, delivery.
Drug-inducedStop offender. Common offenders include tramadol, bupropion, clozapine, imipenem, isoniazid (treat with B6), theophylline, cyclosporine, tricyclics.
Acute stroke, traumatic brain injury, CNS infection, intracranial surgeryMany centres start a short course of AED (typically levetiracetam) for 1–4 weeks. Long-term need depends on recurrence risk.
CNS infectionEmpirical antimicrobials before LP if needed; do not delay treatment.

7 · Antiseizure medications — choosing wisely

Choice depends on the seizure type/syndrome, the patient's comorbidities and other drugs, and side-effect tolerability. The goal is monotherapy at the lowest effective dose.

8 · Status epilepticus — stepwise protocol

Definition (modern)

Continuous seizure activity > 5 min, or two or more seizures without recovery between, is status epilepticus (SE) and is a neurological emergency. The 5-min threshold is operational because isolated seizures rarely last longer and irreversible neuronal injury can begin at 5 min. Non-convulsive SE must be considered if a patient does not return to baseline within 20 min of a convulsion stopping.

Stepwise pharmacotherapy (Neurocritical Care Society)

  1. 0–5 min — Stabilisation phase. ABC, position, oxygen, IV access, finger-stick glucose, vital signs, IV thiamine + dextrose if hypoglycaemic.
  2. 5–20 min — First-line (benzodiazepine).
    • IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg/dose; may repeat once after 5–10 min).
    • If no IV: IM midazolam 10 mg (≥ 40 kg) — non-inferior to IV lorazepam in the RAMPART trial.
    • Alternatives: IV diazepam 0.15 mg/kg or rectal diazepam 0.2 mg/kg.
    Common error: under-dosing. Use the full weight-based dose.
  3. 20–40 min — Second-line (urgent control). Give concurrently with or immediately after the benzodiazepine, do not wait. Choose one:
    • IV fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg (max rate 150 mg PE/min) or phenytoin 20 mg/kg (50 mg/min, with cardiac monitoring).
    • IV valproate 40 mg/kg (rate 3–6 mg/kg/min).
    • IV levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (max 4.5 g, rate 2–5 mg/kg/min).
    The ESETT trial showed similar efficacy (≈ 47%) between phenytoin, valproate, and levetiracetam.
  4. 40–60 min — Refractory SE. Failure of benzodiazepine + second-line agent. Intubate. Start continuous IV anaesthetic:
    • Midazolam 0.2 mg/kg bolus → 0.05–2.9 mg/kg/h infusion.
    • Propofol 1–2 mg/kg bolus → 30–100 µg/kg/min (watch for propofol-infusion syndrome at high dose > 48 h).
    • Pentobarbital 5–15 mg/kg → 0.5–10 mg/kg/h (long half-life, prolonged ventilation).
    Continuous EEG mandatory; titrate to seizure suppression or burst-suppression depending on protocol.
  5. > 24 h on infusion — Super-refractory SE. Add ketamine (NMDA antagonist), ketogenic diet, hypothermia, immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, rituximab if autoimmune encephalitis suspected). Refer to a specialist tertiary centre.
Time targets: Benzodiazepine within 5 min of seizure onset. Second-line agent within 20 min. Anaesthetic within 40–60 min if not controlled. Underdosing and delay are the most common errors.

Concurrent workup in SE

Bedside glucose, CBC, electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), renal/liver, AED levels, troponin, ABG, pregnancy test, urine toxicology, lactate. Non-contrast CT. MRI when stable. Lumbar puncture if infection suspected (empirical antimicrobials first if delay). Continuous EEG within 1 h if not waking, maintained ≥ 48 h.

9 · Special populations

Women with epilepsy

  • Pre-conception: review AEDs and switch off teratogens when possible. Avoid valproate in women of childbearing age unless absolutely necessary (4–9% risk of major malformation; impaired IQ). Avoid polytherapy. Lamotrigine and levetiracetam have the best pregnancy safety data.
  • Folic acid 0.4–5 mg daily from before conception.
  • Lamotrigine levels fall in pregnancy (induced glucuronidation); monitor and titrate up.
  • Oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives reduce lamotrigine levels and are reduced in efficacy by enzyme-inducing AEDs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, topiramate > 200 mg). Counsel about reliable contraception.
  • Catamenial epilepsy — seizure clustering around menses; specific treatment options exist.

Older adults

Stroke is the leading cause of new-onset epilepsy after 60. Choose AEDs with minimal interactions and renal dosing in mind. Lamotrigine and levetiracetam are usually preferred. Avoid phenytoin where possible due to long-term cerebellar toxicity, cognitive effects, and interactions.

Driving and lifestyle

Most jurisdictions require a seizure-free period (often 6–12 months) before resuming driving. Counsel about avoiding ladders, working at heights, swimming alone, unsupervised bathing of infants, and prompt reporting of any breakthrough seizure.

Drug-resistant epilepsy

Failure of two appropriately chosen and dosed AEDs to control seizures = drug-resistant epilepsy. Up to 80% of well-selected drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy patients become seizure-free after surgical resection. Refer to an epilepsy centre for evaluation (video-EEG, MRI, neuropsychological testing).

10 · Red flags & when to refer

Refer urgently / admit:
  • Status epilepticus.
  • First-ever seizure (most warrant ED evaluation and same-week neurology).
  • New focal deficit, fever, or meningism with seizure.
  • Suspected eclampsia.
  • Pregnant woman with breakthrough seizure on AED.
  • Suspected infantile spasms.
  • Failure to wake within 20 min of a convulsion stopping → suspect non-convulsive SE.
Routine neurology referral:
  • Established epilepsy with breakthrough seizures despite adherence.
  • Drug-resistant epilepsy (failure of two AEDs).
  • Pre-conception counselling.
  • Suspected non-epileptic events for video-EEG.
