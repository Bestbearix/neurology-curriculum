Why this module?

MS is the most common non-traumatic cause of disability in young adults; ~95% of patients reach a neurologist via a primary-care referral with vague symptoms (visual blur, numbness, fatigue, weakness). The diagnostic and therapeutic landscape has shifted dramatically:

  • The 2017 McDonald criteria allow many patients to be diagnosed at the first attack.
  • NMOSD (AQP4-IgG) and MOGAD (MOG-IgG) have split from "MS" into distinct diseases with different treatments — sometimes the same MS drugs worsen these conditions.
  • High-efficacy DMTs (anti-CD20, natalizumab) are increasingly used early for active disease.
  • For NMOSD, three monoclonal antibodies (eculizumab/ravulizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab) gained FDA approval 2019–2020.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Recognise classic MS presentations: optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, INO, brainstem/cerebellar syndromes, Uhthoff, L'hermitte.
  • Apply the 2017 McDonald criteria for DIS and DIT.
  • Distinguish MS from NMOSD and MOGAD by clinical and laboratory features.
  • Manage an acute relapse (steroids; PLEX for severe).
  • Counsel about the DMT ladder — including the safety profiles of natalizumab (PML), anti-CD20 (hypogammaglobulinaemia), alemtuzumab (secondary autoimmunity).
  • Identify when to escalate therapy (breakthrough disease, NEDA-3 failure).
  • Recognise NMOSD red flags (LETM, area postrema syndrome, severe ON) and refer for AQP4/MOG-targeted treatment.
The single most important habit: in any first inflammatory demyelinating event, send serum AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG before assuming MS. NMOSD and MOGAD treatments differ from MS dramatically — interferon-β, natalizumab, and fingolimod can worsen NMOSD.

2. Classic MS clinical presentations
  • Optic neuritis — unilateral painful vision loss; pain worse with eye movement; RAPD; reduced colour vision; recovers over weeks.
  • Transverse myelitis — subacute weakness + sensory level + bladder; usually short and partial in MS.
  • INO — failure of adduction with abducting eye nystagmus. Bilateral INO in a young person is MS until proven otherwise.
  • L'hermitte sign — electric shock down spine on neck flexion (cervical cord lesion).
  • Uhthoff phenomenon — transient symptom worsening with heat. Not a relapse.
  • Sensory: numbness, tingling, dysesthesia. Fatigue, cognitive slowing, depression.
3. The 2017 McDonald criteria — DIS and DIT

Diagnose MS by showing dissemination in space (DIS) and dissemination in time (DIT), after excluding alternatives.

MRI DIS: ≥ 1 T2 lesion in ≥ 2 of: periventricular, juxtacortical/cortical, infratentorial, spinal cord.

MRI DIT: simultaneous gadolinium-enhancing AND non-enhancing lesions, OR new T2/gad lesion on follow-up MRI.

Big 2017 change: in CIS patients, CSF-specific oligoclonal bands can substitute for DIT, enabling earlier MS diagnosis at first attack.

4. Imaging & CSF

MRI brain + whole spine with and without gadolinium. Typical MS lesions: ovoid, periventricular ("Dawson fingers"), juxtacortical, infratentorial, callosal. Spinal lesions in MS are usually short (< 2 vertebral segments) and peripheral. Gadolinium enhancement = active inflammation (lasts 2–8 weeks).

CSF in MS: oligoclonal bands present (95%), mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (< 50), normal glucose. Higher counts suggest NMOSD/MOGAD/infection.

5. Relapse management

Relapse = new or worsening neurological symptoms ≥ 24 h, without fever/infection, > 30 days from prior attack.

  • Always exclude pseudo-relapse first (fever, UTI, dehydration, sleep deprivation).
  • IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3–5 days (oral equivalent works equally well). Hastens recovery; no long-term disability change.
  • Plasma exchange (PLEX) 5–7 cycles for severe steroid-refractory attacks (especially ON, TM, brainstem); lower threshold in NMOSD.
6. The DMT ladder

High efficacy: anti-CD20 (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, rituximab off-label), natalizumab (PML risk; stratify by JCV), alemtuzumab (secondary autoimmunity), cladribine.

Moderate efficacy: S1P modulators (fingolimod, siponimod, ozanimod, ponesimod), fumarates (dimethyl, diroximel).

Lower (platform): teriflunomide, interferons (IFNβ), glatiramer acetate.

Modern paradigm: start high-efficacy for active disease, especially in young patients. Reassess MRI at 6–12 months for "NEDA-3" (no relapses, no new MRI lesions, no disability progression); escalate if breakthrough.

Critical safety notes:
  • Natalizumab + JCV-positive + long duration → PML risk. Stratify with JCV antibody index.
  • Anti-CD20 → hypogammaglobulinaemia with repeated dosing; check IgG.
  • Stopping fingolimod can trigger MS rebound; never just stop.
  • Pre-DMT screen: HBV, HCV, HIV, TB, VZV titre, JCV (if natalizumab).
7. NMOSD — what every clinician must know

An astrocytopathy driven by AQP4-IgG. Six core syndromes (2015 IPND criteria):

  1. Optic neuritis (often bilateral, severe, poor recovery)
  2. LETM
  3. Area postrema syndrome — intractable nausea/vomiting/hiccups > 48 h
  4. Acute brainstem syndrome
  5. Symptomatic narcolepsy / acute diencephalic syndrome with NMOSD MRI
  6. Symptomatic cerebral syndrome with NMOSD brain lesions

Diagnosis: 1 core syndrome + AQP4-IgG positive = NMOSD. AQP4-IgG-negative cases require 2 core syndromes and additional MRI features.

Treatment: acute — high-dose steroids + early PLEX. Long-term: eculizumab/ravulizumab (C5 inhibitor), inebilizumab (anti-CD19), satralizumab (anti-IL-6R), or rituximab (off-label). All FDA approved 2019–2020 for AQP4+ NMOSD.

MS DMTs interferon-β, natalizumab, and fingolimod can worsen NMOSD — never start them without checking AQP4-IgG.
8. MOGAD — the newer entity

MOG-IgG-associated disease. Distinct from MS and NMOSD. International criteria 2023 (Banwell et al.).

  • Optic neuritis (most common; often bilateral; disc swelling; better recovery than NMOSD)
  • Transverse myelitis (conus often involved)
  • ADEM-like presentation (especially in children)
  • Cerebral cortical encephalitis with seizures (FLAMES — cortical FLAIR hyperintensity)

~50% monophasic; ~50% relapsing. CSF OCBs in only ~10–20% (vs ~95% in MS).

Treatment: acute high-dose steroids. For relapsing disease: rituximab, MMF, IVIG, tocilizumab (off-label). NMOSD-specific mAbs (eculizumab, inebilizumab, satralizumab) NOT approved for MOGAD.

9. ADEM and tumefactive demyelination

ADEM: monophasic post-infectious demyelination, predominantly in children. Encephalopathy required for diagnosis. ~50% paediatric ADEM is MOG-IgG positive. Treat with high-dose IV methylprednisolone, then taper; PLEX or IVIG if refractory. Most adults recover well; relapse should prompt reassessment.

Tumefactive MS: single or few large (> 2 cm) demyelinating lesions with mass effect, ring enhancement (often "open ring" facing white matter), perilesional oedema. Can mimic tumour; biopsy occasionally needed.

Marburg variant: fulminant, often monophasic and fatal within a year.

10. When to refer
  • First clinical demyelinating event for full workup and DMT initiation.
  • Severe acute attack (LETM, severe ON, brainstem) — for consideration of PLEX, NMOSD/MOGAD workup.
  • Suspected NMOSD/MOGAD — for antibody-targeted treatment.
  • Breakthrough disease on DMT — escalate.
  • Pregnancy planning.
  • Atypical features suggesting mimic.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 28-year-old woman with painful left vision loss over 3 days

She reports retro-orbital pain worse with eye movement. Exam: LE visual acuity 20/200, RAPD, reduced red colour saturation, swollen disc on fundoscopy. MRI brain shows 3 ovoid periventricular T2 lesions and 1 gadolinium-enhancing juxtacortical lesion. CSF: 6 WBC/µL, 2 oligoclonal bands not in serum. AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG negative.

Diagnosis: Clinically isolated syndrome (left optic neuritis) meeting 2017 McDonald criteria for MS (DIS: lesions in 2 typical regions; DIT: simultaneous enhancing + non-enhancing lesions; OCBs supportive).

Plan: Acute: IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3–5 days for the optic neuritis. Long-term: start high-efficacy DMT (anti-CD20 ocrelizumab or ofatumumab) given young age and confirmed MS. Pre-DMT: HBV/HCV/HIV/TB/VZV screening; baseline labs. MRI at 6 months to assess for NEDA-3. Counsel about vitamin D, smoking cessation, exercise, mood, pregnancy planning.

Case 2 — A 42-year-old woman with bilateral severe vision loss and paraplegia

One week ago she developed bilateral painful vision loss to hand motion. Now: paraplegia, T8 sensory level, urinary retention. MRI: bilateral optic nerve enhancement; cord lesion extending from T2 to T10 (LETM). Brain MRI shows a lesion near area postrema. AQP4-IgG positive at high titre.

Diagnosis: Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, AQP4-IgG positive.

Plan: IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 5 days plus early PLEX (5 cycles) — lower threshold for PLEX in NMOSD because attacks are stepwise and disabling. Start long-term immunotherapy: eculizumab, inebilizumab, or satralizumab (all FDA-approved 2019–2020 for AQP4+ NMOSD), or rituximab off-label if access limited. Meningococcal vaccination before eculizumab. Avoid interferon-β, natalizumab, fingolimod — these MS drugs worsen NMOSD. Long-term monitoring; family planning counselling.

Case 3 — A 9-year-old boy with confusion, hemiparesis, and seizures 2 weeks after viral illness

Acute encephalopathy, fever, multifocal neurological signs including left hemiparesis and a focal seizure. MRI: multiple large, confluent T2 hyperintensities in deep grey matter (thalami, basal ganglia), brainstem, and subcortical white matter. CSF: 50 WBC/µL lymphocytic, mild protein elevation, no OCBs. MOG-IgG positive.

Diagnosis: ADEM phenotype of MOGAD.

Plan: IV methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/day (max 1 g) × 3–5 days, followed by oral taper over 4–6 weeks (steroid taper may reduce relapse risk in MOGAD). PLEX or IVIG if steroid-refractory or severe. ICU monitoring as needed. Antiseizure medication. Most children recover well. Long-term monitoring — if relapsing course emerges, consider maintenance immunotherapy (rituximab, MMF, monthly IVIG, or tocilizumab off-label).

Case 4 — A 35-year-old established MS patient on natalizumab presents with new cognitive change and right-sided weakness

On natalizumab for 3 years. JCV antibody index 2.4 (high). Recent labs unremarkable. New MRI shows asymmetric, subcortical, non-enhancing white-matter lesions in the right frontal lobe, sparing the cortex but extending into U-fibres — different pattern from her usual MS lesions.

Diagnosis: Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) suspected.

Plan: STOP natalizumab immediately. Confirm with CSF JCV DNA PCR (highly sensitive at experienced labs). MRI follow-up. PLEX to remove natalizumab and accelerate immune reconstitution. Manage immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) — may need steroids. Transition to a different DMT (anti-CD20 if she stabilises). Mortality from PML is significant; early recognition essential.

JCV-positive patients with high index, particularly > 2 years on natalizumab, have PML risk approaching 1% per year. Stratify before continuing.
Case 5 — A 50-year-old woman with 18 months of insidious right leg weakness and stiffness

No discrete relapses. Progressive spastic monoparesis of the right leg with hyperreflexia and an upgoing right plantar response. MRI brain: multiple periventricular T2 lesions in a typical MS distribution; cord MRI: 2 short cervical T2 lesions. CSF: 4 OCBs, IgG index elevated. AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG negative. Excluded: B12, copper, HTLV-1, vasculitis screen, syphilis, HIV.

Diagnosis: Primary progressive MS (PPMS) by 2017 McDonald criteria — 1 year of progression + DIS in brain (typical lesions) + spinal cord lesions + CSF-specific OCBs.

Plan: Start ocrelizumab — the only DMT with PPMS approval (most benefit in younger patients with active MRI disease). Pre-DMT screening. Aggressive rehabilitation, PT/OT, fall prevention. Symptom management: baclofen for spasticity, treat depression and fatigue. Vitamin D, vascular risk factor control, smoking cessation. MRI follow-up to assess for "active" disease.
