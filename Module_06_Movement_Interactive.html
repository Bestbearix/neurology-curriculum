Why this module?

Movement disorders are common in general practice — essential tremor is the most prevalent adult movement disorder, Parkinson disease the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer, and drug-induced movement disorders are everywhere antipsychotics and antiemetics are prescribed. The trap is conflating phenomenologies: not every tremor is PD, not every twitch is a tic, not every "spasm" is dystonia.

After this module you will:

  • Describe the phenomenology before naming the disease.
  • Distinguish ET, PD tremor, intention tremor, and functional tremor.
  • Apply MDS-style PD diagnostic criteria and recognise red flags for Parkinson-plus.
  • Choose initial therapy for PD and know which antiemetics and antipsychotics are safe.
  • Recognise hemiballism, chorea, focal dystonia, and the tic spectrum.
  • Recognise the drug-induced emergencies (acute dystonia, NMS, serotonin syndrome) and treat them.
The single principle to anchor everything: phenomenology first, diagnosis second. "This is asymmetric rest tremor with bradykinesia and rigidity" → then think PD. "This is symmetric postural tremor that improves with alcohol" → then think ET.

Lessons

1. Phenomenology first — the vocabulary you must speak fluently

Decide hypokinetic (parkinsonism) vs hyperkinetic first. Then if hyperkinetic, name it:

  • Tremor — rhythmic oscillation.
  • Dystonia — sustained twisting / abnormal posture; often action-induced; sensory tricks may help.
  • Chorea — brief, irregular, unpredictable, flowing.
  • Ballism — large-amplitude proximal flinging.
  • Athetosis — slow distal writhing.
  • Myoclonus — quick shock-like jerk.
  • Tic — stereotyped, semi-voluntary, with premonitory urge and brief suppressibility.
  • Akathisia — subjective inner restlessness with urge to move.
Mislabelling the phenomenology is the single most common error in this field. Take time to observe before reaching for a diagnosis.
2. Approach: history + examination essentials

History: location, periodicity, triggers/relievers, sensory tricks, rest vs action, urge to move, family history, medications (current and historical dopamine-blocker exposure), nonmotor symptoms (anosmia, RBD, constipation, depression — PD prodrome).

Examination: watch first; activation manoeuvres (writing, spirals, arms outstretched, finger taps, walking); tone (cogwheel = parkinsonism); postural reflexes (pull test); oculomotor (slow saccades, downgaze palsy = PSP); cognition/mood; KF rings (Wilson).

4. Essential tremor — treatment

First-line: propranolol 60–320 mg/d or primidone 25–250 mg/d. Add-on: topiramate, gabapentin, intermittent benzodiazepines. Botulinum toxin for focal head/voice tremor. Refractory: MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy or DBS (VIM thalamic nucleus).

5. Parkinson disease — diagnosis

Clinical diagnosis. Cardinal motor features (TRAP): tremor at rest, rigidity, akinesia/bradykinesia, postural instability (late).

Diagnosis: bradykinesia + rest tremor and/or rigidity; supportive features: asymmetric onset, clear levodopa response, levodopa-induced dyskinesia. Exclude red flags for Parkinson-plus.

Nonmotor prodrome often years before motor: anosmia, REM sleep behaviour disorder, constipation, depression, orthostatic hypotension.

Imaging: MRI usually normal in PD. DaT scan shows reduced striatal uptake in PD/Parkinson-plus; normal in ET, drug-induced parkinsonism, and SWEDD.

7. Levodopa complications — fluctuations & dyskinesia

After 2+ years of therapy, ~30%+ develop motor fluctuations and dyskinesia from loss of the long-duration response.

  • Peak-dose dyskinesia — choreiform at maximum benefit; lower dose, add amantadine.
  • Diphasic dyskinesia — at onset/offset; smoother delivery (intestinal gel, longer-acting agonists).
  • Off dystonia — painful early-morning foot cramps; overnight dopamine agonist or extended-release levodopa.
  • Wearing off — shorten interval, add COMT or MAO-B, consider device-based therapy.
8. Atypical parkinsonism / Parkinson-plus

Red flags: symmetric onset, poor levodopa response, early falls (< 3 years), early dementia or hallucinations, early autonomic failure, vertical gaze palsy, cerebellar signs, apraxia/alien-limb, rapid progression.

9. Dystonia, chorea, tics, myoclonus — fast recap

Dystonia: focal forms most common — cervical dystonia, blepharospasm, spasmodic dysphonia, writer's cramp. Treat with botulinum toxin q12 weeks. Trial levodopa in any childhood-onset dystonia (dopa-responsive dystonia).

Chorea: first-line cause to remember is Huntington (AD, CAG repeat). Hemichorea/hemiballism → contralateral subthalamic nucleus lesion; always check glucose (non-ketotic hyperglycaemia). Treat with VMAT2 inhibitors (tetrabenazine, deutetrabenazine, valbenazine).

Tics & Tourette: CBIT (habit reversal therapy) first-line; alpha-2 agonists, VMAT2 inhibitors, atypical antipsychotics for impairing tics.

Myoclonus: classify by origin (cortical, subcortical, spinal, peripheral). Valproate, clonazepam, levetiracetam, piracetam.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 68-year-old with a slowly progressive right-hand tremor

3 years of right-hand pill-rolling tremor at rest. Wife says he is slower, expressionless, and his handwriting has shrunk. Exam: right cogwheel rigidity, bradykinesia, reduced right arm swing, micrographia. Pull test borderline. No autonomic symptoms. MRI normal.

Diagnosis: Parkinson disease (asymmetric rest tremor + bradykinesia + rigidity, no Parkinson-plus red flags).

Plan: start carbidopa/levodopa (e.g. 25/100 mg ½ tab TID, titrate to 1 tab TID over 2 weeks). Counsel about expected response, side effects, no need to delay treatment. Avoid metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, typical antipsychotics. Address constipation, sleep, depression. Movement-disorder referral.

Case 2 — A 72-year-old with symmetric parkinsonism, multiple falls, and downward gaze problems

1 year of slowness with backwards falls (4 in last 6 months). Symmetric rigidity, hypomimia, axial rigidity, very slow saccades, impaired downgaze on examination. Levodopa 600 mg/d for 6 weeks — no meaningful improvement. MRI: midbrain atrophy ("humming bird" sign).

Diagnosis: Progressive supranuclear palsy (Richardson syndrome).

Plan: Continue levodopa trial up to ~1000 mg/d. Refer to specialist. Falls prevention. Physiotherapy. Anticipate dysphagia and pseudobulbar affect.

Case 3 — A 45-year-old woman with sudden left-sided flinging movements

One-day history of large-amplitude wild flinging of the left arm and leg. T2DM, HbA1c 13%, random glucose 520 mg/dL, no ketones. MRI: T1 hyperintensity in right putamen.

Diagnosis: non-ketotic hyperglycaemic hemichorea/hemiballism.

Plan: Aggressively correct hyperglycaemia (movements typically resolve over days to weeks). Symptomatic VMAT2 inhibitor (tetrabenazine) or low-dose haloperidol if severe and disabling. Educate about diabetes; long-term glycaemic control prevents recurrence.

Case 4 — A 19-year-old with new dystonic posture and a strange voice

3 months of progressive limb stiffness, dystonic posturing of the right arm, dysarthric speech, behaviour change, school decline. Labs: AST/ALT elevated. Eye exam: Kayser–Fleischer rings present.

Diagnosis: Wilson disease (neurological presentation).

Plan: Confirm with serum ceruloplasmin (low), 24-h urinary copper (elevated), MRI brain (basal ganglia + brainstem signal change, often a "face of giant panda" in midbrain). Treat with copper chelation (penicillamine or trientine) and zinc; refer to specialist. Screen siblings (autosomal recessive). Lifelong therapy. Always exclude Wilson disease in any movement disorder presenting before age ~50.

Case 5 — A 30-year-old man brought to ED with severe neck arching and oculogyric crisis after one dose of an antiemetic

Received metoclopramide 10 mg IV for nausea 90 minutes ago in the ED. Now has forced upward eye deviation, severe retrocollis, distress, normal mental status.

Diagnosis: acute dystonic reaction.

Plan: Give benztropine 1–2 mg IV/IM (or diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV/IM). Dramatic relief within minutes. Document the reaction; oral benztropine for 24–48 h to prevent recurrence. Patient should avoid metoclopramide and prochlorperazine in future; alert on chart. Use ondansetron for future antiemesis.

Case 6 — A 45-year-old woman 2 weeks after starting an SSRI plus tramadol presents with confusion, fever, and shaking

Temp 39.5 °C, tachycardia, BP 170/100, agitated, sweating with hyperactive bowel sounds. Exam: dilated pupils, generalized rigidity worse in legs, sustained ankle clonus, hyperreflexia, intermittent myoclonus.

Diagnosis: serotonin syndrome (SSRI + tramadol — both serotonergic). Distinguishes from NMS by clonus and hyperreflexia.

Plan: Stop all serotonergic drugs; IV fluids and cooling; benzodiazepines for agitation and rigidity; cyproheptadine 12 mg PO/NG load then 2 mg q2h up to 32 mg/d if severe. ICU monitoring. Improves within 24–72 h. Counsel about serotonergic drug stacking.
