Why this module?

Movement disorders are common wherever medicine is practised. Essential tremor is the most prevalent adult movement disorder; Parkinson disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disease; restless legs syndrome affects somewhere between 5% and 25% of adults; and drug-induced movement disorders appear wherever antipsychotics and antiemetics are prescribed — which is everywhere. The trap is conflating phenomenologies: not every tremor is Parkinson disease, not every twitch is a tic, not every "spasm" is dystonia, and not every late-onset ataxia is idiopathic.

What you should be able to do after this module

  • Name the phenomenology before naming the disease — and know the four traps in naming.
  • Separate essential tremor, Parkinson tremor, dystonic tremor, drug-induced tremor and functional tremor at the bedside.
  • Apply MDS-style criteria for Parkinson disease, and the MDS 2022 criteria for MSA.
  • Start Parkinson therapy at the right time — and explain why delaying levodopa is no longer defensible.
  • Recognise chorea, ballism, dystonia, tics and myoclonus, and run the reversible-cause sweep for each.
  • Work up a late-onset ataxia, including the repeat expansions that standard sequencing misses.
  • Treat restless legs the way the 2024 guideline says, not the way it used to be taught.
  • Make a positive, rule-in diagnosis of a functional movement disorder.
  • Recognise and treat every movement-disorder emergency.
The single principle to anchor everything: phenomenology first, diagnosis second. "This is asymmetric rest tremor with decremental bradykinesia and rigidity" → then think Parkinson disease. "This is symmetric postural tremor that improves with alcohol, with no other signs" → then think essential tremor. "This is brief, unpredictable and flows from part to part" → then think chorea, and check a glucose.

Lessons

1 · Phenomenology first — the vocabulary you must speak fluently

Decide hypokinetic (parkinsonism) versus hyperkinetic first. Then, if hyperkinetic, name it:

  • Tremor — rhythmic oscillation. Always state the activation condition: rest, postural, kinetic, intention, task-specific, position-specific.
  • Dystonia — sustained twisting or abnormal posture; patterned; often action-induced; overflow to adjacent muscles; sensory trick relieves.
  • Chorea — brief, irregular, unpredictable, flowing from part to part. Motor impersistence (milkmaid grip, darting tongue).
  • Ballism — large-amplitude proximal flinging; chorea of the shoulder and hip.
  • Athetosis — slow, writhing, distal; slow chorea.
  • Myoclonus — quick shock-like jerk (positive) or brief lapse of tone (negative — asterixis).
  • Tic — stereotyped, semi-voluntary, with a premonitory urge and brief suppressibility at a cost.
  • Stereotypy — identical, prolonged, non-goal-directed repetition; no urge; onset before age 3.
  • Akathisia — subjective inner restlessness driving movement; almost always drug-linked.
  • Ataxia — normal force, wrong trajectory.
Four traps in naming. (1) Tic vs stereotypy vs compulsion — only the tic has a premonitory urge. (2) Dystonic tremor vs parkinsonian tremor — dystonic tremor is jerky, direction-specific and has a null point. (3) Myoclonus vs tremor — myoclonus is discontinuous even when fast; EMG burst <100 ms. (4) Asterixis is not a tremor — it is negative myoclonus and it means metabolic or toxic until proven otherwise.
2 · Approach: the history and examination that actually change the diagnosis

History: location · tempo and periodicity (continuous vs paroxysmal) · triggers and relievers (sudden movement, prolonged exercise, fasting, alcohol, caffeine, specific tasks) · sensory tricks · rest vs action · inner urge and suppressibility · three-generation family history · current and historical drug exposure · non-motor symptoms (anosmia, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, orthostasis) · functional impact.

Examination: watch for a full minute before touching the patient. Activation manoeuvres: writing, Archimedes spirals on one page from both hands, pouring water, arms outstretched and wing-beating, finger-to-nose, rapid taps looking for decrement, and walking with attention to arm swing, stride, turning and freezing. Distraction and entrainment with a contralateral tapped rhythm. Tone: cogwheel vs clasp-knife vs paratonia vs dystonic co-contraction. Pull test. Saccade velocity and downgaze. Slit lamp for Kayser–Fleischer rings. Blood film for acanthocytes.

The sign-based approach to gait. Do not wait for a full classic syndrome. Each single axial sign has its own differential: widened base (cerebellar, sensory, vestibular, frontal, functional); narrowed base (dystonia, spastic, functional); festination and freezing (PD, vascular, NPH, PSP-PGF); magnetic gait (NPH, vascular); lateral trunk flexion (Pisa syndrome); retropulsion (PSP).

Default first-tier workup: FBC with film, U&E, calcium, magnesium, glucose/HbA1c, LFTs, TFTs, CK, ESR/CRP, ANA, antiphospholipid antibodies, B12 — and in anyone under 50, caeruloplasmin, serum copper, 24-hour urinary copper and a slit-lamp examination. MRI with iron-sensitive (SWI/GRE) sequences.

The spiral rule. Have the patient draw an Archimedes spiral, then rest their hands in the lap. Kinetic > rest → dystonic tremor. Rest > kinetic → Parkinson disease. In a DaT-supported cohort of 102 patients this single visual comparison gave 90% sensitivity, 88% specificity and near-perfect inter-rater agreement (κ 0.92). It costs nothing and can spare a DaT scan.

Treating essential tremor: propranolol 60–320 mg/day or primidone 25→250 mg/day first-line (a network meta-analysis of 33 RCTs supports propranolol and atenolol among orals); topiramate, gabapentin or intermittent benzodiazepine as add-on; botulinum toxin for head or voice tremor; and MRgFUS thalamotomy or VIM-DBS for refractory disease — DBS ranked highest of all interventions in the same analysis.

Drug-induced tremor: amiodarone, SSRIs/SNRIs, amitriptyline, lithium, valproate, β-agonists, dopamine-receptor antagonists, VMAT2 inhibitors, ethanol and cocaine. Risk rises with polypharmacy, age, high dose and immediate-release preparations. Unilateral, task-specific, sudden-onset, distractible or entrainable features argue against a simple drug cause.

4 · Parkinson disease — diagnosis, and the levodopa question the trials have settled

TRAP: rest Tremor, Rigidity, Akinesia/bradykinesia (essential — slowness with decrement), Postural instability (late; if early, question the diagnosis). MDS criteria = bradykinesia plus rest tremor and/or rigidity, with supportive criteria, absolute exclusions and red flags.

Prodrome: hyposmia, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, orthostatic hypotension — often years to decades ahead. PSG-proven RBD is the strongest single prodromal marker of a synucleinopathy.

Imaging: MRI to exclude mimics; DaT-SPECT reduced in PD and Parkinson-plus, normal in ET, dystonic tremor, drug-induced and functional parkinsonism. Cardiac MIBG reduced in PD/DLB/PAF, normal in MSA — the best discriminator when PD vs MSA is the real question.

Three questions the literature has answered.
1. Does delaying levodopa prevent motor complications? No. LEAP at 5 years (n=321): no difference in UPDRS at 3 or 5 years; dyskinesia 46/160 early-start vs 62/161 delayed-start (p=0.06). Meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (n=1149): no difference in motor complications (OR 1.39, p=0.37) or dyskinesia (OR 1.52, p=0.11), and fewer fluctuations in early starters (OR 0.70, p=0.02).
2. Should you start with an agonist instead? It delays dyskinesia at the cost of worse motor scores and agonist-specific harms — impulse-control disorders, somnolence, hallucinations. CALM-PD framed this trade-off.
3. Is rasagiline disease-modifying? Unproven. ADAGIO (n=1176): 1 mg met all three endpoints, 2 mg did not — and delayed-start designs carry intrinsic confounders (tiny effect sizes, incomplete blinding, subjective UPDRS items, practice effects). Prescribe it for symptoms.

Impulse-control disorders: screen every patient, every visit, with a partner present. First-line management is gradual reduction of the dopamine agonist (watch for agonist withdrawal syndrome), then other PD drugs; second-line, reverse the last change, CBT, STN-DBS, quetiapine or clozapine.

DBS timing: EARLYSTIM randomised 251 patients with early motor complications (mean age 52) — STN-DBS beat medical therapy on quality of life by 7.8 PDQ-39 points at 2 years. Do not wait for end-stage disease.

Never give metoclopramide, prochlorperazine or haloperidol to a patient with parkinsonism. Use ondansetron or domperidone for nausea; quetiapine, clozapine or pimavanserin for psychosis. And never abruptly stop dopaminergic therapy — missed doses in hospital cause parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome. Prescribe the patient's own timings, and use a rotigotine patch or NG levodopa if nil by mouth.
5 · Atypical parkinsonism — including the MDS 2022 MSA criteria

Red flags: symmetric onset · poor levodopa response despite 600–1000 mg/day for a month · falls within 3 years · early dementia or hallucinations · early severe autonomic failure · cerebellar signs · downgaze palsy · apraxia, cortical sensory loss or alien limb · stridor · rapid progression.

PSP is not one picture: PSP-RS (classic, survival 5–7 years), PSP-P (asymmetric, tremor, initial levodopa response, survival 8–12 years, frequently mislabelled PD for years), PSP-PGF (pure gait freezing), PSP-CBS. Cognition is an early feature, not a late one — a dysexecutive syndrome from dorsolateral prefrontal atrophy, with apathy as the commonest behavioural symptom.

MSA — MDS 2022 criteria. Clinically established requires at least one autonomic feature (unexplained voiding difficulty with post-void residual ≥100 mL, urge incontinence, or neurogenic OH ≥20/10 mmHg within 3 minutes), plus poorly levodopa-responsive parkinsonism or a cerebellar syndrome (≥2 features), plus at least two supportive features and at least one MRI marker, with exclusions absent. Clinically probable relaxes each of these (PVR of any volume, OH within 10 minutes, ≥1 supportive feature, no MRI marker needed).

Three bedside additions that change MSA practice: bladder-scan a post-void residual (it is now a formal criterion); ask the partner about nocturnal stridor (it carries a risk of sudden death); and explicitly request iron-sensitive MRI sequences, telling the radiologist you are looking for MSA.

Corticobasal syndrome vs degeneration. The Armstrong criteria broadened the recognised phenotype but lack specificity: in a clinicopathological validation, all 14 patients with corticobasal syndrome but non-CBD pathology met probable or possible CBD criteria, and no clinical feature was commoner in proven CBD. Diagnose the syndrome; keep the differential open.

DBS in atypical parkinsonism: a systematic review of 66 patients across 25 studies (only 3 randomised) concludes current evidence does not support it. Say this clearly to families who have read about DBS online.

6 · Dystonia — recognition, classification and botulinum toxin

Four clues that a movement is dystonic: patterned phenomenology · alleviating manoeuvre (sensory trick) · worsening with voluntary movement · overflow to adjacent muscles (mirror dystonia in the other hand while writing).

Two axes: Axis I = clinical (age at onset, body distribution, temporal pattern, associated features — isolated vs combined); Axis II = aetiology (inherited, acquired, idiopathic). A 2026 review argues many dystonias share a final common mechanism — altered sensorimotor circuit development in critical windows — which is why targeting the shared mechanism may eventually treat genetically distinct forms.

Focal dystonias you will meet: cervical dystonia (neck pain is often the dominant complaint) · blepharospasm (pretarsal injection beats preseptal) · laryngeal dystonia · writer's cramp · oromandibular dystonia.

Botulinum toxin evidence: daxibotulinumtoxinA in cervical dystonia (ASPEN-1 pooled, n=357) gave TWSTRS −12.0 vs −4.6 placebo with a median duration ≈24 weeks — roughly double conventional toxin — and lower dysphagia and weakness rates. Quality of life (CDIP-58) is a validated endpoint: ask about pain, sleep and social withdrawal, not just head position.

Always trial levodopa in childhood- or adolescent-onset dystonia. Dopa-responsive dystonia (Segawa, GCH1) responds dramatically to 50–100 mg/day. Clues: onset in a leg with gait disturbance, diurnal fluctuation (worse by evening, better after sleep), family history. It is regularly misdiagnosed as cerebral palsy for decades. Give a full month before concluding it is not responsive.
Status dystonicus — escalating generalised spasms with rhabdomyolysis, renal failure and respiratory compromise. ICU, escalating sedation, hydration, treat the trigger, and urgent GPi-DBS or intrathecal baclofen.
7 · Chorea — reversible causes first, always
Check a capillary glucose in every patient with acute chorea or hemiballism, before anything else. A review of 243 published sources found the vast majority of unilateral or markedly asymmetric chorea is acquired, not genetic — commonest being non-ketotic hyperglycaemic hemichorea, then Sydenham chorea, antiphospholipid syndrome and drug-induced chorea. Symmetric chorea does not exclude a reversible cause either.

Diabetic striatopathy (100 pooled cases): mean age 67, 67% female, hemichorea in 71%, mean glucose 468 mg/dL and HbA1c 13.7%, basal ganglia T1 hyperintensity in 80%. Treatment is prompt glycaemic control, with a dopamine blocker or VMAT2 inhibitor for symptom relief meanwhile.

Huntington disease: AD, CAG ≥40 in HTT; chorea + subcortical dementia + psychiatric disturbance; juvenile (Westphal) variant presents with parkinsonism, seizures and ataxia. Predictive testing requires a formal counselling protocol — never send it from a general clinic.

8 · Tics and Tourette syndrome — when to treat and with what

Tourette: ≥2 motor tics and ≥1 phonic tic, >1 year, onset before 18. ~1% of school-aged boys. 85–90% have a psychiatric comorbidity — ADHD, OCD, anxiety, depression — and these usually affect quality of life more than the tics.

Three indications for drug treatment (not tic count): physical discomfort or injury · emotional or social problems · functional interference. The goal is improved functioning, not tic elimination.

Two myths to correct. Stimulants for comorbid ADHD are not contraindicated in Tourette. And suppressing tics does not make them worse later — the rebound is short-lived.
Functional tic-like behaviours rose sharply from around 2019: abrupt onset in late adolescence or early adulthood, complex repetitive behaviours and phrases, frequent coprolalia, arm and trunk predominance, marked variability with spontaneous remissions, no childhood tic history, and poor antipsychotic response. Manage as FND, not as Tourette.

Asterixis — negative myoclonus. Elicit it deliberately (wrists extended, fingers spread; also feet and tongue) in every encephalopathic patient: it is usually asymptomatic and never volunteered. Most often hepatic encephalopathy, renal failure, hypercapnia, or drugs that raise ammonia (valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin). Unilateral asterixis suggests a contralateral thalamic or midbrain lesion.

Drug-induced myoclonus — a systematic review of 1115 reports lists level-A classes: etomidate · ceftazidime and cefepime · ciprofloxacin · citalopram, escitalopram, paroxetine, sertraline · amitriptyline · amantadine · clozapine and quetiapine · carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin, valproate · fentanyl and morphine · bismuth salts.

New myoclonus on a ward = medication review plus renal function. Opioid, gabapentinoid and cephalosporin myoclonus in renal impairment is common, under-recognised and reversible. And if there is also clonus, hyperreflexia and autonomic change, think serotonin syndrome.

Myoclonus-dystonia (SGCE, maternally imprinted so transmission looks paternal): childhood-onset subcortical myoclonus in the upper body, milder dystonia, striking alcohol responsiveness — with a real risk of alcohol misuse — and anxiety/OCD as part of the phenotype. Perampanel has been reported effective where many antiseizure drugs failed; GPi-DBS works in refractory cases.

Post-hypoxic: distinguish acute status myoclonus on day 1 (poor-prognosis marker, as one component of multimodal prognostication) from Lance-Adams action myoclonus in a patient who regained consciousness — which is compatible with good recovery.

10 · Ataxia new

Split by tempo first. Hyperacute → stroke, intoxication, Wernicke (give thiamine before glucose). Acute–subacute → post-infectious cerebellitis, Miller Fisher, autoimmune/paraneoplastic, drug toxicity, vitamin deficiency, MS. Chronic-progressive → MSA-C, ILOCA, hereditary, alcohol, hypothyroidism, structural. Episodic → EA1/EA2, SCA27B, metabolic, vestibular migraine.

Imaging-led approach to chronic ataxia: (a) no cerebellar atrophy; (b) pure cerebellar or olivopontocerebellar atrophy; (c) specific features — hot cross bun, molar tooth (Joubert), middle cerebellar peduncle T2 (FXTAS), dentate signal change (CTX, Wilson), linear pontine hypointensities plus thickened peripapillary RNFL (ARSACS), superficial siderosis, iron (NBIA), calcification (PFBC).

ILOCA vs MSA-C — the question that matters most. Of 72 ILOCA patients, 32 converted to MSA-C at a median 5.0 years. Baseline RBD or orthostatic dizziness predicted conversion; the hot-cross-bun sign did not (it appears late). Plasma NfL helped estimate timing. The "phalanx sign" on finger-to-nose testing was 61.9% sensitive but 95.7% specific for MSA-C (κ 0.73). So in every late-onset ataxia: ask about dream enactment, measure lying and standing BP, and bladder-scan a post-void residual.
"The genetic tests were negative" does not close an adult-onset ataxia. FGF14-GAA (SCA27B) and RFC1 (CANVAS) are repeat expansions that standard exome — and many genome pipelines — miss. A published case describes an FGF14 expansion missed after negative genome sequencing in a man with five years of alcohol-triggered episodic unsteadiness. Ask which test was done, and request targeted repeat-expansion testing.

Fulminant cerebellitis is a neurosurgical emergency. In a three-case paediatric series, the child given emergent posterior fossa decompression recovered fully, the child whose EVD was delayed died, and the child given urgent EVD recovered completely. Outcome tracks the speed of CSF diversion, not the steroid dose.

Symptomatic management is not nothing: intensive coordinative physiotherapy is the best-evidenced intervention of all, plus OT, SALT, aids, driving assessment, and the non-motor burden. Drugs worth trying in the right phenotype: 4-aminopyridine (downbeat nystagmus, EA2, SCA27B), acetazolamide (EA2), riluzole and N-acetyl-leucine (modest), vitamin E (curative in AVED if early).

11 · Restless legs syndrome — the guideline reversed new

Five essential criteria: urge to move the legs, usually with uncomfortable sensations · begins or worsens at rest · relieved by movement · worse in the evening or night · not solely explained by a mimic (cramps, positional discomfort, arthritis, venous stasis, akathisia, neuropathic pain).

AASM 2024 — a genuine reversal.
Strong — recommended: gabapentin enacarbil · gabapentin · pregabalin · IV ferric carboxymaltose.
Conditional — suggested: IV low-molecular-weight iron dextran · IV ferumoxytol · oral ferrous sulfate · dipyridamole · extended-release oxycodone and other opioids · bilateral high-frequency peroneal nerve stimulation.
Suggested AGAINST routine use: pramipexole · ropinirole · rotigotine · levodopa · bupropion · carbamazepine · clonazepam · valproic acid · valerian.
Strongly recommended AGAINST: cabergoline.

Iron thresholds are RLS-specific: supplement (oral or IV) if ferritin ≤75 ng/mL or transferrin saturation <20%; IV only if ferritin is 75–100 ng/mL; in children, treat for ferritin <50 ng/mL. Check both ferritin and transferrin saturation at presentation and whenever symptoms worsen.

First step is always removing aggravators: alcohol, caffeine, antihistamines (including OTC sleep aids), antidopaminergic drugs, serotonergic antidepressants, and untreated obstructive sleep apnoea.

Augmentation — the reason dopamine agonists fell from first-line. Symptoms become more intense, start earlier in the day, appear after shorter rest, and spread to the arms and trunk. It is dose-dependent, and the instinctive response (increase the dose) makes it worse. Management: recheck and replete iron, do not increase the dopaminergic, cross-taper to an α2δ ligand, cover the transition with a low-dose opioid if needed. Prevention is better: don't start an agonist first-line.
12 · Stiff-person spectrum and functional movement disorders new

Stiff-person spectrum disorders

Fluctuating axial and limb stiffness, painful spasms, gait disturbance, hypersensitivity to noise, touch and emotional stress. The spectrum runs from classic SPS (lumbar hyperlordosis, "tin soldier" gait, falls without protective reflexes) through stiff-limb syndrome and SPS-plus (brainstem/cerebellar) to PERM (encephalopathy, dysautonomia, respiratory compromise). Up to 70% are GAD65-positive — titre matters, since low titres are common in type 1 diabetes and thyroid disease; others carry amphiphysin (paraneoplastic — breast, SCLC), glycine receptor, or DPPX antibodies. EMG hallmark: continuous motor unit activity in agonist and antagonist simultaneously, abolished by sleep, anaesthesia, benzodiazepines or nerve block. A meta-analysis of 38 studies (1166 patients) confirms autoimmune comorbidities are highly prevalent — screen for type 1 diabetes, thyroid disease, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo and coeliac disease.

Three parallel treatment arms: (1) GABAergic symptomatic — diazepam and clonazepam as backbone, maintenance plus rescue dosing, add baclofen and tizanidine, then gabapentin/pregabalin; (2) immunotherapy — IVIg best evidenced, then steroids, PLEX, azathioprine/mycophenolate, rituximab, and CAR-T in refractory disease; (3) careful physiotherapy, heat, aquatic therapy, trigger desensitisation and falls prevention.

Benzodiazepine doses in SPS are often far higher than usual. Never stop them abruptly, including on hospital admission — withdrawal can precipitate life-threatening spasms and autonomic instability.

Functional movement disorders

Incidence 4–5 per 100 000 person-years; 3–8% of movement-disorder clinic visits. In the largest cohort (n=4905): mixed FMD 23%, tremor 22%, weakness 18%, then gait and dystonia. Diagnosis is positive and rule-in — internal inconsistency (variability, distractibility, entrainment) and incongruence with recognised disease. At least 37 positive signs are described; the workhorses are entrainment and the co-activation sign for tremor, and Hoover sign for weakness.

Mechanism to explain to the patient: abnormalities in predictive coding and sensorimotor integration, an altered sense of agency, maladaptive attentional focus, and disrupted limbic–motor connectivity. The hardware is intact; the problem is in how movement is predicted, attended to and owned — which is why it changes with attention, and why it can improve.

Treatment: the explanation is the first treatment (demonstrate the sign to the patient) → FND-informed physiotherapy focused on movement retraining → tailored psychotherapy → drugs only for comorbid depression, anxiety, pain and sleep. Prognosis is variable and often guarded, which is why early diagnosis matters.

Psychological stressors are not required for the diagnosis — DSM-5 removed that requirement. And FMD coexists with organic disease in roughly 10–15%: diagnosing one never excludes the other.
13 · Paroxysmal dyskinesias and hemifacial spasm new

Paroxysmal dyskinesias

Brief recurrent episodes of dystonia, chorea or ballism with a normal examination between attacks. Classified by precipitant:

A review of 500 genetically proven cases makes two points: mutations show marked pleiotropy (PRRT2 also causes benign familial infantile epilepsy, hemiplegic migraine and episodic ataxia in the same family), and not every clinical PKD/PNKD/PED carries the expected gene — a negative test does not overturn the clinical syndrome. Search for secondary causes in adult-onset cases: MS (paroxysmal tonic spasms, very carbamazepine-responsive), stroke, trauma, HIV, hypoparathyroidism, glycaemic disturbance, thyrotoxicosis, drugs.

Dyskinesia or seizure? Consciousness is preserved, there is no postictal confusion, an aura is often felt in the affected limb, and the precipitant is stereotyped. A phone video from the family is the single most valuable investigation.

Hemifacial spasm

Unilateral clonic then tonic contraction in the facial nerve territory, usually from vascular compression at the CN VII root exit zone. Persists in sleep and often shows the "other Babinski" sign (eyebrow elevation with eye closure) — which separates it from blepharospasm (bilateral, disappears in sleep, sensory tricks help). Every patient needs a dedicated posterior-fossa MRI with MRA to find the vessel and exclude a CPA tumour, epidermoid or AVM.

Botulinum toxin is the primary treatment — balance neurological benefit against facial symmetry; pretarsal injection was more effective with fewer adverse events than preseptal (5.2% vs 9.4% overall). Microvascular decompression is potentially curative: in a meta-analysis of 3556 patients, effectiveness did not differ between vertebral-artery-associated and non-VA-associated cases, but VA-associated cases had significantly more transient and permanent complications, including hearing loss. Counsel according to which vessel is responsible.

14 · Drug-induced movement disorders — and the anticholinergic reset
Abandon the term "extrapyramidal symptoms." It lumps together disorders that are neurobiologically distinct and have different treatments — and it drives the reflex misuse of anticholinergics. Name the specific disorder: drug-induced parkinsonism, acute dystonia, akathisia, or tardive dyskinesia.

Tardive dyskinesia affects roughly 22% of European patients on antipsychotics, regardless of subclass, and is routinely overlooked. Document a baseline AIMS before starting any DRBA, repeat it, and ask the family — patients are often unaware. Both valbenazine (once daily, 40→80 mg) and deutetrabenazine (twice daily with food) improve AIMS in double-blind trials.

Which drugs cause dystonia? FAERS analysis of 28 938 reports: metoclopramide most frequent and strongest signal, then prochlorperazine and haloperidol; aripiprazole second most frequently reported. Most events occurred within 30 days of starting. Dystonia was not in the package insert of eight implicated drugs. Aripiprazole + risperidone showed an interaction signal.

The prescribing lesson. Metoclopramide is the single biggest driver of drug-induced dystonia and tardive syndromes in general practice. Limit it to about 5 days, never use it in parkinsonism, and never leave it on a repeat prescription without an end date.
15 · Wilson disease, NBIA, PFBC — the must-not-miss metabolic causes

Wilson disease (ATP7B, AR). Caeruloplasmin alone is not a reliable test — it is an acute-phase reactant, falsely normal in inflammation, pregnancy and oestrogen use. Combine it with serum copper, 24-hour urinary copper, slit-lamp examination (KF rings in >95% of neurological Wilson, often absent in purely hepatic disease), LFTs and MRI. Use the Leipzig score for non-classic presentations and the New Wilson Index serially for prognosis in acute liver failure. Late-onset and asymptomatic cases exist — age over 40 does not exclude it.

Treatment: trientine is generally preferred over penicillamine in neurological presentations (penicillamine can cause initial paradoxical worsening); zinc for maintenance, presymptomatic patients and pregnancy; lifelong therapy — stopping can precipitate fulminant hepatic failure; transplant for acute liver failure or decompensated cirrhosis. Screen all first-degree relatives, with targeted genetic testing when the proband's variants are known.

NBIA: PKAN (PANK2, eye-of-the-tiger), BPAN (WDR45, biphasic — childhood delay then adolescent/adult parkinsonism-dystonia), PLAN, MPAN. Dystonia (often oromandibular with tongue-biting), parkinsonism, chorea, spasticity, retinopathy. Seizures occurred in 49.2% of 305 reported NBIA cases, concentrated in PLAN and BPAN — but only ~10% of PKAN. Image with SWI/GRE.

Primary familial brain calcification: in 516 genetically confirmed individuals — SLC20A2 61%, XPR1 16%, MYORG 13%, PDGFB 12%, PDGFRB 5%, JAM2 2%. Nearly one-third of carriers were clinically unaffected. Among affected patients, parkinsonism and speech disturbance led the motor features; cognitive deficits, headache and depression the non-motor. The basal ganglia were always calcified. Exclude calcium disorders first, and counsel families that incidental calcification is common and often benign.

16 · Investigating when the tests are not on site new

In a hospital without same-day MRI, a neuroimmunology laboratory or copper studies in the building, the question is never simply which test. It is: does this result change what I do today, this week or this month — and what do I do while I wait?

The organising principle. Movement disorders are diagnosed at the bedside and confirmed by a test. So urgency is set by one question: is there a treatment I would start, stop or change in the next few hours or days if I knew the answer? If yes, the test is urgent and may justify transfer. If no, batch it into a planned referral visit — the patient is not harmed by the wait.
TierTimeframeMeaning

Know what your CT can answer. It is not merely a poor MRI. CT is better than MRI for basal ganglia calcification, and adequate for acute haemorrhage, hydrocephalus and fourth-ventricle compression, large infarcts, striatal hyperdensity in diabetic striatopathy, and mass lesions. It is poor or useless for posterior fossa and brainstem infarct, midbrain and putaminal atrophy patterns, iron and signal change, demyelination and early encephalitis.

Much of the Wilson workup is local. Slit lamp through your own ophthalmologist (Kayser–Fleischer rings in over 95% of neurological Wilson); LFT, FBC, coagulation, reticulocytes and Coombs on site; and the 24-hour urine collected locally — but ask the laboratory for a copper-free acid-washed container first, or the sample is wasted and you lose another week. Caeruloplasmin is send-away and must never be interpreted alone.

B12 is the easiest win. Cheap assay, safe parenteral treatment, and irreversible cord damage if you wait. Send the level and start treatment the same day in a plausible picture — nitrous oxide use, vegan diet, metformin, post-gastrectomy, older age. Give folate only after B12. Thiamine is not even worth testing: give it parenterally, before glucose, and do not wait for the classic triad.

The rule that matters most here: do not let a pending antibody result delay immunotherapy in a deteriorating patient. Autoimmune encephalitis is diagnosed on clinical criteria supported by MRI, EEG and CSF, and antibody-negative disease is real — so even a returned negative panel does not close the case. Take paired serum and CSF, store a pre-treatment aliquot, cover infection, and start corticosteroids, IVIg or plasma exchange on clinical grounds.

Make one referral trip do the work of three: phenomenology in the first line of the letter · your own results sent on paper or they will be repeated · the tests named explicitly ("MRI with iron-sensitive SWI/GRE sequences" gets a different scan from "MRI brain") · imaging, LP with paired serum, neurophysiology and the specialist opinion batched into one visit · the 24-hour urine started before the trip so it travels with the patient · and a statement of what you have already excluded and already started, so your empirical treatment is not stopped on arrival. For anything in tier B, telephone before you transfer.
