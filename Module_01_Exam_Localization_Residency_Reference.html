1 The diagnostic method: localize, then characterize

▎Key points
  • Neurologic diagnosis is two questions: Where is the lesion? (localize) and What is it? (characterize).
  • Tempo is the single best aetiology clue: hyperacute (seconds–minutes) → vascular/seizure; subacute (days–weeks) → inflammatory/infectious/neoplastic; chronic (months–years) → degenerative/hereditary/compressive.
  • A precise location + an accurate tempo narrows a huge differential before any test.

This discipline separates the resident from the screening clinician. A generalist recognizes "left-sided weakness"; the resident decides whether that localizes to the right cortex, internal capsule, brainstem, cord, root, plexus, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction, or muscle — because each implies a different workup, urgency, and set of dangers.

2 The neurologic history

▎Key points
  • The history should read as a linear story that leads sensibly into the examination.
  • For each symptom pin: onset/tempo, distribution, provoking/relieving factors, associated symptoms, prior episodes.
  • Positive vs negative symptoms: loss of function (weakness, numbness, visual loss) = destruction/conduction block; positive phenomena (tingling, jerking, scintillations) = abnormal excitation (seizure, migraine, nerve irritability).
  • Interrogate "sudden." Maximal-at-onset (vascular) differs from a march over minutes (seizure, migraine aura) or progression over days (mass, inflammation).
  • Distribution localizes: focal vs multifocal vs diffuse; proximal vs distal; length-dependent vs patchy; crossed (face one side, body the other).
  • Collateral history is essential for any cognitive or episodic complaint — the patient may be an unreliable narrator, and the exam may reveal that a new informant is needed.
  • Context: vascular risk factors, medications/toxins, systemic disease, family history, occupational and travel exposures.

3 The top-down examination

▎Key points
  • Perform and record in a uniform "top-down" order (Merritt): mental status → cranial nerves → motor → reflexes → coordination → sensation → gait.
  • Consistency prevents omissions; if a portion is deliberately skipped, state so.
  • Tailor depth to the problem — a full cognitive battery is wasted on an ulnar palsy; a hand complaint demands detailed intrinsic-muscle and sensory mapping.

Include the targeted general exam when it bears on the neurologic question — heart rate/blood pressure, carotid and cardiac auscultation (stroke), skin and eyes (congenital, metabolic, infectious clues), and general appearance (cachexia, obesity).

4 Mental status & higher cortical function

▎Key points
  • Two components: psychiatric (affect, mood, thought form/content) and cognitive (consciousness, attention, language, memory, visuospatial, executive).
  • Screen: orientation, digit span forward/backward, serial 7s, three-item recall at 3 min.
  • Characterize language first (fluent vs non-fluent), then comprehension, repetition, naming, reading, writing — the pattern localizes the aphasia and hemisphere.

Test praxis (commanded/imitated gesture — e.g., "show me how to use a hammer"), visuospatial ability (clock drawing, figure copy, line bisection), and gnosis (object/picture recognition). Re-emergent primitive reflexes (grasp, snout, suck, glabellar) point to frontal-lobe disease.

5 Cranial nerves

▎Key points
  • Cranial-nerve findings are localizing gold — nuclei and tracts are anatomically precise.
  • Visual fields: monocular = pre-chiasmal; bitemporal = chiasm; homonymous = retrochiasmal.
  • Facial weakness: forehead-sparing = upper-motor-neuron (central VII); whole-hemiface = peripheral VII (Bell).

Test smell only when an anterior-fossa lesion is suspected. Assess pupils (size; light direct/consensual; near response), lids, and the full range of ocular movement. Trigeminal: facial sensation to pin/cotton, corneal reflex, masseter bulk, jaw jerk. Facial: observe at rest and with movement. Hearing by tuning fork (Weber/Rinne distinguish conductive from sensorineural). Palate/gag (only asymmetry is meaningful) and tongue (atrophy, fasciculation, deviation toward a weak hypoglossal side).

6 Motor, tone & reflexes

▎Key points
  • Assess bulk, tone, power (MRC 0–5), and the involuntary repertoire; record the pattern of weakness, not just severity.
  • Tone: spasticity (velocity-dependent, corticospinal) vs rigidity (lead-pipe/cogwheel, extrapyramidal) vs flaccidity (acute LMN / spinal shock).
  • The Babinski sign (great-toe dorsiflexion + fanning) reliably marks corticospinal damage.

Find subtle weakness the MRC scale misses: pronator drift (outstretched supinated arm drifts down and pronates) and asymmetric forearm "orbiting"; in the legs, resting hip external rotation and downward drift of the flexed prone leg betray a pyramidal lesion. Sample reflexes at biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, Achilles; facilitate hypoactive reflexes with the Jendrassik manoeuvre. Beware plantar-response mimics — brisk voluntary withdrawal is not pathological, but a slow triple-flexion response carries the same meaning as Babinski.

Root reflex map: biceps C5–6, brachioradialis C5–6, triceps C7, patellar L3–4, Achilles S1. A lost reflex localizes the root/level.

7 Sensation & coordination

▎Key points
  • Two great systems travel separately: dorsal columns (vibration/proprioception, ascend ipsilaterally, decussate in the medulla) and spinothalamic (pain/temperature, decussate within 1–2 segments).
  • Sensory testing is subjective — do it last and briefly; map deficits to a dermatome, nerve territory, sensory level, or hemibody pattern.
  • Coordination (finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements) is interpretable only in limbs with adequate power.

Add vibration/proprioception (dorsal columns) and temperature (spinothalamic) when a tract lesion or polyneuropathy is in question. Dissociated loss — one modality lost, the other spared — is a powerful localizer (see §11). Cerebellar signs (dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia) are ipsilateral to a hemispheric cerebellar lesion.

8 Gait & station

9 The first split: upper vs lower motor neuron

▎Key points
  • When weakness is present, the UMN-vs-LMN distinction splits the neuraxis in two and drives everything downstream.
  • Caveat: in the first hours of an acute UMN lesion (stroke, acute cord compression = "spinal shock") tone and reflexes may be reduced before spasticity evolves.
  • Mixed UMN + LMN signs without sensory loss → suspect motor neuron disease.

10 Localizing along the neuraxis

▎Key points
  • Once UMN vs LMN is settled, place the lesion at a level by asking what each level uniquely produces.
  • Crossed-signs rule: ipsilateral face + contralateral body (motor or sensory) = brainstem. Pinpoint by the cranial nerve — CN III = midbrain, VI/VII = pons, IX–XII = medulla.
  • Cortical sign present? Not a lacune — look for a large-vessel/embolic source.

11 Dissociated sensory loss & classic cord syndromes

▎Key points
  • Because the two sensory systems run separately in the cord, dissociated loss localizes powerfully.

12 Pattern recognition & pitfalls

▎Key points
  • Reason from syndromes, not isolated signs.
  • Sensory testing is prone to over-interpretation and suggestibility — keep it brief and don't lead the patient.
  • Make functional (non-organic) diagnoses on positive rule-in signs, never by exclusion alone.

High-yield syntheses

  • Hemiparesis + cortical signs → contralateral cortex; without cortical signs, equal face/arm/leg → internal capsule (lacune).
  • Crossed deficit → brainstem; add the cranial nerve to pinpoint the level.
  • Sensory level + paraparesis + sphincter signs → spinal cord; image urgently to exclude compression.
  • Stocking-glove loss + absent ankle jerks → polyneuropathy.
  • Fatigable ptosis/diplopia, normal sensation/reflexes → neuromuscular junction.
  • Symmetric proximal weakness, sensation intact → myopathy.
  • Mixed UMN + LMN, no sensory loss → anterior horn / motor neuron disease.
Non-organic signs (give-way weakness, midline splitting of vibration/pinprick, non-anatomical boundaries, Hoover sign) suggest a functional component — but functional and organic disease coexist. Reconcile exam with history; if they conflict, re-take the history rather than force the findings.
