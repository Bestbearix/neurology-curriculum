Module 1 — Neurologic Examination & Localization Residency

Resident-depth reference · Synthesized from Adams & Victor's Principles of Neurology (12e, Ch. 1, "Approach to the Patient With Neurologic Disease") and Merritt's Neurology (14e, Ch. 3 "The Neurologic History" and Ch. 4 "The Neurologic Examination"). Companion to the general-physician Module 1 — this version trains the exam as an instrument of localization, not a checklist.

The central premise. The neurologic history generates a hypothesis about where the lesion is and what it is; the examination is the experiment that confirms or refutes it. As Merritt's frames it, history and examination are complementary, and the exam exists to "accurately localize neurologic dysfunction and develop a differential diagnosis." Localization comes first; aetiology follows.

1. The diagnostic method: localize, then characterize

Neurologic diagnosis is a two-step act of reasoning. First, localize: which part of the nervous system, from cortex to muscle, would produce exactly this combination of findings? Second, characterize: given the location and the tempo of onset, what disease process is responsible? Tempo is the single most informative aetiological clue — hyperacute (seconds to minutes) suggests vascular or seizure; subacute (days to weeks) suggests inflammatory, infectious, or neoplastic; chronic (months to years) suggests degenerative, hereditary, or compressive. A precise localization plus an accurate tempo narrows a vast differential to a short list before a single test is ordered.

This discipline is what separates the resident from the screening clinician. A general physician may recognize "left-sided weakness"; the resident must decide whether that weakness localizes to the right cortex, the right internal capsule, the brainstem, the cord, a root or plexus, the peripheral nerve, the neuromuscular junction, or muscle — because each implies a different workup and a different set of dangers.

2. The neurologic history

The history should be a logical, linear narrative that "leads sensibly into the examination, without many surprises." For each symptom, establish onset and tempo, anatomical distribution, provoking and relieving factors, associated symptoms, and prior episodes. A few resident-level habits:

  • Pin the tempo precisely. "Sudden" must be interrogated — a deficit maximal at onset (vascular) differs fundamentally from one that marches over minutes (seizure, migraine aura) or progresses over days (mass, inflammation).
  • Distribution localizes. Focal vs multifocal vs diffuse; proximal vs distal; length-dependent vs patchy; crossed (face one side, body the other) — each pattern points to a level.
  • Use collateral sources for any cognitive or episodic complaint; the examination itself may reveal that the historian is unreliable and a new informant is needed.
  • Negative symptoms vs positive symptoms. Loss of function (negative — weakness, numbness, visual loss) implies destruction or conduction block; positive phenomena (tingling, jerking, scintillations) imply abnormal excitation (seizure, migraine, peripheral irritability).

3. The examination — a top-down approach

Perform and record the examination in a uniform order to avoid omissions; if a portion is intentionally skipped, state so explicitly. Merritt's recommends an anatomical "top-down" sequence — mental status and cranial nerves (the head), then motor and reflexes (the body), then sensation and coordination, and finally gait. Tailor depth to the problem: an exhaustive cognitive battery is wasted on an ulnar compression palsy, while a hand complaint demands detailed intrinsic-muscle and sensory mapping. Include the targeted general exam — heart rate, blood pressure, carotid and cardiac auscultation, skin and eyes — when they bear on the neurologic question.

Mental status & higher cortical function

Two artificially separable components: the psychiatric (affect, mood, thought form and content) and the cognitive (level of consciousness, attention, language, memory, visuospatial and executive ability). Screen with orientation, digit span forward and backward, serial 7s, and three-item recall at three minutes. If language is in question, characterize spontaneous speech (fluent vs non-fluent), then test comprehension, repetition, naming, reading, and writing — the combination localizes aphasia type and hemisphere. Test praxis (commanded and imitated gestures), visuospatial ability (clock drawing, figure copying, line bisection), and gnosis (object and picture recognition). Re-emergent primitive reflexes (grasp, snout, suck, glabellar) point to frontal-lobe disease.

Cranial nerves

Cranial-nerve findings are localizing gold because their nuclei and tracts are anatomically precise. Test smell only when an anterior-fossa lesion is suspected. Map visual fields by confrontation, each eye separately, and proceed to formal perimetry if abnormal — the field pattern localizes the lesion along the visual pathway (monocular = anterior to chiasm; bitemporal = chiasm; homonymous = retrochiasmal). Assess pupils (size, light direct and consensual, near response), lids, and the full range of ocular movements. Trigeminal: facial sensation to pin and cotton, corneal reflex, masseter bulk, and the jaw jerk. Facial: observe at rest and with movement (a forehead-sparing pattern indicates an upper-motor-neuron facial lesion, whereas whole-hemiface weakness indicates a peripheral seventh-nerve lesion). Hearing by tuning fork (Weber and Rinne distinguish conductive from sensorineural loss). Palate elevation and gag (only asymmetry is meaningful), and tongue inspection for atrophy, fasciculation, and deviation toward a weak hypoglossal side.

Motor system

Assess bulk, tone, power, and the involuntary repertoire. Inspect for atrophy and fasciculations (lower-motor-neuron signs). Tone distinguishes spasticity (velocity-dependent, corticospinal) from rigidity (lead-pipe or cogwheel, extrapyramidal) and from the flaccidity of acute lower-motor-neuron or acute "spinal shock" lesions. Grade power 0–5 on the MRC scale and record the pattern of weakness, which is more informative than its severity.

Subtle weakness is found, not graded. Pronator drift (the outstretched supinated arm drifts down and pronates) and asymmetric forearm "orbiting" reveal mild corticospinal weakness that MRC testing misses. In the legs, external rotation of the hip at rest and downward drift of the flexed prone leg betray a pyramidal lesion.

Reflexes

Sample the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis (supinator), patellar, and Achilles tendons; facilitate hypoactive reflexes with the Jendrassik manoeuvre. The plantar response is essential: the Babinski sign (dorsiflexion of the great toe with fanning) is a dependable marker of corticospinal-tract damage. Beware mimics — a quick voluntary withdrawal is not pathological, whereas a slow spinal "triple-flexion" response carries the same significance as a Babinski. Loss of superficial reflexes (abdominal, cremasteric), especially when unilateral, supports a corticospinal lesion.

Coordination, sensation & gait

Coordination: finger–nose–finger, heel–knee–shin, and rapid alternating movements test the cerebellum and its connections; interpret only in limbs with adequate power. Sensation is the most subjective portion and is performed last and briefly to limit fatigue and over-reporting — survey pin and light touch over face, arms, trunk, and legs, then map any abnormality to a dermatome, a peripheral-nerve territory, a sensory level, or a hemibody/limb pattern. Add vibration and proprioception (dorsal columns) and temperature (spinothalamic) when a tract or polyneuropathy is in question. Gait and station integrate the whole motor and sensory system and frequently reveal the diagnosis at a glance — hemiparetic circumduction, spastic scissoring, parkinsonian festination, cerebellar wide-based ataxia, sensory (dorsal-column) ataxia with a positive Romberg, steppage of foot drop, and waddling of proximal myopathy each have a signature.

4. The first decision: upper vs lower motor neuron

When weakness is present, the most consequential single distinction is upper- versus lower-motor-neuron, because it splits the neuraxis in two.

FeatureUpper motor neuron (UMN)Lower motor neuron (LMN)
ToneIncreased (spastic, velocity-dependent)Decreased (flaccid)
ReflexesHyperreflexic; Babinski presentHypo- or areflexic; plantar flexor
BulkPreserved (disuse atrophy late)Early, marked atrophy
FasciculationsAbsentPresent
Weakness patternPyramidal: extensors weaker in arms, flexors weaker in legsPattern of the affected root, nerve, or muscle
The clinical caveat. In the first hours of an acute UMN lesion (acute stroke, acute cord compression — "spinal shock"), tone and reflexes may be reduced before spasticity and hyperreflexia evolve. Mixed UMN + LMN signs in the same region without sensory loss should raise motor neuron disease.

Once UMN vs LMN is settled, place the lesion at a level. Work down the axis, asking what each level uniquely produces.

The crossed-signs rule. Ipsilateral face with contralateral body — whether motor or sensory — is brainstem until proven otherwise, because the corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts have already decussated or are about to, while the cranial-nerve nucleus is ipsilateral.

6. Sensory localization & classic cord syndromes

Two great somatosensory systems travel separately in the cord, which makes dissociated sensory loss a powerful localizer. The dorsal columns carry vibration and proprioception, ascend ipsilaterally, and decussate in the medulla. The spinothalamic tract carries pain and temperature and decussates within one to two segments of entry, ascending contralaterally. A lesion that strikes one system and spares the other localizes to the cord or brainstem.

Romberg interpretation. A positive Romberg (falls only with eyes closed) indicates a sensory (dorsal-column or proprioceptive) ataxia, not cerebellar disease — cerebellar patients are unsteady with eyes open and closed.

7. Putting it together: pattern recognition

The mature clinician reasons from syndromes, not isolated signs. A few high-yield syntheses:

  • Hemiparesis with cortical signs → contralateral cortex; without cortical signs and equal face/arm/leg → internal capsule (lacune).
  • Crossed motor or sensory deficit → brainstem; add the specific cranial nerve to pinpoint the level (CN III = midbrain, VI/VII = pons, IX–XII = medulla).
  • Sensory level + paraparesis + sphincter signs → spinal cord; image the cord urgently to exclude compression.
  • Length-dependent stocking-glove sensory loss with absent ankle jerks → polyneuropathy.
  • Fatigable ptosis and diplopia, normal sensation/reflexes → neuromuscular junction.
  • Symmetric proximal weakness, sensation intact → myopathy.
  • Mixed UMN and LMN signs, no sensory loss → anterior horn / motor neuron disease.

8. Pitfalls & non-organic signs

Sensory testing is "subject to overinterpretation and suggestibility" — keep it brief and avoid leading the patient. Recognize patterns that do not respect anatomy: give-way (collapsing) weakness, splitting of midline vibration or pinprick exactly at the midline, non-anatomical sensory boundaries, and Hoover sign (lack of involuntary contralateral hip extension when the "weak" leg is asked to flex against resistance) suggest a functional component — but functional and organic disease coexist, and a functional diagnosis is made on positive rule-in signs, never by exclusion alone. Finally, always reconcile the examination with the history: if the exam contradicts the story, re-take the history rather than forcing the findings.
