Why this module?

Sleep complaints are everywhere in primary care and frequently overlooked. Untreated obstructive sleep apnea silently drives hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and crashes. REM sleep behaviour disorder is a window into pre-clinical Parkinson disease. Insomnia and circadian disorders are highly treatable with non-pharmacological strategies many clinicians under-utilise.

After this module you will:

  • Take a structured sleep history and decide on a working diagnosis at the bedside.
  • Use Epworth, STOP-BANG, ISI, and the IRLSSG criteria fluently.
  • Diagnose and treat OSA, central sleep apnea, and Cheyne-Stokes; know the ASV caveat.
  • Recognise narcolepsy type 1 and arrange MSLT ± CSF orexin.
  • Counsel patients with RBD about synucleinopathy risk and treat safely.
  • Treat RLS with iron and gabapentinoids first, avoiding dopamine-agonist augmentation.
  • Recommend CBT-I first-line for insomnia and prescribe hypnotics rationally.
  • Diagnose and treat circadian rhythm disorders.
The single principle that prevents missed diagnoses: always ask about (1) snoring and witnessed apnoeas, (2) cataplexy on laughter, (3) dream enactment, and (4) restless leg sensations. Plus an Epworth. These five things take 60 seconds and surface the four most-missed sleep diagnoses in primary care: OSA, narcolepsy, RBD, and RLS.

Lessons

1. Sleep architecture and the two-process model
Stage% nightEEG hallmarks
N1~5%Low voltage mixed; slow rolling eyes; hypnic jerks

N3 (SWS)~25%Delta ≥ 20% epoch; most restorative; NREM parasomnias

  • Process C (circadian): SCN → melatonin (peaks ~3 am); entrained by light.
  • Process S (homeostatic): adenosine accumulation during wake.
  • Wake-promoting: orexin (lateral hypothalamus — lost in narcolepsy type 1), histamine, NE, ACh, 5-HT, DA.
  • Sleep-promoting: VLPO (GABA + galanin), adenosine, melatonin.
  • REM atonia: subcoeruleus → glycinergic inhibition of α-motor neurons; loss → RBD.
2. Bedside approach — 8 questions + key exam findings
  1. Main problem — onset, maintenance, EDS, or abnormal behaviour?
  2. Sleep schedule weekdays vs days off (circadian, social jet lag).
  3. Sleep latency, awakenings, total sleep time (insomnia phenotype).
  4. Snoring, witnessed apnoeas, gasping/choking (OSA).
  5. Urge to move legs, evening worsening, relief with movement (RLS).
  6. Falling asleep at the wheel / in conversation (EDS — Epworth).
  7. Dream enactment — punching, kicking, shouting (RBD).
  8. Caffeine, alcohol, drugs (Rx/OTC/recreational), shift work, electronics, naps.

Exam: BP, BMI, neck circumference (> 17 in / 43 cm men; > 16 in / 41 cm women), modified Mallampati, tonsil size, retrognathia, nasal patency; parkinsonism / cognition / autonomic exam; PHQ-9.

OSA definitions: apnea = > 90% airflow drop ≥ 10 s; hypopnea = ≥ 30% drop + ≥ 3% (AASM) / ≥ 4% (Medicare) desat or arousal. AHI: 5–14 mild, 15–29 moderate, ≥ 30 severe.

4. Insomnia — CBT-I first, hypnotics second

Diagnosis: difficulty falling/staying asleep ≥ 3 nights/week ≥ 3 months despite adequate opportunity + daytime consequences. Exclude OSA, RLS, depression, drugs, hyperthyroid, GORD, prostate.

CBT-I components:
  • Stimulus control: bed for sleep + sex only; leave bed after ~20 min not asleep; consistent wake time.
  • Sleep restriction: limit time in bed to actual sleep time → expand as efficiency > 85–90%.
  • Cognitive restructuring + relaxation; sleep hygiene as adjunct.
  • Digital CBT-I apps and brief 4-session CBT-I are evidence-based.

5. Obstructive sleep apnea

~14% men, ~5% women in midlife (likely higher). Drives HTN, AF, stroke, T2DM, MVA. STOP-BANG ≥ 3 → diagnostic test. HSAT for uncomplicated; PSG for complex.

  1. CPAP first-line for moderate-severe; adherence = key challenge (≥ 4 h/night ≥ 70% nights).
  2. BiPAP for CPAP intolerance, high pressures, hypoventilation.
  3. Mandibular advancement device for mild-moderate or CPAP-intolerant.
  4. Weight loss: 10% body weight → ~25–30% AHI reduction.
  5. Positional therapy for supine-related disease.
  6. Surgery: T&A in children (first-line); UPPP, MMA, septoplasty in adults; tracheostomy last resort.
  7. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Inspire) for selected moderate-severe CPAP-intolerant patients (BMI < 32, no concentric palatal collapse).
  8. Modafinil/armodafinil/pitolisant/solriamfetol for residual EDS despite adherent CPAP.
SAVE/ISAACC: CPAP did NOT reduce cardiovascular events in non-sleepy OSA + CV disease. Most benefit is in sleepy patients, severe OSA, treatment-resistant HTN.
6. Central sleep apnea, Cheyne-Stokes & hypoventilation
  • Cheyne-Stokes in HF: crescendo-decrescendo; treat HF; CPAP often helps. ASV is contraindicated if LVEF ≤ 45% (SERVE-HF: increased CV mortality).
  • High-altitude periodic breathing: physiological; acetazolamide effective.
  • Opioid-induced CSA: ataxic / Biot pattern; reduce opioid; CPAP/ASV variable.
  • Treatment-emergent CSA: on CPAP initiation; often resolves spontaneously; ASV if persistent.
  • OHS: BMI ≥ 30 + daytime PaCO₂ ≥ 45 mmHg; BiPAP + weight loss.
  • Neuromuscular hypoventilation (ALS, dystrophies): nocturnal NIV improves survival; trigger criteria orthopnea, FVC < 50%, SNIP < 40 cm H₂O.
7. Narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia

Type 1: orexin loss; HLA-DQB1*06:02 in > 95%; tetrad EDS + cataplexy (pathognomonic) + sleep paralysis + hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations; disrupted nocturnal sleep; refreshing naps; vivid dreams in short naps. MSLT mean SL ≤ 8 + ≥ 2 SOREMPs; CSF orexin < 110 pg/mL.

Type 2: no cataplexy; MSLT criteria same; CSF orexin usually normal; some later develop cataplexy and reclassify.

Idiopathic hypersomnia: long unrefreshing naps, severe sleep inertia, long total sleep; MSLT ≤ 8 min + < 2 SOREMPs OR documented ≥ 11 h/24 h sleep.

SymptomDrug
EDSModafinil, armodafinil, pitolisant, solriamfetol, methylphenidate, sodium oxybate

Behavioural: scheduled 15–20 min naps (refreshing), driving counselling, school/work accommodations.

8. REM sleep behaviour disorder and the synucleinopathy prodrome
  • Dream enactment — punching, kicking, shouting, jumping; bed-partner reports; injuries common; older males predominate.
  • PSG: REM sleep without atonia + clinical history.
  • Distinguish from NREM parasomnias (first half, no recall, confused arousal), nocturnal seizures (stereotyped, brief), severe OSA (respiratory arousals).
Isolated RBD ≈ prodromal synucleinopathy.
  • Up to ~80% convert to PD / DLB / MSA over 10–15 years.
  • Counsel sensitively; offer annual surveillance (cognitive, motor, autonomic); enable research participation.
  • Secondary RBD: SSRIs, SNRIs, mirtazapine, TCAs, narcolepsy, brainstem lesions.

Treatment: bedroom safety first (pad corners, move tables, partner sleeps separately if injury risk). Then melatonin 3–12 mg at bedtime first-line (better tolerability) or clonazepam 0.25–1 mg (historically first-line; falls, daytime sedation, worsens OSA). Reduce/stop SSRI if implicated.

9. Restless legs syndrome / PLMD

IRLSSG criteria (all 5 — URGE mnemonic): Urge to move legs; Rest worsens; Gets better with movement; Evening/night worse; not solely explained by another condition.

Workup: ferritin + TSAT; pregnancy; ESRD; neuropathy; drugs (antihistamines, antidepressants esp. mirtazapine and SSRIs/SNRIs, dopamine antagonists, lithium).

StepDetail
IronReplace if ferritin < 75 ng/mL or TSAT < 20%; oral ferrous sulfate + vitamin C (or alternate-day); IV ferric carboxymaltose for severe/refractory
First-line drugα2δ ligand: gabapentin enacarbil 600 mg, gabapentin 300–1200 mg, pregabalin 75–300 mg at bedtime — preferred over dopamine agonists (no augmentation)

Augmentation: dopamine agonist paradoxical worsening — earlier onset, more severe, spread to upper limbs. Reduce / rotate / discontinue dopamine agonist; switch to gabapentinoid; treat any iron deficit.
10. Parasomnias — NREM vs REM

NREM (disorders of arousal — from N3): sleepwalking, sleep terrors, confusional arousals. First third of night; no recall; sit up confused; family history common. Triggers: sleep deprivation, alcohol, fever, OSA, stress. Management: safety, trigger avoidance, treat OSA; clonazepam for high-injury risk.

REM:

  • RBD (above).
  • Sleep paralysis: hypnagogic/hypnopompic; reassure; regularise sleep; treat OSA.
  • Nightmare disorder (PTSD): image rehearsal therapy first-line; prazosin can reduce trauma-related nightmares.

Other: sleep-related eating disorder, sexsomnia, exploding head syndrome, hypnic jerks.

NREM vs RBD differentiation: NREM = first half + no recall + confused; RBD = second half + vivid recall + dream content matches movement + REM without atonia.

12. Sleep in neurological disease & when to refer
  • PD: RBD often predates motor onset; insomnia, EDS, "sleep attacks" on dopamine agonists, RLS, OSA; cross-ref Module 6.
  • Dementia: sundowning, fragmented sleep, irregular sleep-wake rhythm; non-pharmacological first; avoid benzodiazepines.
  • Stroke: OSA in ~50%; CPAP improves selected outcomes; post-stroke fatigue distinct from EDS.
  • MS: RLS more common; fatigue vs EDS; nocturnal spasms.
  • Epilepsy: sleep deprivation precipitates seizures; nocturnal frontal-lobe seizures misdiagnosed as parasomnia — video EEG helps; many AEDs affect sleep.
When to refer: suspected narcolepsy/IH; significant cataplexy; suspected RBD; OSA with resistant HTN/AF/stroke/MVA risk; severe RLS with augmentation; nocturnal events suggestive of seizures (video EEG); CSA in heart failure (cardiology + sleep medicine).

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 55-year-old male, BMI 34, hypertensive on three antihypertensives, presents with morning headache, fatigue, and his wife reports loud snoring and witnessed apneas

Neck 44 cm, Mallampati IV. ESS 15. STOP-BANG: snore + tired + observed apnea + BP + BMI > 35 (just) + age > 50 + neck > 40 + male = 7/8.

Diagnosis: High probability moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea.

Plan:

  1. Arrange home sleep apnea test (HSAT) — uncomplicated middle-aged adult.
  2. If HSAT positive (AHI ≥ 15): CPAP titration; mask fitting; humidification; behavioural support — target adherence ≥ 4 h/night ≥ 70% nights.
  3. Weight loss counselling (10% body weight → ~25–30% AHI reduction); bariatric surgery discussion if BMI rises > 35–40.
  4. Optimise hypertension management — OSA is a treatable cause of resistant hypertension.
  5. Driving counselling: do not drive when sleepy; legal reporting obligations vary by jurisdiction.
  6. If CPAP intolerant: mandibular advancement device, hypoglossal nerve stimulation (if BMI < 32, no concentric palatal collapse on DISE), UPPP / MMA in select cases.
Case 2 — A 17-year-old girl with falling asleep in school, vivid dreams, episodes where her knees buckle when she laughs hard, and a sense of being unable to move on waking

ESS 22. Daytime naps are refreshing and short, with vivid dreams. Knee buckling lasts seconds, consciousness preserved, triggered by laughter. Sometimes feels paralysed at sleep onset for 30 seconds. HLA-DQB1*06:02 positive.

Diagnosis: Narcolepsy type 1 (with cataplexy).

Plan:

  1. Confirm: overnight PSG (rule out OSA — common mimic) + next-day MSLT (mean SL ≤ 8 min + ≥ 2 SOREMPs). CSF orexin-A < 110 pg/mL if equivocal.
  2. Schedule: regular sleep–wake schedule, planned 15–20 min naps (refreshing).
  3. EDS: modafinil 200 mg morning, titrate; pitolisant or solriamfetol as alternatives.
  4. Cataplexy: pitolisant (also helps EDS) or low-dose SNRI/SSRI (venlafaxine, fluoxetine) initially; sodium oxybate (Xyrem, low-Na Xywav, or once-nightly Lumryz) for severe cataplexy or fragmented sleep.
  5. Driving and school accommodations; tell about the safety implications.
  6. Sleep paralysis / hypnagogic hallucinations: usually improve with SNRI/oxybate; reassurance.
  7. Multidisciplinary support; patient education; address mood/psychosocial impact.
Case 3 — A 68-year-old man's wife reports he has been "fighting in his sleep" — punching, kicking, shouting — over the past 2 years. He has fallen out of bed twice with minor injuries. Awake exam shows mild bradykinesia and reduced arm swing on the right

Dream content involves attackers; he recalls fragments on waking. No daytime sleepiness, no snoring. PSG: REM sleep without atonia + observed dream-enactment behaviour during REM.

Diagnosis: REM sleep behaviour disorder + emerging Parkinson disease — isolated RBD is a prodromal synucleinopathy in up to 80% over 10–15 years.

Plan:

  1. Bedroom safety first: pad sharp corners, move bedside tables, mattress on the floor or bed rails; partner sleeps separately if injury risk persists.
  2. Melatonin 3–12 mg at bedtime — first-line per 2023 AASM RBD guideline (better tolerability than clonazepam).
  3. Alternative: clonazepam 0.25–1 mg at bedtime — historically first-line; falls, daytime sedation, worsens OSA, cognitive effects.
  4. Stop / reduce any SSRI / SNRI / mirtazapine if implicated.
  5. Counsel about the synucleinopathy prodrome — sensitively and honestly. Annual surveillance: motor (UPDRS), cognitive (MoCA, RBANS), autonomic (orthostatic vitals, constipation, urinary, sexual symptoms), olfactory (UPSIT), DaTscan if appropriate.
  6. Refer to movement-disorders neurology; offer research participation in prodromal Parkinson disease cohorts.
  7. Cross-ref Module 6 (PD) and Module 7 (DLB).
Case 4 — A 42-year-old woman has an urge to move her legs in the evening relieved by walking; lying down makes it worse. Her sleep is disrupted. She is on mirtazapine for depression. Ferritin 28 ng/mL, TSAT 14%

All 5 IRLSSG criteria met. Mirtazapine is a known RLS exacerbator.

Diagnosis: Restless legs syndrome (RLS), iron-deficient, exacerbated by mirtazapine.

Plan:

  1. Iron repletion: oral ferrous sulfate 325 mg with vitamin C, ideally alternate-day to improve absorption; target ferritin ≥ 75 ng/mL and TSAT > 20%. If severe, refractory, or oral intolerant: IV ferric carboxymaltose 1000 mg.
  2. Discuss alternative antidepressant — bupropion does not worsen RLS; SSRIs / SNRIs / mirtazapine can worsen.
  3. Sleep hygiene; reduce caffeine, alcohol, nicotine.
  4. First-line drug if needed (post-AASM 2024): α2δ ligand — gabapentin enacarbil 600 mg, gabapentin 300–600 mg, or pregabalin 75–150 mg at bedtime.
  5. Avoid starting dopamine agonist as first-line (augmentation risk). Reserve for refractory cases at the lowest effective dose, with ICD warnings.
  6. Refractory / severe: low-dose opioid (oxycodone, methadone, tramadol) under specialist supervision.
Case 5 — A 28-year-old presents with 9 months of difficulty falling asleep (latency > 60 minutes), multiple awakenings, ISI 19, no snoring, no leg symptoms, mild work stress, drinks 4 cups of coffee per day, evening alcohol "to relax"

No cataplexy, no dream enactment. PHQ-9 mild. Sleep diary shows variable bedtime, daytime naps to compensate.

Diagnosis: Chronic insomnia disorder (ISI moderate clinical), with behavioural maintaining factors.

Plan:

  1. Education: caffeine half-life and evening alcohol disrupt sleep architecture (alcohol shortens REM and fragments second half of night).
  2. CBT-I as first-line — refer to therapist or digital CBT-I (Sleepio, SHUTi); brief 4-session CBT-I works.
  3. Stimulus control: bed only for sleep + sex; leave bed if not asleep ~20 min; consistent wake time.
  4. Sleep restriction: limit time in bed to actual sleep + 30 min; expand as efficiency rises.
  5. Sleep hygiene: cut caffeine after noon; no evening alcohol; reduce screens before bed.
  6. If pharmacotherapy desired short-term: dual orexin antagonist (lemborexant, daridorexant, suvorexant) or low-dose doxepin 3 mg for maintenance; avoid Z-drugs and benzodiazepines as long-term agents.
  7. Address depression/anxiety if PHQ-9/GAD-7 elevated.
Case 6 — A 16-year-old "cannot fall asleep" until 3 am and "cannot wake" before noon. School performance is suffering. On weekends he sleeps from 3 am to noon and feels well. Tries melatonin 5 mg at 10 pm without effect

Sleep quality good when allowed his preferred schedule. No snoring, no cataplexy, no leg symptoms, no dream enactment. Examination unremarkable.

Diagnosis: Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder (DSWPD).

Plan:

  1. Two-week sleep diary + actigraphy to confirm phase delay and rule out chronic insufficient sleep.
  2. Morning bright-light therapy: 10,000 lux for 30–60 min on waking (light box) — entrains the SCN earlier.
  3. Low-dose melatonin 0.3–0.5 mg ~5–6 hours before desired sleep onset (NOT at bedtime, NOT high-dose — high doses are ineffective for phase shift and can be sedating). The 5 mg at 10 pm timing was too late and too high.
  4. Evening blue-light avoidance; screen filters; consistent wake time including weekends (no > 1 hour later).
  5. Gradual advance of sleep schedule by 15 min every 2–3 days.
  6. School accommodation for later start when possible.
  7. Avoid sedative-hypnotics (often refractory in DSWPD and don't fix the underlying phase delay).
