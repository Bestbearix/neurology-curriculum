1. Definitions: seizure, epilepsy, provoked vs unprovoked

A seizure is the transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. Epilepsy (ILAE practical definition) is diagnosed by any of: (1) two unprovoked (or reflex) seizures >24 h apart; (2) one unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk comparable to the general two-seizure risk (≥60%) over 10 years; or (3) diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome. The crucial early fork is provoked (acute symptomatic) seizures — driven by a transient insult (hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, alcohol withdrawal, drugs, eclampsia, acute stroke or infection) and treated by correcting the cause — versus unprovoked seizures, which carry recurrence risk and define epilepsy.

Why the distinction drives management: a single provoked seizure from a correctable metabolic cause does not by itself warrant chronic antiseizure medication, whereas an unprovoked seizure with an epileptogenic lesion on MRI or epileptiform EEG carries a high recurrence risk and usually does.

2. ILAE 2017 classification — three levels

The 2017 ILAE framework classifies in three sequential levels, with etiology considered at every step because it carries therapeutic consequences.

LevelCategoriesResident action
1 · Seizure typeFocal onset · Generalized onset · Unknown onset (each further motor / non-motor; focal further by awareness)Classify the event from semiology + EEG
2 · Epilepsy typeFocal · Generalized · Combined generalized & focal · UnknownFrame the workup and drug choice
3 · Epilepsy syndromeSpecific syndromic diagnosis (e.g., JME, childhood absence, mesial temporal lobe epilepsy)Refine prognosis, drug, and surgical candidacy

The six etiologic groups (assessed throughout) are structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, and unknown. The term developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) recognizes that both the underlying condition and the epileptic activity contribute to impairment.

3. Seizure types & semiology

The 2017 operational seizure classification begins with onset. Focal-onset seizures are subclassified by awareness (focal aware, replacing "simple partial"; focal impaired awareness, replacing "complex partial") and by the first prominent sign (motor vs non-motor). A focal seizure that spreads is a focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure (replacing "secondarily generalized"). Generalized-onset seizures engage bilateral networks from the start (tonic–clonic, absence, myoclonic, tonic, atonic, and others). When onset is missed, the seizure is unknown onset. Some types — epileptic spasms, tonic seizures — may be of focal or generalized onset.

Mimics matter. Convulsive syncope (brief myoclonic jerks after a faint), psychogenic non-epileptic events (PNES — closed eyes, asynchronous/pelvic-thrusting movements, fluctuating course, preserved awareness during bilateral motor activity), and migraine/TIA can masquerade as seizures. Video-EEG is the arbiter.

4. Syndromes & etiologies

Syndromic diagnosis pairs seizure type(s) with age of onset, EEG signature, imaging, comorbidity, and prognosis. The ILAE has published dedicated syndrome classifications for neonatal/infantile onset and for childhood onset (both in the resource set). High-yield adult-relevant syndromes:

  • Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy — teens; morning myoclonus, GTCs, absences; photosensitivity; lifelong; valproate highly effective but teratogenic — use levetiracetam/lamotrigine in people of childbearing potential.
  • Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis — focal impaired-awareness seizures, often drug-resistant; the prototypical surgically remediable syndrome.
  • Childhood absence epilepsy — ethosuximide (absence only) or valproate; lamotrigine adjunct.
  • Lennox-Gastaut / DEEs — mixed seizure types, cognitive impairment, slow spike-wave.

5. First seizure: workup & when to treat

After a first unprovoked seizure: history and witness account, a focused exam, glucose and electrolytes, toxicology, and pregnancy testing as indicated; EEG (epileptiform discharges raise recurrence risk) and MRI brain (epilepsy protocol) to find an epileptogenic lesion. The decision to start an antiseizure medication after a single unprovoked seizure balances recurrence risk against drug burden: an abnormal EEG, an MRI lesion, a nocturnal seizure, or a significant prior brain insult shift toward treatment. Counsel on driving restrictions (jurisdiction-specific), seizure precautions, sleep, alcohol, and triggers.

6. Antiseizure-medication selection

Match the drug to the epilepsy type, the patient's comorbidities and childbearing potential, and the side-effect/interaction profile. Monotherapy is the goal; about half of patients are controlled on the first appropriate drug.

SANAD I (Lancet 2007) — long-term outcomes of standard vs newer ASMs. Arm A (focal/unclassified): lamotrigine was clinically better than carbamazepine for time to treatment failure → a first-line choice for focal epilepsy. Arm B (generalized/unclassified): valproate was superior (better time to treatment failure and to 12-month remission) → first-line for generalized epilepsy where appropriate.
Monotherapy network meta-analysis (individual-participant data). For focal seizures, lamotrigine performs best for treatment failure (driven by tolerability), with levetiracetam comparable (HR LTG vs LEV 1.01); carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, valproate, phenytoin and topiramate fare worse on failure-for-any-reason. For generalized seizures, valproate remains most effective. NICE-type guidance: lamotrigine or levetiracetam first-line for focal; valproate for generalized (with the teratogenicity caveat).

Drug pitfalls. Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin and tiagabine can worsen absence and myoclonic seizures — avoid in generalized epilepsies. Lamotrigine requires slow titration (Stevens-Johnson risk); valproate is teratogenic and causes neurodevelopmental harm; carbamazepine/phenytoin are strong enzyme inducers (interactions, including with contraceptives). Screen HLA-B*15:02 before carbamazepine in at-risk Asian populations.

7. Status epilepticus — the stepwise protocol & ESETT

The ILAE 2015 operational definition is built on two time points: t1 (the time abnormally prolonged seizure activity is unlikely to stop spontaneously and treatment should start — 5 minutes for generalized convulsive seizures) and t2 (the time at which long-term consequences begin — 30 minutes for convulsive status). Treat from 5 minutes; do not wait for 30.

PhaseTimeTreatment
Stabilization0–5 minABCs, O₂, IV access, fingerstick glucose, thiamine if indicated, monitoring
First-line5–20 minBenzodiazepine, adequately dosed: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg/dose); or IM midazolam 10 mg; or IV diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg
Second-line20–40 minIV ASM: levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, OR valproate — all comparable (ESETT)
Third-line (refractory)>40 minAnesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital) with continuous EEG
ESETT (the Established Status Epilepticus Treatment Trial). A 3-arm, double-blind, comparative-effectiveness RCT of levetiracetam vs fosphenytoin vs valproate as second-line therapy after benzodiazepines found no significant difference — each stopped status and improved responsiveness in roughly half of patients. Practical implication: choose by availability and patient factors (e.g., avoid valproate in pregnancy/hepatic disease; fosphenytoin cautions in cardiac disease).
The commonest error is under-dosing the benzodiazepine (and then prematurely escalating). Give the full weight-based first-line dose before moving on. Pre-hospital IM midazolam is effective when IV access is delayed.
Refractory & super-refractory SE. Refractory = failure of first- and second-line therapy; super-refractory = continuing or recurring ≥24 h after anesthesia. Ongoing seizure activity itself drives pharmacoresistance (receptor trafficking), underscoring early, adequate treatment and continuous-EEG monitoring. Always search for and treat the underlying cause (including autoimmune/NORSE).

8. Drug-resistant epilepsy & surgery

Drug-resistant epilepsy (ILAE): failure of two appropriately chosen, adequately dosed, tolerated ASM regimens to achieve sustained seizure freedom. These patients should be referred to a comprehensive epilepsy center for pre-surgical evaluation (video-EEG, MRI, neuropsychology, sometimes PET/SPECT and intracranial EEG).

Surgery evidence. Randomized and controlled data support anterior temporal lobectomy for drug-resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy as superior to continued medical therapy for seizure freedom (the resource set includes the RCT assessments and the AAN practice parameter on temporal/neocortical resection). Newer minimally invasive options — including SEEG-guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation, compared with anterior temporal lobectomy in a pilot RCT — and laser interstitial thermal therapy expand the toolkit, trading some efficacy for lower morbidity. Palliative options (vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, deep brain stimulation, callosotomy) help non-resective candidates.

Don't delay referral. Surgically remediable syndromes (especially MTLE with hippocampal sclerosis) are often left on escalating polytherapy for years; early referral after two failed drugs improves the odds of seizure freedom and quality of life.

9. Special populations

Pregnancy & childbearing potential: avoid valproate (highest major-congenital-malformation and neurodevelopmental risk); levetiracetam and lamotrigine are preferred; prescribe folate; lamotrigine levels fall in pregnancy and need monitoring. Status in pregnancy threatens mother and fetus — treat promptly; benzodiazepines and levetiracetam are reasonable. Elderly: lower seizure threshold, more provoked seizures (stroke, metabolic), heightened drug sensitivity and interactions — levetiracetam and lamotrigine are generally well tolerated. SUDEP (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy) risk rises with frequent GTCs and nocturnal seizures — counsel patients and optimize control.
