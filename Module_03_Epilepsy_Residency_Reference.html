1 Definitions: seizure, epilepsy, provoked vs unprovoked

▎Key points
  • Seizure = transient signs/symptoms from abnormal excessive/synchronous neuronal activity. Epilepsy = an enduring predisposition to seizures.
  • ILAE epilepsy diagnosis: (1) two unprovoked seizures >24 h apart; OR (2) one unprovoked seizure with ≥60% 10-year recurrence risk; OR (3) an epilepsy syndrome.
  • Provoked (acute symptomatic) seizures — correct the cause, usually no chronic ASM; unprovoked seizures carry recurrence risk and define epilepsy.
Provoked (acute symptomatic)Unprovoked
TriggerTransient insult (hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, alcohol withdrawal, drugs, eclampsia, acute stroke/infection)None identifiable, or remote/structural
Recurrence riskLow once cause is correctedHigher — especially with EEG epileptiform activity or an MRI lesion

Why the distinction drives management: a single provoked seizure from a correctable metabolic cause does not by itself warrant long-term ASM; an unprovoked seizure with an epileptogenic lesion or epileptiform EEG usually does.

2 ILAE 2017 classification — three levels

▎Key points
  • Classify in three sequential levels — seizure type → epilepsy type → epilepsy syndrome — with etiology considered at every step.
  • Six etiologic groups: structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown.
  • Terminology updates: "partial" → focal; "simple/complex partial" → focal aware / focal impaired awareness; "secondarily generalized" → focal to bilateral tonic–clonic.
LevelCategoriesResident action
1 · Seizure typeFocal onset · Generalized onset · Unknown onset (each motor / non-motor; focal by awareness)Classify the event from semiology + EEG
2 · Epilepsy typeFocal · Generalized · Combined generalized & focal · UnknownFrame the workup and drug choice
3 · Epilepsy syndromeSpecific diagnosis (JME, childhood absence, mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, Lennox-Gastaut…)Refine prognosis, drug, surgical candidacy

The term developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) recognizes that both the underlying condition and the epileptic activity contribute to impairment.

3 Seizure types & semiology

▎Key points
  • Start with onset (focal / generalized / unknown), then awareness and the first prominent sign (motor vs non-motor).
  • Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic replaces "secondarily generalized."
  • Video-EEG is the arbiter for mimics (syncope, PNES, migraine/TIA).

Mimics matter. Convulsive syncope (brief jerks after a faint), PNES (closed eyes, asynchronous/pelvic-thrusting movements, fluctuating course, preserved awareness during bilateral motor activity), and migraine/TIA masquerade as seizures — video-EEG resolves it.

5 First seizure: workup & when to treat

▎Key points
  • Workup: witnessed account, glucose/electrolytes, tox, pregnancy test as indicated, EEG and MRI (epilepsy protocol).
  • Treat after a single unprovoked seizure when recurrence risk is high: epileptiform EEG, MRI lesion, nocturnal seizure, or significant prior brain insult.
  • Counsel on driving restrictions (jurisdiction-specific), sleep, alcohol, and triggers.

The decision to start an ASM after one unprovoked seizure balances recurrence risk against drug burden — an abnormal EEG or an epileptogenic MRI lesion roughly doubles recurrence risk and shifts toward treatment.

6 Antiseizure-medication selection

▎Key points
  • Focal epilepsy: lamotrigine or levetiracetam first-line (SANAD + monotherapy meta-analysis).
  • Generalized epilepsy: valproate most effective — but avoid in people of childbearing potential (teratogenic).
  • Monotherapy is the goal; ~half are controlled on the first appropriate drug.
SANAD I (Lancet 2007). Arm A (focal/unclassified): lamotrigine clinically better than carbamazepine for time to treatment failure → first-line for focal. Arm B (generalized/unclassified): valproate superior (treatment failure & 12-month remission) → first-line for generalized where appropriate.
Monotherapy network meta-analysis (IPD). For focal seizures, lamotrigine performs best for treatment failure (tolerability-driven), with levetiracetam comparable (HR LTG vs LEV ≈ 1.01); CBZ/OXC/VPA/PHT/TPM fare worse. For generalized seizures, valproate remains most effective.
ScenarioPreferredNotes
Focal epilepsyLamotrigine or levetiracetamLTG better tolerated; titrate slowly (rash/SJS)
Generalized epilepsyValproate (if not childbearing potential)Most effective; otherwise LEV/LTG
AbsenceEthosuximide or valproateEthosuximide for absence-only
Childbearing potentialLevetiracetam, lamotrigineAvoid valproate; folate; specialist input
Drug pitfalls. Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin and tiagabine can worsen absence and myoclonic seizures — avoid in generalized epilepsies. Lamotrigine needs slow titration (Stevens-Johnson risk); valproate is teratogenic + neurodevelopmentally harmful; CBZ/PHT are strong enzyme inducers (interactions incl. contraceptives). Screen HLA-B*15:02 before carbamazepine in at-risk Asian populations.

7 Status epilepticus — the stepwise protocol

▎Key points
  • Timepoints: t1 = 5 min (treat), t2 = 30 min (injury) for convulsive SE.
  • Give a full-dose benzodiazepine first — under-dosing is the commonest error.
  • Second-line = levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate (all comparable — ESETT).
  • Refractory (>40 min) → anesthetic infusion + continuous EEG.
PhaseTimeTreatment
Stabilization0–5 minABCs, O₂, IV access, fingerstick glucose, thiamine if indicated, monitoring
First-line5–20 minBenzodiazepine, full dose: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg/dose); or IM midazolam 10 mg; or IV diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg
Second-line20–40 minIV ASM: levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, OR valproate — all comparable (ESETT)
Third-line (refractory)>40 minAnesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol, pentobarbital) + continuous EEG
ESETT. A 3-arm, double-blind comparative-effectiveness RCT of levetiracetam vs fosphenytoin vs valproate as second-line therapy found no significant difference — each stopped status and improved responsiveness in roughly half of patients. Choose by availability and patient factors (avoid valproate in pregnancy/hepatic disease; fosphenytoin cautions in cardiac disease).
The commonest error is under-dosing the benzodiazepine and then prematurely escalating. Give the full weight-based first-line dose before moving on. Pre-hospital IM midazolam is effective when IV access is delayed.
Refractory vs super-refractory. Refractory = fails first- and second-line; super-refractory = continuing/recurring ≥24 h after anesthesia. Ongoing seizure activity drives pharmacoresistance (receptor trafficking) — treat early and adequately; hunt for the cause (including autoimmune/NORSE).

8 Drug-resistant epilepsy & surgery

▎Key points
  • Drug-resistant epilepsy (ILAE): failure of two appropriately chosen, adequately dosed, tolerated ASM regimens to achieve sustained seizure freedom → refer to a comprehensive epilepsy center.
  • Anterior temporal lobectomy is superior to continued medical therapy for drug-resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy.
  • Refer early — surgically remediable syndromes are often left on years of polytherapy.
Surgery evidence. Randomized/controlled data support anterior temporal lobectomy for drug-resistant MTLE (RCT assessments + AAN practice parameter in the resource set). Minimally invasive options — SEEG-guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (pilot RCT vs ATL) and laser interstitial thermal therapy — trade some efficacy for lower morbidity. Palliative options: vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, deep brain stimulation, callosotomy.

9 Landmark trials — who was included & excluded

▎Key points
  • The eligibility criteria are the indication — the ESETT and SANAD populations define where their conclusions apply.
  • ESETT answers "benzodiazepine-refractory convulsive status, which second-line drug?"; SANAD answers "newly diagnosed epilepsy, which first-line drug by seizure type?"

ESETT (established status epilepticus, 2nd-line drug)

IncludedExcluded / design
  • Age ≥2 years (children and adults)
  • Established convulsive status epilepticus — persisting/recurrent convulsions despite an adequate dose of a benzodiazepine (≥ minimum cumulative dose)
  • Randomized to a single weight-based dose of levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate, infused over 10 min (double-blind)
  • Seizure stopped by benzodiazepines (i.e., not "established"); known allergy/contraindication to a study drug
  • Precipitant needing specific therapy (e.g., major trauma, hypoglycemia) at discretion; pregnancy (per protocol)
  • Primary endpoint: clinical cessation of seizures and improving responsiveness at 60 min without additional ASM

Result: ~47% seizure cessation with each of the three drugs — no significant difference → choose by availability and contraindications.

SANAD (newly diagnosed epilepsy, 1st-line drug)

ArmIncluded populationComparison & result
Arm APatients for whom carbamazepine would be standard — i.e., focal (partial) epilepsy (88% partial, 10% unclassified in the cohort), clinician uncertain of best drugCBZ vs gabapentin vs lamotrigine vs oxcarbazepine vs topiramate → lamotrigine best for time to treatment failure
Arm BPatients for whom valproate would be standard — i.e., generalized or unclassifiable epilepsy (63% idiopathic generalized, 25% unclassified)Valproate vs lamotrigine vs topiramate → valproate best for treatment failure and 12-month remission

Excluded: patients where the clinician was already sure of the best drug, or with a clear contraindication to a study drug. SANAD II later reaffirmed lamotrigine for focal and valproate for generalized epilepsy.

Reading criteria on rounds: ESETT applies only after an adequate benzodiazepine has failed (established SE) — it does not sanction skipping or under-dosing the benzodiazepine. SANAD's arms map to seizure type: don't apply the "valproate wins" result to a focal epilepsy, or the "lamotrigine wins" result to generalized epilepsy.

11 Special populations

▎Key points
  • Pregnancy / childbearing potential: avoid valproate; prefer levetiracetam/lamotrigine; folate; LTG levels fall in pregnancy (monitor).
  • Elderly: lower threshold, more provoked seizures; LEV/LTG well tolerated.
  • SUDEP risk rises with frequent GTCs and nocturnal seizures — counsel and optimize control.

Status epilepticus in pregnancy threatens mother and fetus — treat promptly (benzodiazepines and levetiracetam are reasonable). In the elderly, watch drug interactions and heightened CNS sensitivity.
