1. Defining dementia, MCI & the cognitive workup

Major neurocognitive disorder (dementia) is acquired decline in ≥1 cognitive domain (memory, executive, language, visuospatial, attention, social cognition) severe enough to impair independence; mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is decline beyond expected for age that does not impair function. The residency task is to (1) confirm a cognitive disorder and its severity, (2) profile the domain pattern and tempo, and (3) reach a syndromic and etiologic diagnosis. Cornerstones: a collateral history, cognitive testing (MoCA/MMSE and domain-specific tasks), a focused neurologic exam, laboratory screen (TSH, B12, metabolic, ± HIV/syphilis), and structural MRI; add CSF/blood biomarkers, FDG-PET, amyloid PET, DaTscan, EEG, or genetics as the syndrome dictates.

Tempo triages the differential. Years of insidious amnestic decline → Alzheimer disease; stepwise or vascular-risk-linked → vascular; months (rapidly progressive) → prion, autoimmune, neoplastic, infectious, toxic-metabolic — an urgent, treatable-until-proven-otherwise workup.

2. Reversible & treatable causes — do not miss

Before attributing decline to a neurodegenerative disease, exclude the treatable: depression (pseudodementia), medication effects (anticholinergics, sedatives), B12/folate deficiency, thyroid disease, normal-pressure hydrocephalus (gait apraxia, urinary incontinence, cognitive slowing — the "wet, wobbly, wacky" triad), obstructive sleep apnea, chronic subdural hematoma, CNS infection (neurosyphilis, HIV), autoimmune/limbic encephalitis, and heavy alcohol use. Many are partially or fully reversible if caught early.

3. Alzheimer disease: biology & the revised criteria

Alzheimer disease (AD) is defined pathologically by amyloid-β plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles with synaptic and neuronal loss, clinically most often an amnestic syndrome (with atypical variants: posterior cortical atrophy, logopenic aphasia, dysexecutive/behavioral). Risk is dominated by age and the APOE ε4 allele (and rare autosomal-dominant APP/PSEN1/PSEN2 forms).

Revised diagnostic & staging criteria (provided). AD is now framed as a biological entity defined by biomarkers, with disease staged across the continuum (preclinical → MCI → dementia). Core 1 biomarkers capture the amyloid-β pathway (amyloid PET, CSF, and blood phospho-tau species); tau and neurodegeneration markers add staging (the amyloid–tau–neurodegeneration, "ATN", logic). The provided evaluations of these revised criteria stress that a biomarker-defined diagnosis must still be integrated with the clinical picture and used cautiously outside research.
Biology ≠ symptoms. A positive amyloid biomarker in a cognitively unimpaired person indicates AD pathology, not dementia. Avoid labeling asymptomatic people with "Alzheimer disease" on biomarkers alone — a key controversy in the revised-criteria debate.

4. Blood biomarkers & the diagnostic pathway

Plasma biomarkers are transforming the workup by offering an accurate, scalable alternative to CSF and amyloid PET.

Plasma p-tau217 (provided). Across memory-clinic and primary-care cohorts, plasma p-tau217 detects AD pathology with high accuracy (areas under the curve ~0.9; positive predictive values ~89–95% in specialty care, ~82% and overall accuracy ~85% in primary care), and newer species (e.g., eMTBR-tau243) aid biological staging. A blood test for AD has been cleared for primary-care use, though the provided commentaries caution about appropriate patient selection, confirmatory testing, and pre-test probability.
Use biomarkers to answer a question, not to screen. Order plasma p-tau217 (or CSF/PET) when a positive or negative result will change management — e.g., confirming AD pathology before offering anti-amyloid therapy — not indiscriminately in the worried well.

5. Alzheimer therapy: symptomatic & anti-amyloid

Symptomatic

Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild–moderate AD and the NMDA antagonist memantine for moderate–severe disease provide modest symptomatic benefit; combine and manage vascular risk, mood, sleep, and behavioral symptoms (non-pharmacologic first).

Disease-modifying anti-amyloid antibodies

Lecanemab & donanemab (provided). These anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies remove amyloid plaques and modestly slow clinical decline in early symptomatic AD (e.g., lecanemab ~27% slowing of CDR-SB over 18 months in its pivotal trial; donanemab in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2). They require confirmed amyloid positivity and carry the hallmark risk of ARIA.
ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities). ARIA-E (vasogenic edema/effusion) and ARIA-H (microhemorrhage/hemosiderosis) are usually asymptomatic but can be serious. Risk is highest in APOE ε4 homozygotes; management requires baseline and serial surveillance MRI, avoiding/again-caution with anticoagulation, and protocolized dose-holding. Infusion reactions also occur. The provided memory-clinic experience shows these therapies are deliverable in practice with careful monitoring.
Regulatory reality. Approvals differ by region — the FDA approved lecanemab and donanemab, while the EMA response has been more restrictive (the provided commentary notes an EMA refusal/limited position on donanemab). Counsel patients on modest benefit, real burden/risk, and eligibility.
Not everything works — EVOKE / EVOKE+ (provided). Two phase-3 trials of oral semaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) in early symptomatic AD found it was not efficacious in slowing clinical progression (CDR-SB) — a reminder that epidemiologic signal does not guarantee trial benefit.

7. Rapidly progressive dementia, CJD & autoimmune encephalitis

A dementia evolving over weeks to months is a neurologic emergency requiring a broad, urgent workup (the "VITAMINS" differential — Vascular, Infectious, Toxic-metabolic, Autoimmune, Metastatic/neoplastic, Iatrogenic, Neurodegenerative-prion, Systemic). Two high-yield poles:

Prion disease (Creutzfeldt-Jakob)

Sporadic CJD presents as rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus, ataxia, and behavioral/mood change (which can predate cognitive signs). Diagnostics: MRI DWI cortical ribboning and basal-ganglia hyperintensity (the provided case highlights precentral-gyrus/cortical DWI signal), RT-QuIC and 14-3-3 in CSF, and EEG periodic sharp-wave complexes. It is uniformly fatal with short survival (median a few months in the sporadic form).

Autoimmune / limbic encephalitis

Diagnostic criteria (Graus/Dalmau, provided). Subacute limbic encephalitis (memory loss, seizures, psychiatric change) with MRI/CSF/EEG support can be diagnosed as possible, probable (including antibody-negative), or definite autoimmune encephalitis, enabling empiric immunotherapy before antibody results return. The provided papers emphasize careful application of the antibody-negative criteria to avoid misdiagnosis, and note the tight link between autoimmune encephalitis and seizures/epilepsy (cross-links to Module 3). Test for NMDA-R, LGI1, CASPR2, GABA-B and others; search for an associated tumor (e.g., ovarian teratoma with NMDA-R).
Treatable dementia mustn't be missed. In any subacute/rapidly progressive dementia, send autoimmune/paraneoplastic panels and consider a trial of immunotherapy — a reversible cause is worth an aggressive search.

8. Risk reduction & care

Dementia prevention is now a policy priority. The provided Nottingham consensus on dementia risk-reduction policy and the broader modifiable-risk literature support addressing hypertension, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, excess alcohol, hearing loss, depression, social isolation, education, air pollution, and traumatic brain injury across the life course. Beyond drugs, high-value care means caregiver support, advance-care planning, driving and safety assessment, managing behavioral and psychological symptoms non-pharmacologically first, and treating comorbid depression, pain, and sleep disorders.
