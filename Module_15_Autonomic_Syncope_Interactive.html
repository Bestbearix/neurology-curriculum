Why this module?

Syncope is one of the commonest ED presentations and one of the most over-investigated; the dichotomy that matters at the front door is benign reflex syncope vs cardiac syncope. Autonomic disorders are hugely under-recognised because their features cross many systems — orthostatic dizziness, post-prandial fatigue, urinary symptoms, dry eyes/mouth, abnormal sweating — and are often dismissed as non-specific. They're highly treatable when correctly identified.

After this module you will:

  • Triage transient loss of consciousness reliably and use the San Francisco and Canadian Syncope Risk Scores.
  • Identify red-flag features that mandate cardiac workup and admission.
  • Measure and interpret orthostatic vitals; distinguish neurogenic from non-neurogenic OH.
  • Diagnose POTS using consensus criteria; outline a graded, exercise-led management plan.
  • Recognise PAF, MSA, PD, and DLB autonomic features and use MIBG to differentiate.
  • Recognise and treat autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, and amyloid autonomic neuropathy.
  • Diagnose paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity using Baguley criteria and treat with morphine, propranolol, gabapentin, and clonidine.
  • Recognise and treat autonomic dysreflexia and GBS dysautonomia.
The single principle that prevents disasters: in any TLoC, ask (1) is it true syncope? (2) was there a prodrome? (3) any red flags (exertion, supine, palpitation, structural heart disease, family history of sudden cardiac death < 40)? If yes to any red flag → ECG + monitoring + cardiology pathway. If clear vasovagal pattern + no red flags → counsel, do not pan-scan.

Baroreflex: carotid sinus + aortic arch → CN IX/X → NTS → vagal output (↓ HR) AND inhibits RVLM (↓ sympathetic → vasodilation). Failure anywhere → OH.

Central autonomic network: insula (right = sympathetic; right insular stroke can cause arrhythmias), hypothalamus, amygdala, anterior cingulate; brainstem NTS, nucleus ambiguus, RVLM, DMV.

3. Syncope workup & risk scores
  1. Detailed history (patient + witness) — prodrome, trigger, posture, exertion, duration, recovery, family history.
  2. Examination — orthostatic vitals at 1, 3, and 5 minutes; cardiac (AS, HCM); neurological.
  3. 12-lead ECG in every patient — long QT, Brugada, HCM, WPW, AV block, ARVC.
  4. Bedside glucose; pregnancy test in reproductive-age women.
  5. Echo if abnormal ECG/exam/structural concern.
  6. Holter / event recorder / implantable loop recorder for recurrent unexplained syncope.
  7. Tilt-table only after cardiac causes excluded.
  8. Brain imaging / EEG only for clear neurological features (focal deficit, prolonged confusion, witnessed seizure).

San Francisco Syncope Rule (CHESS) — any one: CHF history, Haematocrit < 30%, ECG abnormal, Shortness of breath, Systolic BP < 90 at triage → 7-day serious outcome risk (sensitivity ~96%).

Canadian Syncope Risk Score — see the dedicated tab. Risk bands: ≤ 0 low (~0.4–0.7%); 1–3 medium; 4–5 high; ≥ 6 very high (~28% 30-day SAE).

Red flags mandating admission: syncope during exertion or supine, preceding palpitation, family history SCD < 40 y, structural heart disease, abnormal ECG (long QT, Brugada, AV block, NSVT, pre-excitation, RBBB+LAFB), syncope with injury or no warning ("drop attacks"), new murmur or signs of CHF.
4. Tilt-table & autonomic function tests
  • Tilt-table test: head-up 60–70° × 20–45 min ± isoproterenol or nitroglycerin provocation. For recurrent syncope of unclear cause after negative cardiac workup.
  • Active stand: BP/HR at 1, 3, 5 min — cheaper; 5-min reading detects delayed OH (PD).
  • HRV tests: deep breathing (parasympathetic), Valsalva ratio (combined), 30:15 ratio (vagal).
  • QSART for sudomotor / small-fibre.
  • Plasma catecholamines: low standing NE → postganglionic failure (PAF, PD); normal supine NE + poor rise → central failure (MSA).
  • 123I-MIBG cardiac scintigraphy: reduced uptake in PD/PAF/DLB (postganglionic); normal in MSA (preganglionic) — discriminating test.
5. Orthostatic hypotension — definitions and neurogenic vs not
  • Classic OH: SBP fall ≥ 20 or DBP fall ≥ 10 within 3 minutes of standing/tilt.
  • Initial OH: transient drop within 15 s; younger; resolves by ~30 s.
  • Delayed OH: drop after 3 minutes — common in PD; need 5-minute reading or tilt.
HR-response trick: ≥ 15 bpm rise with the BP drop → volume / drug cause. < 15 bpm rise (or HR-to-systolic-drop ratio < 0.5) → neurogenic OH.
Non-neurogenicNeurogenic
Volume depletion, anaemia, diuretics, antihypertensives, vasodilators, α-blockers, TCAs/MAOIs, antipsychotics, dopamine agonistsPAF, MSA, PD, DLB, diabetic / amyloid / paraneoplastic / Sjögren / AAG, GBS, SCI, MS
6. Management of OH and supine HTN
  1. Non-pharmacological first:
    • Review and reduce offending drugs.
    • Fluid 2–2.5 L/day; salt 6–10 g/day (avoid in HF/CKD).
    • Bolus 0.5 L water in < 5 min raises BP within 5–10 min.
    • Counter-pressure manoeuvres (leg cross + tense, squat, hand grip, abdominal contraction).
    • Compression: abdominal binder > thigh-high stockings.
    • Head of bed up 10–20° at night (reduces supine HTN and nocturnal natriuresis).
    • Avoid hot environments, large meals (postprandial), Valsalva.
  2. Pharmacotherapy:
    • Midodrine 2.5–10 mg po TID — last dose by 6 pm; warn against lying down within 4 h.
    • Droxidopa 100–600 mg TID — last dose 3 h before bed; FDA-approved for neurogenic OH.
    • Fludrocortisone 0.1–0.2 mg/day — monitor K, weight, watch for CHF.
    • Pyridostigmine 30–60 mg TID — modest, less supine HTN; useful adjunct.
    • Specialist options: octreotide, atomoxetine, desmopressin, EPO.
  3. Supine hypertension: head of bed up; carb snack at bedtime; short-acting evening antihypertensive only if SBP > 160–180; avoid long-acting agents (carry-over morning OH).
7. POTS — postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome

Consensus criteria: sustained HR rise ≥ 30 bpm (≥ 40 in adolescents) within 10 minutes of standing/tilt, no > 20 mmHg SBP drop, chronic symptoms ≥ 3 months, no other explanation.

Young women predominantly; often triggered by viral illness (incl. post-COVID), surgery, trauma, pregnancy. Subtypes: neuropathic, hyperadrenergic (NE > 600 pg/mL on standing, HTN), hypovolaemic. Overlaps with EDS, MCAS, ME/CFS, post-acute COVID.

  1. Non-pharmacological first: salt 6–10 g/day + fluid 2–3 L/day; compression (abdominal binder + waist-high stockings); graduated exercise (recumbent → upright over months) — the most effective long-term intervention; avoid prolonged standing in heat.
  2. Pharmacotherapy: low-dose propranolol 10–20 mg TID/QID or ivabradine 2.5–7.5 mg BID; fludrocortisone; midodrine (especially neuropathic); pyridostigmine; methyldopa / clonidine for hyperadrenergic; IV saline boluses for acute flares.
  3. Address mast-cell comorbidity (H1 + H2 antihistamines) and mood as appropriate.
8. Chronic autonomic failure — PAF, MSA, PD, DLB
DiseaseDistinguishing features
PAF (Bradbury-Eggleston)Profound OH + urinary/ED for years, no parkinsonism or cognitive change; ~30% phenoconvert. Reduced MIBG (postganglionic).
MSA-A (Shy-Drager)Early OH + urogenital + parkinsonism (poor levodopa response) and/or cerebellar ataxia within years. Stridor is a key clue. NORMAL MIBG (preganglionic). Median survival 6–9 y.
PDOH usually later; supine HTN common; constipation often the earliest autonomic symptom. Reduced MIBG.

MIBG cardiac scintigraphy is the most discriminating test between MSA (normal — preganglionic) and PD/PAF/DLB (reduced — postganglionic).
9. AAG, diabetic, amyloid & other autonomic neuropathies
  • Acute pandysautonomia / AAN: subacute severe OH + parasympathetic failure (dry eyes/mouth, dilated unreactive pupils, fixed HR, urinary retention) + GI dysmotility, often post-viral; monophasic.
  • AAG: anti-α3-gAChR antibodies (~50%); chronic/subacute; IVIG, PLEX, steroids, rituximab.
  • Diabetic autonomic neuropathy: cardiovascular (resting tachy, OH, silent MI), GI (gastroparesis, diabetic diarrhoea, constipation), urogenital (ED often earliest), sudomotor (gustatory sweating), pupillary, hypoglycaemia unawareness. Tight glycaemic control + symptom-directed care.
  • Amyloid (AL or hereditary ATTR): family history, carpal tunnel, cardiomyopathy, autonomic + small-fibre neuropathy. hATTR therapy: patisiran, inotersen, vutrisiran (RNA-based silencers), tafamidis (stabiliser); AL → chemotherapy.
  • Other: Sjögren (sicca + SF/autonomic neuropathy), paraneoplastic (anti-Hu, CRMP5; SCLC), HIV, HSAN III (Riley-Day; Ashkenazi; IKBKAP/ELP1).
10. PSH — paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity after brain injury

"Sympathetic storms" in 5–30% of severe TBI; also after anoxia, stroke, encephalitis. Episodic simultaneous ↑ HR, ↑ BP, ↑ RR, ↑ temp, sweating + dystonic posturing. Misdiagnosed as sepsis, seizures, PE, withdrawal.

Baguley criteria (2014): Clinical Feature Scale (severity) + Diagnosis Likelihood Tool (features + antecedent brain injury). Combined ≥ 27 = probable.

  1. Reduce noxious stimuli; treat triggers (full bladder, constipation, infection, pain).
  2. Abortive: morphine 1–2 mg IV (gold standard), benzodiazepine, propofol, dexmedetomidine.
  3. Prevention: scheduled propranolol (BBB-penetrating non-selective β), gabapentin, clonidine, baclofen (with posturing), bromocriptine; combinations often.
  4. Avoid haloperidol (worsens dystonic features).
  5. Improves over weeks–months as brain recovers.
11. Acute autonomic emergencies in other settings
  • GBS dysautonomia (Module 9): > 50% have lability of BP, HR, arrhythmias, ileus, urinary retention — major mortality driver. Telemetry monitoring; avoid pushed cardioactive drug boluses.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia (SCI above T6, Module 10): surge of HTN + headache + bradycardia + sweating above lesion, triggered by noxious stimulus below (distended bladder, faecal impaction, pressure ulcer). Sit up, loosen clothing, identify and remove trigger; nifedipine IR or topical/sublingual nitrate if BP persists.
  • Tetanus: autonomic instability is the major modern-tetanus killer — Mg infusion + benzodiazepines + α-blockade.
  • NMS / serotonin syndrome / MH / sympathomimetic toxidrome: Module 13.
  • Pheochromocytoma: paroxysmal HTN + headache + sweating + palpitations. α-blockade BEFORE β-blockade (avoid unopposed α-vasoconstriction). Surgical removal definitive.
12. When to refer
  • Same day: high-risk syncope (CSRS ≥ 4, abnormal ECG, exertional, structural heart disease, family history SCD); PSH; autonomic dysreflexia; suspected pheochromocytoma crisis; severe GBS dysautonomia.
  • Within 1–2 weeks: recurrent unexplained syncope after low-risk workup; suspected autonomic neuropathy; suspected MSA / PAF / DLB; new POTS; severe diabetic autonomic neuropathy.
  • Routine: chronic OH stable on therapy; established PAF / PD with autonomic features.
  • Always involve: cardiology for cardiac causes of syncope; movement disorders for MSA/PD; autonomic clinic for tilt-table, MIBG, complex polypharmacy.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 22-year-old medical student feels faint while watching a procedure; she becomes pale, nauseated, sweaty, then collapses briefly. Recovers within 30 seconds, alert and oriented

No injury. No palpitation. No exertion. ECG normal. No structural heart disease. No family history of sudden cardiac death. Brief myoclonic jerk witnessed during the event.

Diagnosis: Vasovagal (reflex) syncope. The brief myoclonic activity is "convulsive syncope" — common and not a seizure.

Plan:

  1. Reassurance and patient education — vasovagal syncope is common and benign in young healthy patients with a classic prodrome and no red flags.
  2. Counsel about triggers (prolonged standing, hot environments, blood, pain) and early-warning physical counter-pressure manoeuvres (leg crossing with tension, hand grip, squat).
  3. Adequate hydration; salt liberalisation if recurrent.
  4. Discharge without further imaging or telemetry — pan-investigation is inappropriate.
  5. If recurrent and disabling: consider tilt-table, then midodrine, fludrocortisone, or — in older patients with cardioinhibitory pattern — pacemaker (selected cases; HRS/ESC guidance).
Case 2 — A 68-year-old man collapses while playing tennis. No prodrome. ECG shows long QT with QTc 510 ms. Family history of his brother dying suddenly at age 38

Recovery rapid after a few seconds of unconsciousness. Echo: normal LV function. Holter: occasional polymorphic ventricular ectopy. Canadian Syncope Risk Score: troponin elevated +2, QTc > 480 ms +2, heart disease history may apply +1, BP normal, age > 65; estimated CSRS ~5.

Diagnosis: High-risk cardiac syncope likely due to long QT syndrome with risk of torsades de pointes.

Plan:

  1. Admit for telemetry and cardiology consultation.
  2. Review and stop QT-prolonging medications.
  3. Electrolytes — correct K and Mg.
  4. Genetic testing (KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A — LQT1, LQT2, LQT3).
  5. β-blocker therapy (typically nadolol for LQT1, propranolol for LQT2). Avoid LQT-aggravating drugs.
  6. ICD consideration — syncope on β-blocker, prior cardiac arrest, family history of sudden cardiac death < 40 y in this patient justify discussion.
  7. Family cascade screening.
  8. Activity restrictions per LQT subtype (swimming for LQT1, auditory triggers for LQT2).
Exertional or supine syncope, syncope without warning, palpitation prodrome, abnormal ECG, family history SCD < 40 — these features are independently high-risk for arrhythmic cause and mandate cardiology pathway.
Case 3 — A 75-year-old with 4-year history of postural lightheadedness, constipation, REM sleep behaviour, and mild parkinsonism poorly responsive to levodopa. Now has urinary retention and inspiratory stridor at night

Supine BP 160/90; standing (3 min) 95/55, HR rises only by 8 bpm. MIBG cardiac scintigraphy: NORMAL uptake. MRI: pontine "hot cross bun" sign and middle cerebellar peduncle hyperintensity.

Diagnosis: Multiple system atrophy, autonomic-predominant (MSA-A; formerly Shy-Drager).

Plan:

  1. Confirm with movement-disorders neurology referral; cross-ref Module 6.
  2. Stridor — sleep medicine + ENT urgently; CPAP often helps; tracheostomy in severe vocal-cord abductor paralysis.
  3. Orthostatic hypotension: head of bed up 10–20°; fluid + salt; abdominal binder; midodrine 2.5–10 mg TID or droxidopa titrated; pyridostigmine adjunct.
  4. Supine hypertension: avoid lying flat; carb snack at bedtime; short-acting evening antihypertensive only if needed.
  5. Urinary retention: urodynamics, intermittent self-catheterisation, α-blocker.
  6. Constipation: PEG-based laxatives, prokinetics; treat aggressively.
  7. Palliative-care input early; multidisciplinary autonomic clinic.
Case 4 — A 28-year-old woman has 9 months of palpitations on standing, lightheadedness, fatigue, and cognitive fog after a viral illness. No syncope. No structural heart disease

Standing HR rises from 78 to 130 within 5 minutes, no significant BP drop. Symptoms relieved by sitting. Mild hypermobility on Beighton score. ECG normal.

Diagnosis: Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), likely post-viral; possible Ehlers-Danlos hypermobility overlap.

Plan:

  1. Confirm with active stand or tilt-table; rule out anaemia, hyperthyroidism, dehydration, pheochromocytoma, mast cell disease.
  2. Non-pharmacological cornerstone: salt 6–10 g/day + fluid 2–3 L/day; abdominal binder + waist-high compression stockings; structured graded exercise programme starting recumbent (rowing, recumbent bike, swimming) and progressing to upright over months — most effective long-term intervention.
  3. Avoid prolonged standing in heat; cool clothing; elevate legs when seated; small frequent meals.
  4. If pharmacotherapy needed: low-dose propranolol 10–20 mg TID/QID or ivabradine 2.5–7.5 mg BID; fludrocortisone; midodrine; consider methyldopa / clonidine for hyperadrenergic POTS.
  5. Screen for and treat comorbid MCAS (H1 + H2), anxiety/depression, GI dysmotility.
  6. Hypermobility — physiotherapy, joint protection education.
  7. Patient education and reassurance — chronic but improves with consistent self-management.
Case 5 — A 25-year-old man with severe TBI 3 weeks ago has episodic tachycardia (HR 140), hypertension (BP 200/110), hyperthermia (39.5 °C), tachypnea, profuse sweating, and dystonic posturing

Episodes triggered by suctioning and turning, lasting 20–30 minutes. No infection on workup. EEG: no seizures. Sepsis ruled out.

Diagnosis: Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (PSH) after severe TBI. Baguley combined score likely ≥ 27 (probable).

Plan:

  1. Identify and remove triggers: catheterise full bladder, treat constipation, control pain, minimise environmental stimuli, schedule cares.
  2. Abortive: morphine 1–2 mg IV (gold standard for breakthrough); benzodiazepine if agitation; propofol or dexmedetomidine if ICU sedation needed.
  3. Scheduled prevention: propranolol (BBB-penetrating non-selective β-blocker), gabapentin, clonidine, baclofen (especially with dystonic posturing), bromocriptine; combinations often required.
  4. Avoid haloperidol (can worsen dystonic features).
  5. Adequate hydration and nutrition; monitor for rhabdomyolysis with severe posturing.
  6. Educate family — PSH usually improves over weeks–months as brain recovery progresses.
Case 6 — A 60-year-old man with T2DM (HbA1c 9.5%) has postural lightheadedness, early satiety with vomiting, erectile dysfunction, and bladder hesitancy. Hypoglycaemia awareness is impaired

Supine BP 145/85; standing (3 min) 105/60 with HR rise only 5 bpm. Resting HR 100. Examination: reduced ankle reflexes, distal pinprick reduction, normal motor.

Diagnosis: Diabetic autonomic neuropathy (cardiovascular + gastroparesis + urogenital + hypoglycaemia unawareness).

Plan:

  1. Tighten glycaemic control while avoiding hypoglycaemia (CGM particularly valuable given unawareness).
  2. Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy: midodrine for OH (last dose by 6 pm); compression; address supine HTN; cardiology screening (silent MI risk).
  3. Gastroparesis: small frequent meals, low fat/fibre; metoclopramide short courses only (extrapyramidal risk — cross-ref Module 6); erythromycin; prucalopride; gastric electrical stimulation in refractory cases.
  4. ED: PDE5 inhibitors first-line; investigate vascular and endocrine contributions.
  5. Bladder: post-void residual measurement; intermittent self-catheterisation if elevated; α-blocker (tamsulosin) for incomplete emptying.
  6. Hypoglycaemia unawareness: CGM, structured education, raise glucose targets temporarily to restore awareness.
  7. Sudomotor symptoms (gustatory sweating): glycopyrrolate topically or systemically; oxybutynin; address triggers.
  8. Refer to autonomic clinic and diabetes neuropathy MDT.
