1 · Big picture: a family of inflammatory demyelinating disorders

For decades clinicians lumped almost all CNS inflammatory demyelination as "MS or a variant." The discovery of the AQP4 (aquaporin-4) IgG antibody (defining NMOSD) and the MOG IgG antibody (defining MOGAD) has split the field into three distinct diseases plus several related entities. The treatments diverge sharply, so getting the diagnosis right is critical.

The single most important triage habit: in any patient presenting with a first inflammatory demyelinating event — optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, brainstem syndrome — send AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG (serum, cell-based assay) before assuming MS. The treatment differs dramatically.

2 · Multiple sclerosis — epidemiology & course

Epidemiology

  • Leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in young adults.
  • US prevalence ~309 / 100,000. Latitude gradient: higher prevalence further from the equator (vitamin D and other factors).
  • Median age of onset 23–24 y; rare before 10 or after 60.
  • Female:male ratio ~2:1 (equal in primary progressive MS).
  • Strongest genetic risk: HLA-DRB1*1501; sibling concordance ~3%; monozygotic twin concordance ~30%.
  • Strongest environmental risk: Epstein-Barr virus infection (essentially required — 99% of MS patients seropositive). Other contributors: low vitamin D, smoking, childhood obesity.

Disease course phenotypes (Lublin 2013)

PhenotypeDescription% at diagnosis
Relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS)Discrete relapses with full or partial recovery; stable between attacks85–90%
Secondary progressive MS (SPMS)RRMS that has transitioned to gradual neurological worsening between (or without) relapses(Many RRMS evolve to SPMS within 10–25 years if untreated)
Primary progressive MS (PPMS)Gradual neurological worsening from onset, without distinct relapses10–15%
Clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)First demyelinating event, before McDonald criteria met—
Radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS)MRI lesions typical for MS, no clinical event—

Each phenotype is further modified by "active" (new clinical relapses or MRI lesions) and "progressing" (gradual accumulation of disability independent of relapses) descriptors.

3 · Common clinical presentations of MS

SyndromeFeatures
Optic neuritisUnilateral painful vision loss over hours-days; pain worse with eye movement; central scotoma; RAPD; reduced colour vision (especially red); usually peaks at 1–2 weeks and recovers over 4–6 weeks. Recovery is usually good in MS (vs. severe in NMOSD).
Transverse myelitis (TM)Sub-acute weakness, sensory level, bowel/bladder dysfunction. In MS, lesions typically < 2 vertebral segments and partial cord involvement. Longitudinally extensive TM (LETM, ≥ 3 segments) suggests NMOSD or MOGAD.
Brainstem / cerebellar syndromesInternuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO — failure of adduction with abducting eye nystagmus; bilateral INO in a young person is MS until proven otherwise), 6th nerve palsy, facial sensory loss, vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria.
Sensory syndromesNumbness, tingling, dysesthesia; often multi-territorial; useless-hand of Oppenheim (proprioceptive loss).
L'hermitte signElectric shock-like sensation down spine on neck flexion — cervical cord demyelination.
Uhthoff phenomenonTransient worsening of existing neurological symptoms with heat (exercise, hot bath, fever) — not a true relapse.
Paroxysmal symptomsTrigeminal neuralgia, tonic spasms, brief sensory phenomena.
FatigueMost common symptom; often disabling.
Cognitive impairmentSlow processing speed, attention, memory; subcortical pattern; present early.
Mood disordersDepression in ~50% lifetime; pseudobulbar affect.
Bladder / bowel / sexualUrgency, retention, incontinence; constipation; sexual dysfunction.
Red flags for the diagnosis "MS": bilateral severe optic neuritis with poor recovery (think NMOSD/MOGAD) · LETM ≥ 3 vertebral segments (NMOSD/MOGAD) · intractable nausea/vomiting/hiccup (NMOSD area postrema) · encephalopathy (think ADEM, MOGAD cerebral cortical encephalitis, autoimmune encephalitis) · constitutional features or systemic disease (sarcoid, lupus, Behçet, Sjögren) · age < 10 or > 50 at first attack · normal MRI brain & cord.

4 · Diagnosis: 2017 McDonald criteria

The 2017 McDonald criteria allow MS to be diagnosed at the first clinical attack in many patients, by demonstrating dissemination in space (DIS) and dissemination in time (DIT). The criteria apply only to patients with a typical demyelinating presentation and after exclusion of alternative diagnoses.

2017 McDonald criteria simplified

Clinical presentationAdditional data required for MS diagnosis
≥ 2 attacks, objective evidence of ≥ 2 lesionsNone
≥ 2 attacks, objective evidence of 1 lesion (plus history of prior attack at another site)None
≥ 2 attacks, objective evidence of 1 lesionDIS by MRI or a second clinical attack at a different site
1 attack, objective evidence of ≥ 2 lesionsDIT by MRI, a second clinical attack, or CSF-specific oligoclonal bands
1 attack, objective evidence of 1 lesion (CIS)DIS by MRI or a second clinical attack at a different site plus DIT by MRI, second attack, or CSF-specific OCBs
Insidious progressive (PPMS suspected)1 year of clinical progression + 2 of: (a) ≥ 1 T2 brain lesion in MS-typical region; (b) ≥ 2 T2 cord lesions; (c) CSF-specific OCBs

MRI DIS criteria (≥ 1 T2 lesion in ≥ 2 of):

  • Periventricular
  • Juxtacortical / cortical
  • Infratentorial (brainstem, cerebellum)
  • Spinal cord

MRI DIT criteria (≥ 1 of):

  • Simultaneous gadolinium-enhancing AND non-enhancing lesions on a single scan
  • New T2 or gadolinium-enhancing lesion on follow-up MRI
Key change in 2017: CSF-specific oligoclonal bands (present in ~95% of MS) can substitute for DIT in patients with CIS. This allows earlier diagnosis (and earlier treatment) at the first clinical attack.

Differential diagnosis — "red flag" mimics

  • NMOSD & MOGAD (see below)
  • Neurosarcoidosis — meningeal enhancement, hilar adenopathy, ACE/calcium
  • SLE, Sjögren, Behçet, antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis
  • CNS lymphoma
  • Susac syndrome (encephalopathy + retinal artery occlusions + sensorineural hearing loss; corpus callosum "snowballs")
  • CADASIL (anterior temporal pole white matter)
  • Vitamin B12 / copper deficiency myelopathy
  • HTLV-1 myelopathy (tropical spastic paraparesis)
  • Neuro-Lyme, neurosyphilis, HIV
  • PML
  • Vascular: cerebral small vessel disease, migraine WMHs (much more common than MS)
  • Leukodystrophies (adrenoleukodystrophy in adults — men with spastic paraparesis and adrenal insufficiency)

5 · Imaging & CSF in MS

MRI — the workhorse

  • Brain MRI with and without gadolinium: typical lesions are ovoid, periventricular (perpendicular to ventricles = "Dawson fingers"), juxtacortical, infratentorial, callosal.
  • Gadolinium-enhancing lesions reflect active inflammation (enhancement typically lasts 2–8 weeks).
  • "Black holes" (T1 hypointense lesions) indicate axonal loss.
  • Spinal cord MRI: lesions usually short (< 2 vertebral segments), peripheral, often cervical; centrally located cord lesions ≥ 3 segments suggest NMOSD or other.
  • 3T preferred; standardised MS-MRI protocol per CMSC/MAGNIMS guidelines.

CSF in MS

  • Oligoclonal bands (OCBs) present in CSF but not serum: ~95% of MS patients. Now formally part of McDonald 2017 criteria.
  • Mildly elevated IgG index.
  • Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (< 50 cells/µL); higher counts suggest NMOSD, MOGAD, or infection.
  • Normal protein and glucose.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Quantifies retinal nerve fibre layer thickness; useful for monitoring optic nerve damage and differentiating subclinical disease.

Evoked potentials

Visual, somatosensory, and brainstem evoked potentials can demonstrate clinically silent demyelination; less commonly used now with MRI dominance.

6 · Relapse management

A relapse is new or worsening neurological symptoms lasting ≥ 24 hours, in the absence of fever or infection, separated from a previous attack by at least 30 days. Heat-related (Uhthoff) and infection-related transient worsening (pseudo-relapse) are NOT relapses.

Acute treatment

TherapyDetail
High-dose corticosteroidsIV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3–5 days (or oral methylprednisolone equivalent dose, equally effective per OCTOPUS, COPOUSEP). Hastens recovery but doesn't change long-term disability. PPI cover; monitor BP, glucose, mood.
Plasma exchange (PLEX)For severe attacks (especially involving optic nerve, spinal cord, or brainstem) refractory to steroids. 5–7 exchanges over 10–14 days. Especially useful in NMOSD and steroid-refractory severe MS attacks.
IVIGLess evidence in MS; an alternative for steroid-intolerant patients or in pregnancy.
Always rule out pseudo-relapse first: check temperature, urine, full septic screen if febrile. Treating an "MS attack" that is actually a UTI with steroids is harmful.

7 · Disease-modifying therapy (DMT) — the ladder

The DMT landscape has expanded enormously. Modern MS care often uses an early high-efficacy treatment approach in young patients with active disease, rather than the older "escalation" model that started with platform agents. Two-thirds of patients with severely active disease on platform therapy will switch within 2 years; outcomes are best with early effective immunotherapy.

Choosing a DMT — modern paradigm

  • Highly active disease (multiple gadolinium-enhancing lesions, frequent relapses, severe attacks, rapid disability accumulation): start high-efficacy from the outset — ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, natalizumab (if JCV negative), cladribine, or alemtuzumab.
  • Less active early RRMS: many neurologists now still favour high-efficacy first; alternatives include S1P modulators or fumarates.
  • Pregnancy considerations: glatiramer acetate has the best safety data; interferons acceptable. Anti-CD20 (especially ocrelizumab) increasingly used — B-cell-depleting effect persists, and re-dosing typically deferred until after breastfeeding. Avoid teriflunomide, fingolimod, cladribine, alemtuzumab in pregnancy.
  • Adherence-limited: oral or infusion preferred over injectables.
  • JCV-positive with high titre: avoid natalizumab if alternatives exist.
  • Treatment goal: NEDA-3 ("no evidence of disease activity") — no relapses, no new MRI lesions, no disability progression. NEDA-4 adds brain atrophy. Reassess MRI at 6–12 months on DMT; if breakthrough disease, escalate.
Critical safety notes:
  • Natalizumab → PML: stratify with JCV antibody index. JCV-negative: low risk. JCV-positive: increasing risk with treatment duration (especially > 2 years) and prior immunosuppressant exposure.
  • Anti-CD20 + hypogammaglobulinaemia: check IgG levels; risk increases with cumulative exposure. Consider IVIG if recurrent infections.
  • Fingolimod stopping → rebound: severe MS reactivation can occur within weeks of discontinuation; transition carefully.
  • Pre-DMT screen: HBV, HCV, HIV, TB (QuantiFERON), VZV immunity, JCV (natalizumab), MRI baseline, malignancy screening, pregnancy counselling.

8 · Progressive MS

FormApproved DMT
Primary progressive MS (PPMS)Ocrelizumab (the only DMT with PPMS approval; modest slowing of progression; greatest effect in younger patients with active MRI disease)
Secondary progressive MS (active form)Siponimod (EXPAND); also anti-CD20 agents and cladribine often used
SPMS without active inflammationLimited DMT benefit; focus on rehabilitation and symptom management

Progressive MS reflects ongoing neurodegeneration that DMTs only modestly affect. Rehabilitation, exercise, vascular risk-factor management, treatment of comorbidities, and symptom-targeted therapy drive quality of life. Early aggressive RRMS treatment is the best strategy to prevent transition to SPMS.

10 · Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD)

An astrocytopathy driven by AQP4-IgG antibodies attacking aquaporin-4 water channels on astrocyte foot processes, causing complement-mediated injury. The 2015 IPND criteria collapsed Devic disease and related entities into NMOSD.

Six core clinical syndromes (any one + AQP4-IgG = NMOSD)

  1. Optic neuritis — often severe, frequently bilateral or sequential, poor recovery
  2. Acute myelitis — usually longitudinally extensive (LETM, ≥ 3 vertebral segments)
  3. Area postrema syndrome — intractable nausea, vomiting, or hiccups for > 48 hours
  4. Acute brainstem syndrome
  5. Symptomatic narcolepsy / acute diencephalic syndrome with NMOSD-typical MRI
  6. Symptomatic cerebral syndrome with NMOSD-typical brain lesions

AQP4-IgG-negative NMOSD

~25% of patients with classic NMOSD phenotype are AQP4-negative; criteria require 2 core syndromes (one of which is optic neuritis, acute myelitis, or area postrema syndrome) and additional MRI features.

Why NMOSD vs MS matters — treatment diverges

  • MS DMTs are ineffective or harmful in NMOSD: interferon-β, natalizumab, and fingolimod can worsen NMOSD.
  • NMOSD-specific monoclonal antibodies (2019–2020 FDA approvals):
DrugMechanismNotes
Eculizumab / RavulizumabComplement C5 inhibitorHighly effective in AQP4+. Meningococcal vaccination required.
InebilizumabAnti-CD19 (broader B-cell depletion than anti-CD20)Highly effective in AQP4+.
SatralizumabAnti-IL-6 receptorSC; effective in AQP4+. Both as monotherapy and add-on.
Rituximab (off-label)Anti-CD20Widely used worldwide; effective in AQP4+.
Azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetilGeneral immunosuppressantsOlder approach; still used internationally.

Acute NMOSD relapses

  • High-dose IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 5 days.
  • Early plasma exchange (PLEX): especially for severe attacks — better recovery if started early (within days). Threshold lower than for MS.
NMOSD attacks are stepwise and disabling — unlike MS where disability accumulates partly via gradual progression, NMOSD damage is almost entirely from individual relapses. Aggressive relapse prevention with monoclonal antibodies is essential.

11 · MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD)

An increasingly recognised demyelinating disorder driven by IgG against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein. Distinct from MS and NMOSD; criteria published in 2023 (Banwell et al., Lancet Neurology).

Clinical phenotypes

  • Optic neuritis (most common) — often bilateral, more recovery-prone than NMOSD; optic disc swelling common.
  • Transverse myelitis — conus involvement common; LETM possible.
  • ADEM-like presentation, especially in children.
  • Cerebral cortical encephalitis with seizures (FLAMES — FLAIR-hyperintense lesions in anti-MOG-associated encephalitis with seizures).
  • Brainstem syndromes.

12 · ADEM, transverse myelitis & other related entities

Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)

  • Monophasic post-infectious / post-vaccination demyelination; predominantly children but occurs in adults.
  • Encephalopathy is required for diagnosis (distinguishes from MS).
  • MRI: multiple T2 hyperintensities, often subcortical, basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem; often confluent.
  • MOG-IgG positive in ~50% of paediatric cases.
  • Treatment: high-dose IV methylprednisolone followed by oral taper; PLEX or IVIG if steroid-refractory.
  • Most adults recover well; relapsing ADEM should prompt re-evaluation (MOGAD, multiphasic ADEM, MS).

Transverse myelitis (TM)

  • Generic syndrome of cord inflammation; differential is broad.
  • Causes: MS (short, partial), NMOSD (LETM), MOGAD, post-infectious, idiopathic, autoimmune (lupus, Sjögren, sarcoid), paraneoplastic, viral, neurosyphilis.
  • Always image the whole cord; serum AQP4 and MOG; CSF; consider PLEX early in severe cases.

Other related entities

  • Marburg variant — fulminant, monophasic, often fatal MS variant; tumefactive lesions with marked oedema.
  • Tumefactive MS — single or few large lesions (> 2 cm) with mass effect, sometimes confused with tumour; ring enhancement (often "open ring" facing white matter), perilesional oedema; biopsy occasionally needed.
  • Balo concentric sclerosis — concentric demyelinated and preserved bands; rare.
  • CIS / RIS — see earlier.

13 · When to refer

Refer urgently:
  • First clinical demyelinating event — for full workup, diagnosis, and DMT initiation.
  • Severe acute attack (especially LETM, severe optic neuritis, brainstem with deficit) — for consideration of PLEX, NMOSD/MOGAD evaluation.
  • Suspected NMOSD / MOGAD — specialist diagnosis and antibody-targeted therapy.
  • Suspected ADEM with encephalopathy — for IV steroids and ICU as needed.
  • Breakthrough disease on current DMT — for escalation.
  • Pregnancy planning in MS or NMOSD.
  • Atypical features suggesting mimic (encephalopathy, systemic disease, etc.).
Refer routinely: all confirmed MS, NMOSD, MOGAD patients should have ongoing neurology/MS clinic follow-up; primary care manages co-morbidities, vaccinations, vascular risk, symptomatic issues between visits.

Workup for first inflammatory demyelinating event

  • MRI brain & whole spine with and without gadolinium
  • Serum AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG (cell-based assays)
  • LP: cell count, protein, glucose, oligoclonal bands (paired serum), IgG index
  • Exclude mimics: vitamin B12 (and copper), TSH, ANA, ENA, anti-Ro/La, ACE, syphilis, HIV, HTLV-1 (if exposure), Lyme (endemic areas)
  • Optic neuritis: OCT, visual evoked potentials
  • Chest X-ray ± CT for sarcoid; consider PET-CT if neoplastic mimic suspected
  • Pre-DMT: HBV, HCV, HIV, TB (QuantiFERON), VZV titre, JCV antibody (if natalizumab considered), pregnancy test, baseline CBC, CMP
