1 Phenomenology — the vocabulary

▎Key points
  • Split first into hypokinetic (too little movement — essentially parkinsonism) and hyperkinetic (too much).
  • Then name the hyperkinetic movement by speed, rhythmicity, suppressibility, stereotypy and whether it flows or jerks.
  • Two or more phenomenologies coexisting in one patient is common and is itself a clue — myoclonus-dystonia, dystonic tremor, parkinsonism with chorea.
  • Suppressibility + premonitory urge is nearly specific to tics. Distractibility + entrainment points to a functional disorder.

The distinguishing question for each. Tremor: is it rhythmic? Chorea: does it flow and wander? Dystonia: is it the same pattern every time, and is there a sensory trick? Re-emergent tremor is the single most useful bedside sign for separating PD from essential tremor. In ET, the postural tremor starts the instant the arms come up. In PD, there is a latency of several seconds while the limb "resets", then the rest tremor re-emerges in the new posture. Count the seconds out loud — it forces you to wait long enough.

2 The diagnostic approach

▎Key points
  • Phenomenology → distribution → tempo → age → exposures → associated signs. In that order, every time.
  • Tempo drives urgency. Hyperacute or subacute onset means a structural, metabolic, toxic, autoimmune or infectious cause until proven otherwise; years of slow progression means degenerative or genetic.
  • Always take a drug history covering 12 months — and ask about antiemetics and "dizziness" tablets by name, because patients do not call them psychiatric drugs.
  • In anyone under 50 with an unexplained movement disorder, Wilson disease must be actively excluded, not merely considered.

The six questions that do most of the work

  1. What is the movement? Name it before you theorise. Video it — on the patient's own phone if necessary.
  2. Where is it, and is it symmetric? Marked asymmetry favours PD, structural lesions, and dystonia; symmetry favours drug-induced, metabolic and genetic causes.
  3. How fast did it come on, and is it progressing? (See the tempo table.)
  4. How old is the patient? Young onset reorders the entire differential toward Wilson, genetic dystonia, NBIA, juvenile HD and drug exposure.
  5. What have they taken? Twelve months, including over-the-counter, herbal, antiemetics, and anything prescribed by another hospital.
  6. What else is on the exam? Eye movements, pyramidal signs, cerebellar signs, cognition, autonomic function, neuropathy — these separate PD from everything that mimics it.
Examine the eyes properly. Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy (PSP), square-wave jerks (PSP, cerebellar), slow saccades (SCA2, HD), apraxia of eyelid opening (PSP), oculogyric crisis (drug-induced, postencephalitic), Kayser–Fleischer rings (Wilson — needs a slit lamp; a normal bedside look does not exclude them), opsoclonus (paraneoplastic). The eye exam is where atypical parkinsonism declares itself first.

3 Investigation strategy & urgency

▎Key points
  • In a hospital without on-site MRI, the useful question is never "what test would I like?" but "what changes management today, what changes it this week, and what can wait for a single referral visit?"
  • Tier A tests are available at the bedside or in your own lab and answer reversible questions — do them on everyone with a new movement disorder.
  • Antibody and genetic results take weeks. Send them, then treat on clinical criteria. Never let a pending antibody delay immunotherapy in a patient who already meets a clinical definition.
  • Batch tier-D tests into one referral trip. A patient who travels four hours should come back with the MRI, the slit lamp, the panel and the specialist opinion done in the same visit — not four visits.

CT is not a poor man's MRI, but it is not useless either. CT will show you an acute haemorrhage, a large infarct, hydrocephalus, a mass with oedema, and gross calcification — which means it will diagnose hemiballism from a basal ganglia stroke, and it will pick up the dense bilateral calcification of primary familial brain calcification. Make one referral trip do the work of three. When you refer, write the request so the receiving centre does the MRI, the slit lamp, the antibody panel and the specialist review in a single attendance. Send the video with the patient. Send your tier-A and tier-C results with them, so nothing is repeated. For a patient travelling from a district in Isan, a wasted trip is not an inconvenience — it is often the reason the diagnosis is never completed.

4 Tremor & its syndromes

▎Key points
  • The 2018 MDS consensus classifies tremor on two axes: clinical features (axis 1) and aetiology (axis 2). Axis 1 first.
  • Essential tremor requires isolated bilateral upper-limb action tremor for at least 3 years, with no other neurological signs.
  • Essential tremor plus = ET with additional soft signs (impaired tandem gait, questionable dystonic posturing, mild memory impairment) — a category deliberately created to stop clinicians calling those patients plain ET.
  • If there are definite dystonic, parkinsonian or cerebellar signs, it is not ET at all — it is a tremor of that disorder.

The "null point". In dystonic head tremor, there is one head position in which the tremor disappears or markedly reduces — the position toward which the dystonia is pulling. Ask the patient to slowly rotate their head through the full range while you watch. Essential head tremor has no null point. This single manoeuvre resolves a large proportion of "refractory ET" referrals.
Enhanced physiological tremor is a diagnosis you should make often, because it is reversible. Thyrotoxicosis, salbutamol and other β-agonists, theophylline, lithium, valproate, SSRIs, tricyclics, amiodarone, caffeine, alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycaemia, fever, anxiety and fatigue all produce it. In a health-worker population it is common enough to be measurable. Check a TSH and read the drug chart before you label anyone with ET.

Treating essential tremor

  1. Establish it is ET — apply the 3-year rule, exclude dystonic tremor and enhanced physiological tremor.
  2. Decide whether it needs treatment at all. Many patients want an explanation and a prognosis, not a drug.
  3. First line: propranolol (start 10–20 mg, titrate; watch asthma, bradycardia, diabetes) or primidone (start 12.5–25 mg at night — the first dose reaction is why you start low). Either is reasonable first; combining them helps some patients.
  4. Second line: topiramate, gabapentin, alprazolam (use sparingly). Botulinum toxin for head or voice tremor.
  5. Refractory and disabling: MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy, or thalamic (VIM) DBS. See §20 for exactly who the FUS trial enrolled.
Focused ultrasound thalamotomy for ET (NEJM 2016). Randomised, sham-controlled, 76 patients analysed. Hand-tremor score improved 47% at 3 months (18.1 → 9.6) versus 0.1% with sham; between-group difference 8.3 points (95% CI 5.9–10.7, P<0.001). Adverse events included gait disturbance and paraesthesia/numbness (38%), persisting at 12 months in 9% and 14% respectively. At 3 years, improvement from baseline remained significant: 38–50% hand tremor, 43–56% disability — with some mild degradation and no progressive or delayed complications.

5 Parkinson disease — diagnosis

▎Key points
  • The MDS criteria require bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity, then apply supportive criteria, absolute exclusions and red flags.
  • Bradykinesia means decrement — progressive reduction in amplitude or speed on repetition — not merely slowness. If there is no decrement, question the diagnosis.
  • Clinically established PD needs ≥2 supportive criteria, no absolute exclusions, and no red flags. Probable PD tolerates red flags only if balanced by supportive criteria.
  • Prodromal features — RBD, hyposmia, constipation, depression — often precede motor onset by a decade and are worth asking about.

Examining for bradykinesia properly

Test finger tapping, hand opening/closing, pronation–supination, toe tapping and heel tapping. Ask for big and fast movements and watch for at least 10–15 repetitions. You are looking for three things: reduced amplitude, reduced speed, and — the specific finding — decrement or hesitation as the sequence continues. Fatigue in a fibromyalgic or depressed patient produces slowness without decrement. Spasticity and cerebellar dysmetria also slow movement, but again without decrement.

"No response to levodopa" is only an exclusion if the trial was adequate. That means titrating to at least 1000 mg/day of levodopa (or the maximum tolerated) for several weeks, with an objective before-and-after assessment, in a patient with at least moderate disease. A 300 mg/day trial for two weeks that "did nothing" excludes nothing.
Ask about REM sleep behaviour disorder in every parkinsonian patient — and ask the bed partner, not the patient. "Does he act out his dreams? Shout, punch, kick, fall out of bed?" Polysomnography-confirmed RBD is one of the strongest prodromal markers of an α-synucleinopathy, and it also shifts you toward DLB and MSA rather than PSP or CBS.

Genetics worth knowing

Monogenic PD accounts for a minority of cases but reshapes counselling. LRRK2 (autosomal dominant, variable penetrance, notably frequent in some populations, often a benign PD-like course) and GBA (a glucocerebrosidase variant — the commonest genetic risk factor, associated with earlier onset and more cognitive decline) are the two you will hear about most. PRKN, PINK1 and DJ-1 cause young-onset recessive disease with excellent and sustained levodopa response but early dyskinesia. Global genetic studies keep showing that the variant spectrum differs substantially between ancestries, so risk estimates derived from European cohorts transfer imperfectly.

6 Parkinson disease — therapy

▎Key points
  • Levodopa is the most effective drug and delaying it does not protect anyone. LEAP showed no difference in progression or motor complications at 5 years between starting 40 weeks earlier or later.
  • Motor complications track disease duration and severity, not cumulative levodopa exposure. Withholding levodopa from a disabled patient buys nothing and costs years of function.
  • Dopamine agonists carry impulse-control disorders — and you must ask directly, repeatedly, and preferably with a relative present.
  • Rasagiline's delayed-start design (ADAGIO) was positive at 1 mg and negative at 2 mg — a dissociation that means neuroprotection is not established.

LEAP — early versus delayed levodopa (long-term follow-up, Mov Disord 2024). Patients with early PD randomised to levodopa 100/25 mg three times daily immediately or after a 40-week delay. At 5 years (321 completers), the adjusted total UPDRS did not differ between groups (estimated difference 0.24, SE 0.13, P = 0.07; at 3 years 0.17, SE 0.13, P = 0.18). Prevalence of wearing-off and levodopa equivalent daily dose were also not significantly different. Conclusion: starting levodopa 40 weeks earlier neither slowed nor accelerated progression, and did not change motor complications.
ADAGIO — delayed-start rasagiline (NEJM 2009). Early-start rasagiline 1 mg/day met all three hierarchical endpoints: slower worsening between weeks 12–36 (0.09 vs 0.14 UPDRS points/week, P = 0.01) and less worsening at 72 weeks (2.82 vs 4.52 points). Early-start 2 mg/day did not. A dose-response dissociation of that kind is not what a true disease-modifying effect looks like, so rasagiline is not established as neuroprotective — a point examiners like.
Impulse-control disorders are missed because nobody asks. Pathological gambling, compulsive shopping, hypersexuality, binge eating and punding occur in a substantial minority on dopamine agonists — risk rises with agonist dose, younger age, male sex and a personal or family history of addictive behaviour. Ask at every visit, ask the spouse, and ask about money specifically. Management: reduce or withdraw the agonist (watch for dopamine agonist withdrawal syndrome — anxiety, panic, diaphoresis, pain, drug craving, which can be severe), substitute levodopa, and consider CBT.

Device-aided therapy

When fluctuations cannot be controlled orally, the options are deep brain stimulation (STN or GPi), continuous intrajejunal levodopa gel, and continuous subcutaneous apomorphine. The ideal DBS candidate mirrors the EARLYSTIM population: levodopa-responsive, without dementia or uncontrolled psychiatric illness, with fluctuations that trouble them. Levodopa responsiveness predicts DBS responsiveness — with the single exception of tremor, which can respond to stimulation even when it did not respond to levodopa.

EARLYSTIM (NEJM 2013). STN stimulation plus medical therapy versus medical therapy alone in patients aged 18–60 with disease duration ≥4 years and motor complications present for ≤3 years. Primary outcome PDQ-39 quality of life at 2 years: stimulation group improved 7.8 points, medical therapy worsened 0.2 — between-group difference 8.0 points, P = 0.002. Neurostimulation was also superior for motor disability, activities of daily living and levodopa-induced complications. The message: DBS is not only a last-resort therapy for very advanced disease.
MRI-guided focused ultrasound in tremor-dominant PD. In the sham-controlled trial, on-medication median tremor scores improved 62% after FUS thalamotomy versus 22% after sham. It is unilateral and lesional — attractive where DBS programming and follow-up are hard to sustain, but it does not treat bradykinesia, rigidity or fluctuations, and it is not reversible.

7 Atypical parkinsonism

▎Key points
  • The alarm bells: early falls, early autonomic failure, early cognitive or bulbar problems, symmetry, poor levodopa response, and pyramidal or cerebellar signs.
  • PSP — falls backward in year one, vertical supranuclear gaze palsy, axial rigidity, applause sign, surprised facies.
  • MSA — autonomic failure with parkinsonism (MSA-P) or cerebellar ataxia (MSA-C); the 2022 MDS criteria added a clinically established tier and an emphasis on MRI markers.
  • CBS — markedly asymmetric akinetic-rigid syndrome with apraxia, cortical sensory loss, myoclonus, dystonia and alien limb.

Ask the family about noisy breathing at night, involve ENT, and consider CPAP — and remember that cognitive and behavioural disturbance, once considered a red flag against MSA, is now recognised to occur in both MSA and PSP and does not by itself exclude the diagnosis.
PSP is more than Richardson syndrome. Recognised variants include PSP-parkinsonism (asymmetric, tremulous, levodopa-responsive early — often diagnosed as PD for years), PSP with progressive gait freezing, PSP-CBS, PSP with speech/language disorder and PSP-frontal. If a "PD" patient develops falls, gaze problems or axial rigidity later, revisit the diagnosis rather than escalating levodopa.

Treatment. There is no disease-modifying therapy for any of these. Give an adequate levodopa trial (up to 1000 mg/day) to everyone — some MSA-P and PSP-P patients get real benefit. Beyond that the work is symptomatic and multidisciplinary: physiotherapy and falls prevention, speech and swallow assessment, orthostatic hypotension management, botulinum toxin for focal dystonia and sialorrhoea, and honest, early conversations about prognosis, feeding and advance care planning. DBS is not indicated in atypical parkinsonism — systematic review evidence shows poor and unsustained outcomes, and it is included here precisely so you do not refer these patients. For CBS, pharmacological options are limited; treat the myoclonus (levetiracetam or clonazepam) and the dystonia (botulinum toxin) symptomatically.

8 Secondary & drug-induced parkinsonism

▎Key points
  • Drug-induced parkinsonism is the commonest reversible parkinsonism and the commonest misdiagnosis of PD. It is usually symmetric and can take months to resolve after the drug stops.
  • The offenders are not only antipsychotics: metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, flunarizine, cinnarizine, valproate, lithium, amiodarone and tetrabenazine all do it.

  • Normal-pressure hydrocephalus, repeated head trauma, manganese and carbon-monoxide exposure, and post-encephalitic states all belong in this differential.

9 Dystonia

▎Key points
  • Classify along two axes: clinical (age at onset, body distribution, temporal pattern, associated features) and aetiological (nervous system pathology, inherited/acquired/idiopathic).
  • The clinical signatures: patterned (same muscles every time), directional twisting, sensory trick (geste antagoniste), overflow, mirror dystonia, and worsening with action.
  • Every young-onset dystonia deserves a levodopa trial — dopa-responsive dystonia is transformative and easily missed.
  • Botulinum toxin is first-line for focal and segmental dystonia; it is the most effective treatment in the whole field.

Acquired and secondary dystonia

Ask about perinatal injury, encephalitis, head injury, stroke, and drug exposure. Pharmacovigilance data confirm that a broad range of drugs — antipsychotics above all, but also antiemetics, some antidepressants and antiepileptics — can precipitate dystonia. Hemidystonia after a contralateral basal ganglia lesion is a classic acquired pattern and always warrants imaging. Rapid-onset dystonia-parkinsonism (ATP1A3) presents over hours to days with a rostrocaudal gradient, often after a physiological stressor.

10 Chorea & Huntington disease

▎Key points
  • Asymmetric or hemichorea means look for a structural or metabolic cause — stroke, hyperglycaemia, a mass.
  • Non-ketotic hyperglycaemia is a leading cause of acute hemichorea, especially in older patients with poorly controlled diabetes, and the chorea usually resolves when glucose is corrected.
  • In HD, treat chorea only when it is disabling — many patients are less troubled by it than their family is.
  • Check a QTc and screen for depression before any VMAT2 inhibitor. Tetrabenazine carries a depression and suicidality warning.

Huntington disease

Autosomal dominant CAG repeat expansion in HTT. ≥40 repeats is fully penetrant; 36–39 shows reduced penetrance; 27–35 is an intermediate allele that does not cause disease but can expand in transmission — particularly through the paternal line, which is the basis of anticipation. The triad is motor (chorea early, then dystonia, parkinsonism, and the functionally more important motor impersistence and bradykinesia), cognitive (executive dysfunction progressing to subcortical dementia), and psychiatric (depression, irritability, apathy, obsessionality, and a markedly raised suicide risk).

Predictive genetic testing in HD must follow a protocol. Pre-test genetic counselling, a defined interval for reflection, psychological assessment, and support at result disclosure are standard — the suicide risk around testing and diagnosis is real. Never send an HTT test as a routine add-on to a blood panel, and never test an asymptomatic minor.

▎Key points

Negative myoclonus — asterixis

A brief lapse of tonic muscle contraction, elicited by asking the patient to hold the arms outstretched with wrists dorsiflexed — the "flapping tremor", which is not a tremor at all. Bilateral asterixis means metabolic encephalopathy (hepatic, uraemic, hypercapnic, drug-induced — gabapentin, phenytoin, valproate, lithium, ceftazidime). Unilateral asterixis is a focal lesion — usually contralateral thalamus, internal capsule or midbrain — and is a genuinely localising sign that is repeatedly missed because nobody holds the arms up for long enough. Hold the posture for 30 seconds.

Myoclonus-dystonia (SGCE) presents in childhood with alcohol-responsive myoclonus of the neck, trunk and arms plus mild dystonia, and frequently with psychiatric comorbidity. The alcohol responsiveness is a strong clue. Perampanel has emerging evidence for the subcortical myoclonus component; clonazepam and valproate are the conventional options, and GPi DBS is used in refractory cases.
Drug-induced myoclonus is broad and under-recognised. Opioids (especially at high dose or in renal failure), gabapentinoids, antidepressants, antipsychotics, antibiotics (cephalosporins, carbapenems, quinolones), lithium, levodopa, contrast agents, and antiepileptics themselves. In a hospitalised patient with new myoclonus, read the chart before you order an EEG.

14 Restless legs syndrome

▎Key points
  • Five essential criteria (URGE): an Urge to move, worse at Rest, relieved by movement (Getting up), worse in the Evening/night — and not better explained by another condition (leg cramps, positional discomfort, neuropathy, akathisia).
  • Check ferritin and transferrin saturation in everyone. Iron repletion is the single most effective disease-modifying intervention, and the threshold is much higher than for anaemia: treat if ferritin <75 µg/L or TSAT <20%.
  • Alpha-2-delta ligands (gabapentin, pregabalin, gabapentin enacarbil) are now preferred first-line over dopamine agonists because agonists cause augmentation.
  • Augmentation — earlier onset, greater intensity, spread to the arms, shorter latency at rest — is the central long-term hazard of dopaminergic treatment and is often mistaken for disease progression, then treated with more agonist.

15 Stiff-person spectrum disorders

▎Key points
  • Axial and proximal rigidity with superimposed painful spasms, exaggerated lumbar lordosis, and a startle-triggered, stimulus-sensitive quality.
  • High-titre anti-GAD65 is the classic association; anti-amphiphysin points to a paraneoplastic cause (breast, small-cell lung) and demands a malignancy search; glycine receptor antibodies associate with PERM.
  • It is strongly associated with other autoimmune disease — meta-analysis confirms a high rate of comorbid autoimmunity, type 1 diabetes and thyroid disease above all.
  • Two arms of treatment: symptomatic GABAergic (benzodiazepines, baclofen) and immunotherapy (IVIG has the best trial evidence; steroids, rituximab, plasma exchange).

The spectrum runs from focal/stiff-limb syndrome, through classic stiff-person syndrome, to PERM (progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus — brainstem signs, autonomic instability, often glycine-receptor antibody positive and often severe). Electromyography shows continuous motor unit activity in an agonist–antagonist pair at rest that cannot be voluntarily relaxed.

It is very commonly misdiagnosed as functional or psychiatric, because the spasms are triggered by emotion and startle, the patients develop marked task-specific phobias (crossing a road, open spaces) that look like agoraphobia, and early examination between spasms can be near-normal. Anxiety in stiff-person syndrome is usually a consequence of the falls and the spasms, not the cause of them. Take a careful history of stimulus-triggered stiffening and look for the fixed lumbar lordosis.
Do not stop benzodiazepines abruptly in these patients. They are often on very high doses of diazepam or clonazepam for genuine reasons, and abrupt reduction — sometimes attempted by a team who see the dose and assume dependence — can precipitate severe spasms, autonomic instability and death.

Explaining the diagnosis

  1. Name it. "You have a functional movement disorder." Give it a real name rather than a list of things it is not.
  2. Show them the sign. Demonstrate the entrainment or the Hoover sign. This is the most persuasive thing you can do — the diagnosis is visible.
  3. Explain the mechanism. A problem with the software, not the hardware: the nervous system is structurally intact but the movement is not being generated normally. Emphasise that the movements are genuinely involuntary.
  4. Say it is not imagined and not their fault, and that it is potentially reversible.
  5. Refer to physiotherapy with a functional-disorder approach — retraining normal movement, not strengthening. Psychological therapy where there are relevant comorbidities, but do not present it as a psychiatric diagnosis.
Two errors, in opposite directions. Diagnosing "functional" because the imaging was normal and the presentation was odd — many organic movement disorders are odd, and dystonia in particular has been repeatedly misdiagnosed this way for decades. And missing a functional overlay in a patient with established PD, where fluctuating, distractible symptoms lead to endless and futile increases in dopaminergic medication.

18 Hemifacial spasm

▎Key points
  • Unilateral, involuntary, synchronous contraction of muscles supplied by the facial nerve, usually starting in orbicularis oculi and spreading down the face; it persists in sleep.

  • Botulinum toxin is first-line — effective in the great majority, repeated every 3–4 months.

19 Drug-induced movement disorders

▎Key points
  • Timing separates them. Acute dystonic reaction — hours to days. Akathisia — days to weeks. Parkinsonism — weeks to months. Tardive syndromes — months to years, and by definition after ≥3 months of exposure (≥1 month over age 60).
  • Akathisia is the one that gets treated with more of the drug that caused it. Ask about the inner restlessness, not just the movement.
  • Tardive dyskinesia is often irreversible — and increasing the dose masks it temporarily, which is how it becomes permanent.
  • VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) are the evidence-based treatment for tardive dyskinesia; anticholinergics help tardive dystonia but worsen tardive dyskinesia.

VMAT2 inhibitors in tardive dyskinesia. Four double-blind trials underpin current practice: deutetrabenazine (ARM-TD, AIM-TD) and valbenazine (KINECT-3, J-KINECT). Anticholinergics: right drug, wrong syndrome. An evidence-based review of anticholinergic use in drug-induced movement disorders makes the same point: prophylactic long-term anticholinergics with antipsychotics are not justified, and they are continued far too often after the original indication has gone.

20 Wilson disease, NBIA & PFBC

▎Key points
  • Wilson disease is treatable and fatal if missed. Exclude it in every patient under 50 with an unexplained movement disorder, and in anyone with a movement disorder plus abnormal liver function or unexplained psychiatric illness.
  • No single test is sufficient. A normal caeruloplasmin does not exclude Wilson — it is an acute-phase reactant and is normal in a meaningful minority.
  • Neurological Wilson almost always has Kayser–Fleischer rings on slit lamp — but hepatic presentations often do not, so their absence never excludes the disease.
  • Never start D-penicillamine alone in a patient with neurological Wilson without specialist input — paradoxical neurological worsening is well described.

  • NMS versus serotonin syndrome turns on the muscle findings and the reflexes: lead-pipe rigidity and hyporeflexia in NMS; clonus, hyperreflexia and myoclonus in serotonin syndrome, greatest in the legs.

22 Landmark trials — who was included & excluded

▎Key points
  • Every practice-changing number below came from a specific enrolled population — the eligibility criteria are the indication.
  • The recurring gatekeepers in movement disorders: age, disease duration, severity threshold on a named scale, prior drug failures, cognitive and psychiatric screening, and QTc.
  • Note how often cognitive impairment and depression are exclusions — which is precisely why you must screen for both before offering these treatments.

Parkinson disease

LEAP — early versus delayed levodopa (long-term follow-up, Mov Disord 2024)

IncludedExcluded
  • Diagnosis of Parkinson disease within the previous 2 years by standard clinical criteria
  • Insufficient disability to warrant antiparkinson medication at entry
  • Age ≥30 years; life expectancy >2 years
  • For the follow-up: completed the first 80 weeks and consented
  • Any previous antiparkinson medication
  • Tremor as the most prominent symptom — a severe near-continuous or disabling rest tremor
  • Dementia
  • Features indicating atypical or secondary parkinsonism

Result: at 5 years (321 completers) no difference in adjusted total UPDRS (difference 0.24, SE 0.13, P = 0.07); no difference in wearing-off prevalence or levodopa equivalent daily dose. Note the exclusions: this does not tell you about tremor-dominant or already-disabled patients.

ADAGIO — delayed-start rasagiline (NEJM 2009)

IncludedExcluded
  • Men and women aged 30–80 years, not currently receiving any PD treatment
  • PD diagnosed by ≥2 of 3 cardinal features (rest tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity); if no rest tremor, unilateral onset required
  • Any antiparkinsonian medication for >3 weeks previously; rasagiline or selegiline at any dose, or coenzyme Q10 >300 mg/day, within 120 days
  • Disease duration >18 months since diagnosis
  • Hoehn and Yahr stage ≥3
  • Atypical or secondary parkinsonism

Result: early-start 1 mg/day met all three hierarchical endpoints (0.09 vs 0.14 UPDRS points/week between weeks 12–36, P = 0.01; 2.82 vs 4.52 points worsening at 72 weeks). Early-start 2 mg/day did not. The dose dissociation is the reason neuroprotection is not accepted.

EARLYSTIM — STN stimulation with early motor complications (NEJM 2013)

IncludedExcluded
  • Age 18–60 years
  • Disease duration ≥4 years
  • Hoehn and Yahr <3 in the on-medication state
  • ≥50% improvement in motor signs with dopaminergic medication (UPDRS-III)
  • Fluctuations or dyskinesia present for ≤3 years
  • UPDRS-II >6 in the worst condition despite treatment, or mild-to-moderate social/occupational impairment
  • Dementia (Mattis Dementia Rating Scale ≤130)
  • Major depression with suicidal thoughts (Beck Depression Inventory II >25)
  • Acute psychosis
  • Any medical or psychological problem interfering with the protocol
  • Disease duration <4 years — deliberately, because atypical parkinsonism would be expected to declare itself by then

Result: PDQ-39 improved 7.8 points with stimulation, worsened 0.2 with medical therapy; between-group difference 8.0 points (P = 0.002), plus superiority for motor disability, ADLs and motor complications.

Tremor

Focused ultrasound thalamotomy for essential tremor (NEJM 2016)

IncludedExcluded
  • Essential tremor diagnosed by a movement-disorders neurologist
  • Moderate-to-severe postural or intention hand tremor (CRST component score ≥2)
  • Disabling — score ≥2 on any of the 8 CRST disability items
  • Tremor refractory to ≥2 medication trials, including ≥1 first-line agent (propranolol or primidone)
  • Stable doses for 30 days; adequate skull density ratio ≥0.45 on screening CT
  • Neurodegenerative condition
  • Unstable cardiac disease; coagulopathy; DVT risk factors
  • Severe depression (PHQ-9 ≥20)
  • Cognitive impairment (MMSE <24)
  • Previous brain procedure — TMS, DBS, stereotactic lesioning or ECT

Result: hand tremor improved 47% vs 0.1% sham; between-group difference 8.3 points (95% CI 5.9–10.7, P<0.001). Gait disturbance and paraesthesia/numbness (38%) persisted at 12 months in 9% and 14%. At 3 years, 38–50% hand-tremor improvement sustained. The skull density ratio is the criterion most often forgotten — some patients are anatomically ineligible.

Focused ultrasound thalamotomy in tremor-dominant PD (JAMA Neurol 2017)

IncludedExcluded
  • Adults with idiopathic tremor-dominant PD confirmed by UK Brain Bank criteria by a movement-disorders neurologist
  • Tremor deemed medication-refractory, severe and disabling
  • Randomised 2:1 to FUS thalamotomy or sham; 27 patients (26 male, median age 67.8)
  • As per the ET trial: significant cognitive impairment, unstable medical disease, coagulopathy, inadequate skull density ratio

Result: on-medication median tremor scores improved 62% (IQR 22–79%) vs 22% after sham. Adverse events: orofacial paraesthesia (20%), finger paraesthesia (5%), ataxia (5%). Unilateral and lesional — it treats tremor only, not bradykinesia, rigidity or fluctuations.

Chorea

Tetrabenazine in Huntington disease (TETRA-HD, Neurology 2006)

IncludedExcluded
  • HD confirmed by characteristic chorea, family history and expanded CAG repeat (n ≥37)
  • Independently ambulatory
  • Screening total functional capacity >5
  • Total maximal chorea ≥10 on the UHDRS motor subscale
  • Accompanied by a caregiver
  • Disabling depression, dysphagia or dysarthria
  • Previous tetrabenazine; current dopamine-depleting drugs, D2 blockers, MAOIs, levodopa, dopamine agonists, amantadine or memantine (D2 blockers allowed if off ≥4 weeks)

Result: chorea fell 5.0 UHDRS units versus 1.5 on placebo (adjusted effect −3.5 ± 0.8).

Deutetrabenazine in Huntington disease (First-HD, JAMA 2016)

IncludedExcluded
  • Ambulatory adults with manifest HD; characteristic motor findings and CAG ≥36
  • UHDRS total maximal chorea ≥8 at screening and baseline
  • Total functional capacity ≥5 (stages I–III)
  • Serious untreated or undertreated psychiatric illness (stable antidepressants allowed)
  • HADS depression subscale ≥11, or any history of significant suicidal thoughts or behaviour
  • Prolonged QTc, left bundle-branch block, hepatic or renal impairment
  • Swallowing Disturbance Questionnaire ≥11; UPDRS speech item ≥3
  • QT-prolonging drugs other than escitalopram/citalopram

Result: total maximal chorea 12.1 → 7.7 with deutetrabenazine versus 13.2 → 11.3 with placebo over 12 weeks. The depression, suicidality, QTc and swallowing exclusions are the safety checklist you should run before prescribing either drug.

Dystonia

DaxibotulinumtoxinA in cervical dystonia (ASPEN-1)

IncludedExcluded
  • Adults 18–80 years
  • Moderate-to-severe cervical dystonia — TWSTRS total score ≥20
  • Randomised 3:3:1 to DAXI 125U, DAXI 250U or placebo (n = 357); 83% had prior botulinum toxin
  • Predominant retrocollis or anterocollis posture
  • Cervical dystonia attributable to an underlying aetiology (i.e. secondary dystonia)

Result: TWSTRS improvement −12.0 (125U) versus placebo; median duration of effect 24.1 weeks (125U). Treatment-related dysphagia 4.7%, muscle weakness 5.4%.

Tics

Behavioural therapy in Tourette syndrome (meta-analysis of RCTs)

IncludedExcluded
  • Randomised controlled trials only
  • Participants aged ≤18 with Tourette syndrome or chronic tic disorder
  • Intervention was behavioural therapy (CBIT, HRT, CBT) versus a non-behavioural comparator
  • Reported YGTSS or PTQ outcomes
  • Non-randomised studies
  • Participants aged >18
  • Non-behavioural interventions
  • No YGTSS or PTQ measure available

Result: behavioural therapy produced significant tic reduction with benefit sustained at follow-up. Note the age ceiling — the adult evidence base is much thinner, which matters when you are asked to treat a 25-year-old.
