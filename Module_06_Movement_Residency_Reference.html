1. Phenomenology first: the diagnostic approach

Movement-disorder diagnosis begins by naming the phenomenology, then classifying as hypokinetic (parkinsonism — too little movement) or hyperkinetic (too much movement), and only then reasoning to an etiology. Getting the phenomenology right is the whole game; a tremor mistaken for ataxia sends the entire workup astray.

TremorRhythmic oscillation; classify by rest / postural / kineticPD (rest), essential tremor (action)

ChoreaBrief, random, flowing, unpredictable; flows between body partsHuntington disease
DystoniaSustained/patterned co-contraction → twisting postures; sensory trick (geste)Cervical dystonia

Two bedside discriminators. A rest tremor that dampens with action suggests parkinsonism; an action tremor that disappears at rest suggests essential tremor. Suppressibility + a premonitory urge identifies a tic; distractibility/variability identifies a functional movement disorder.

2. Parkinson disease: diagnosis

Parkinsonism = bradykinesia plus rest tremor and/or rigidity. Idiopathic Parkinson disease is the commonest cause, supported by asymmetric onset, a 4–6 Hz pill-rolling rest tremor, and a robust, sustained response to levodopa. Non-motor features — hyposmia, REM sleep behavior disorder, constipation, depression — often precede motor onset by years. Pathology is α-synuclein Lewy pathology with nigrostriatal dopaminergic loss; the provided global-genetics review underscores monogenic (e.g., LRRK2, GBA, PRKN) and polygenic contributions and their ancestry-dependent frequencies.

Red flags against idiopathic PD ("parkinson-plus" clues): early falls, early autonomic failure, symmetric onset, poor/absent levodopa response, early dementia or hallucinations, vertical gaze palsy, cerebellar signs, or rapid progression — pursue an atypical diagnosis (§4).

3. Parkinson disease: therapy & the key trials

Levodopa (with a decarboxylase inhibitor) remains the most effective symptomatic drug. Other classes: dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine), MAO-B inhibitors (rasagiline, selegiline), COMT inhibitors, amantadine (also for dyskinesia), and anticholinergics (tremor, younger patients). Long-term levodopa is limited by motor fluctuations (wearing-off, on–off) and dyskinesia; agonists cause less dyskinesia but more somnolence, edema, and impulse-control disorders.

Levodopa timing — LEAP (NEJM 2019; long-term follow-up 2024). Early versus delayed levodopa (levodopa/carbidopa 300/75 mg/day for 80 weeks vs delayed start) showed no disease-modifying effect — levodopa is symptomatic, not neuroprotective, and does not accelerate progression. The corollary (also in the provided review on delaying levodopa): there is no benefit to withholding levodopa when a patient needs symptom control.
Neuroprotection — ADAGIO (delayed-start rasagiline). A delayed-start design testing whether rasagiline is disease-modifying; results were dose-inconsistent (1 mg met, 2 mg did not, the hierarchical endpoints), so disease modification was not established. Practically, MAO-B inhibitors are useful symptomatic/adjunctive agents, not proven neuroprotectants.
Device therapy — EARLYSTIM (NEJM 2013). Subthalamic deep-brain stimulation added to medical therapy in patients with early motor complications (mean age ~52, disease ~7.5 y) improved quality of life and motor disability versus best medical therapy — supporting DBS earlier than the traditional late-stage indication in selected patients.
Impulse-control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping/eating) are dose-related class effects of dopamine agonists — screen at every visit, warn patients and families, and reduce/rotate the agonist if they emerge.

The provided PSP/MSA cognitive-behavioural and MSA functional-MRI papers emphasize that cognitive and behavioural involvement is common in these "parkinson-plus" syndromes and aids differentiation from idiopathic PD.

Always take a drug history. Drug-induced parkinsonism (dopamine-receptor blockers) is the most common reversible mimic — stop the offending agent before labeling a patient with PD.

5. Tremor & essential tremor

Essential tremor (ET) is a bilateral, largely symmetric action (postural/kinetic) tremor of the hands/forearms, often with head or voice involvement and a family history; alcohol responsiveness is characteristic. The concept of "ET-plus" designates ET with additional soft signs (e.g., questionable dystonic posturing, mild memory impairment). Distinguish from PD (rest tremor, bradykinesia), dystonic tremor, and enhanced physiologic tremor.

Treatment

  • First-line medical: propranolol and primidone.
  • MRI-guided focused-ultrasound thalamotomy (VIM): a randomized sham-controlled trial demonstrated significant, durable reduction of medication-refractory hand tremor after unilateral incisionless thalamotomy, with sustained benefit at 3-year follow-up; it is now an established option alongside DBS for refractory ET (and for tremor-dominant PD).
  • Deep-brain stimulation (VIM): effective, and bilateral/adjustable, for refractory tremor.
Focused ultrasound (provided RCT + 3-year follow-up). Incisionless, MRI-guided thermal ablation of the thalamic VIM improves contralateral hand tremor and quality of life versus sham; gait/balance and sensory paresthesias are the main adverse effects, and current practice treats one side to limit bilateral risks.

6. Dystonia

Dystonia is sustained or intermittent muscle co-contraction producing twisting, patterned, often directional postures/movements, frequently with a sensory trick (geste antagoniste) that transiently relieves it. Classify by axis: clinical (age at onset, body distribution — focal/segmental/generalized, temporal pattern, associated features) and etiologic (inherited/acquired/idiopathic; the provided pathogenesis review highlights shared network mechanisms and neurodevelopmental origins). Common focal forms: cervical dystonia, blepharospasm, laryngeal dystonia, writer's cramp.

Treatment

  • Botulinum toxin — first-line for focal dystonia (e.g., cervical dystonia). The resource set includes randomized data for newer/longer-acting formulations (daxibotulinumtoxinA) and quality-of-life gains with a liquid abobotulinumtoxinA formulation.
  • Oral agents (trihexyphenidyl, baclofen, benzodiazepines) for generalized/segmental disease; a levodopa trial in any young-onset dystonia to catch dopa-responsive dystonia.
  • DBS (globus pallidus internus) for medically refractory generalized/segmental and selected cervical dystonia.
Never miss dopa-responsive dystonia. Childhood-onset dystonia (often diurnal, worsening through the day) can respond dramatically to low-dose levodopa — a therapeutic trial is mandatory.

7. Chorea, Huntington disease & tardive syndromes

Chorea is brief, random, flowing, unpredictable movement. Causes span genetic (Huntington disease — autosomal-dominant CAG repeat in HTT; plus the provided review of rare hereditary/acquired choreas), immune/infectious (Sydenham, autoimmune), metabolic (hyperglycemic non-ketotic chorea with characteristic striatal T1 hyperintensity), vascular, and drug-induced. Huntington disease combines chorea with cognitive decline and psychiatric disturbance.

Symptomatic treatment of chorea

VMAT2 inhibitors. Tetrabenazine and deutetrabenazine reduce chorea in Huntington disease (deutetrabenazine offers a better tolerability/less-frequent-dosing profile than tetrabenazine); the provided safety and efficacy data support their use. For tardive dyskinesia, the VMAT2 inhibitors valbenazine and deutetrabenazine are the evidence-based, FDA-approved treatments (comparative analysis provided).
Tetrabenazine caution: depression/suicidality and parkinsonism are dose-limiting; screen mood before and during therapy. Prevent tardive dyskinesia by minimizing dopamine-receptor-blocker exposure in the first place.

8. Tics, Tourette syndrome & restless legs

Tics are stereotyped, temporarily suppressible movements/vocalizations preceded by a premonitory urge and relieved by performing the tic. Tourette syndrome requires multiple motor tics plus ≥1 vocal tic with childhood onset; ADHD and OCD are common comorbidities. Note the recent phenomenon of functional tic-like behaviors (abrupt onset, older age, complex phrases, lack of premonitory urge) as a distinct entity.

Behavioural therapy first (provided). Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT)/habit-reversal training produces sustained tic reduction in children and adolescents and is a first-line treatment. Pharmacotherapy (alpha-2 agonists such as clonidine/guanfacine; VMAT2 inhibitors; antipsychotics) is added for impairing tics.

PANDAS (pediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorder associated with streptococcal infection) is a controversial post-streptococcal abrupt-onset tic/OCD syndrome covered in the resource set. Restless legs syndrome (RLS) — an urge to move the legs, worse at rest and in the evening, relieved by movement — is treated by correcting iron deficiency (check ferritin; supplement if low), then alpha-2-delta ligands (gabapentin/pregabalin) as preferred first-line drug therapy; dopamine agonists are now second-line because of augmentation.

9. Functional movement disorders

Functional (psychogenic) movement disorders are common and potentially reversible. Diagnosis has shifted from exclusion to a positive rule-in approach based on internal inconsistency (distractibility, variability, entrainment of tremor to an external rhythm) and incongruity with recognized disease, with electrophysiology as a useful adjunct. Management rests on a clear, validating explanation of the diagnosis, demonstration of the positive signs to the patient, and FND-informed physiotherapy/psychological therapy.

Make a positive diagnosis. Tremor entrainment (the functional tremor takes on the frequency of a voluntarily tapped rhythm) and distractibility are rule-in signs — deliver the diagnosis confidently and without stigma (cross-links to Module 21).
