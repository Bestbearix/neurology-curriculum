Why this module?

Neurology is the field where careful bedside reasoning still beats the scanner. Both Adams & Victor's and Merritt's open with the same point: an experienced neurologist arrives at the right diagnostic territory before ordering imaging, by listening carefully and examining systematically. This module rebuilds that habit for the practicing physician.

What you will be able to do after this module

  • Take a focused neurologic history that captures tempo, quality, and red flags.
  • Perform a screening neurologic exam in 5–7 minutes and know when to expand to the comprehensive exam.
  • Translate exam findings into an anatomic localization — cortex, brainstem, cord, root, plexus, nerve, NMJ, or muscle.
  • Recognize the half-dozen syndromes that demand urgent referral.

1. The clinical method — the five-step (or four-question) framework

Adams & Victor's frames neurologic reasoning as a 5-step sequence:

  1. Gather symptoms and signs (history + exam).
  2. Interpret them in terms of physiology and anatomy.
  3. Localize — name the part(s) affected. Recognize the syndrome.
  4. From anatomy + tempo + course + history, infer the etiology.
  5. Estimate functional impact and prognosis.

Merritt's reframes the same as four questions, asked in parallel:

  1. What anatomic structures are affected?
  2. What is the nature of the disorder (syndrome / category)?
  3. What are the most likely etiologies?
  4. What "can't miss" diagnosis must be excluded?
Pearl: If you cannot answer Question 1, you almost certainly cannot answer the others. Start with anatomy.
2. The neurologic history — what to ask and why

The five elements that separate a neurologic history from a generic one:

  • Timing & tempo. Sudden onset (seconds) suggests vascular or seizure; minutes-to-hours suggests migraine or demyelination; days suggests infection or GBS; months suggests tumor; years suggests degenerative.
  • Quality. "Dizzy" is meaningless — refine into vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, or psychogenic. "Numb" must become loss of sensation, paresthesia, or weakness.
  • Aggravators / alleviators. Postural change, exertion, heat, head position, Valsalva.
  • Associated symptoms. Headache + focal deficit; weakness + bowel/bladder; vertigo + hearing loss.
  • Witness account. Mandatory for seizures, syncope, dementia, sleep events. Patients cannot describe their own loss of awareness.

Merritt's emphasises a special discipline: avoid leading questions, clarify ambiguous terms, and explicitly ask questions designed to refute your leading hypothesis. Diagnostic momentum (the copy-pasted EHR diagnosis) is a known source of error.

Watch out: Long-standing headache → usually primary (migraine, tension). New headache — especially after 50 — needs structural workup. The history element that matters most is "Is this new for you?"

5. Cranial nerves — high-yield localizing patterns

Examine I → XII in order. Two patterns matter most for the general physician:

  • CN VII — UMN vs LMN: UMN spares the forehead (bilateral cortical input); LMN involves the whole face. Bell's palsy is LMN.
  • CN III — pupil tells you the urgency: dilated pupil → surgical (PCom aneurysm, uncal herniation); spared pupil → usually microvascular (diabetes, HTN).

Other key signs:

  • RAPD → optic-nerve or retinal disease on that side.
  • Bitemporal hemianopia → chiasm (pituitary lesion).
  • Homonymous hemianopia → retrochiasmal lesion (PCA territory if congruous + complete).
  • Tongue deviates toward the weak side (LMN, CN XII).
  • Uvula deviates away from the weak side (CN X).
HINTS exam (for acute vertigo): central features are normal head-impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, skew deviation. Better than early MRI for posterior stroke in skilled hands.
6. Motor exam — bulk, tone, strength, drift

Sequence: observe → bulk → tone → strength → drift.

Tone types: spasticity (velocity-dependent, UMN), rigidity (lead-pipe/cogwheel, basal ganglia), paratonia (frontal/dementia), flaccidity (LMN or acute UMN).

MRC strength scale: 0 none · 1 flicker · 2 gravity-eliminated · 3 against gravity · 4 against resistance · 5 normal.

Pronator drift is the most sensitive bedside sign for subtle pyramidal weakness. A drift with downward and pronating motion → UMN pattern.

Pearl: Both UMN and LMN signs in the same patient (e.g. hyperreflexia with fasciculations) is the classic combination for motor neuron disease (ALS).
7. Sensory exam — which tract, which pattern

Two main tracts to remember:

  • Spinothalamic (pain, temperature) — crosses at the level of entry into the cord.
  • Dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception) — cross in the medulla.
  • Light touch — carried in both.

8. Coordination, gait, and stance

Cerebellar disease causes ipsilateral findings: dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, hypotonia, scanning dysarthria. Midline lesions impair trunk and gait disproportionately; hemispheric lesions impair the limbs on the same side.

Gait is fast and rich diagnostic information — always watch the patient walk.

Romberg test assesses dorsal columns and vestibular input, not cerebellum. A patient who falls only with eyes closed has a positive Romberg.
9. Localization principles — a practical algorithm
  1. Central or peripheral? Hyperreflexia, upgoing toe, spasticity, cognitive or cranial-nerve signs → central. Hyporeflexia, atrophy, fasciculations, dermatomal sensory loss → peripheral.
  2. Central → where?
    • Cortex: aphasia, neglect, focal seizure, cortical sensation loss.
    • Subcortex / capsule: dense hemiparesis without cortical signs.
    • Brainstem: cranial nerve ipsilateral + long-tract contralateral.
    • Cerebellum: ipsilateral ataxia without weakness.
    • Cord: sensory level, bilateral long-tract signs, sphincter loss.
  3. Peripheral → where?
    • Root: dermatome + myotome ± pain.
    • Plexus: multi-root, multi-nerve in one limb.
    • Single nerve: classic territory.
    • Polyneuropathy: symmetric, length-dependent.
    • NMJ: fatigable, ocular/bulbar, no sensory loss.
    • Muscle: proximal > distal, no sensory loss, preserved reflexes early.

10. Red flags — when to refer urgently
Same-day or emergency referral:
  • Sudden focal deficit (stroke / TIA window)
  • Thunderclap headache (SAH until proven otherwise)
  • New headache + fever and/or stiff neck (meningitis)
  • New severe headache after 50 with jaw claudication or scalp tenderness (GCA)
  • First-ever seizure or status epilepticus
  • Acute spinal cord syndrome: bilateral leg weakness, sensory level, or new sphincter problem
  • Acute ascending weakness with areflexia (Guillain-Barré)
  • Rapidly progressive bulbar weakness or dyspnea (MG crisis, GBS)
  • Sudden monocular vision loss
  • Acute vertigo with any central HINTS feature
  • Painful CN III palsy with dilated pupil (PCom aneurysm)
  • Cauda equina symptoms

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 68-year-old man with sudden left arm weakness

He was watching TV when his left arm went limp. On arrival 40 min later: alert, speaks fluently with no aphasia. Left lower-face droop, left arm and leg 2/5, sensory loss left side, left homonymous hemianopia. Reflexes 3+ on left, plantar upgoing on left.

What is your localization?

This is a complete right MCA-territory picture: face + arm + leg + hemisensory + hemianopia + cortical signs in a vascular tempo. UMN signs already (hyper-reflexia, Babinski). Activate the stroke pathway — time is brain.

Teaching point: "Face + arm + leg one side + hemisensory + hemianopia" = large vessel cortical stroke. Smaller lacunar (capsular) strokes give pure motor or pure sensory hemi-syndromes without cortical signs.
Case 2 — A 34-year-old woman with diplopia and a numb cheek

Two days of horizontal double vision worse on right gaze, and a "fuzzy" patch over the right cheek. Two years ago she had a transient episode of painful right vision loss that resolved over weeks.

Localization?

The earlier episode is consistent with right optic neuritis. The current findings — failure of left-eye adduction on right gaze with abducting nystagmus of the right eye, and ipsilateral V1 sensory loss — suggest a left medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion (INO) with V territory involvement, i.e. a pontine/midbrain lesion. Two CNS lesions, separated in space and time, in a young woman → multiple sclerosis.

Case 3 — A 58-year-old man with worsening leg stiffness and urinary urgency

Six months of progressive stiffness in both legs, worse at the end of the day. New urinary urgency. Neck pain. Exam: spastic gait, both legs 4/5 with increased tone, brisk knee jerks, sustained ankle clonus, upgoing plantars, sensory level at the umbilicus (T10). Arms normal.

Localization?

Bilateral UMN findings in the legs + sensory level + sphincter symptoms = spinal cord lesion above T10. Neck pain points to cervical spondylotic myelopathy or a compressive lesion at the cervical level. Urgent MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine.

Don't miss: any progressive paraparesis with bowel/bladder change is a cord lesion until proven otherwise — image both above the suspected level and the conus.
Case 4 — A 26-year-old woman with droopy eyes by evening

Several weeks of intermittent double vision and droopy eyelids, mostly in the evening. Says she chews fine at the start of a meal but tires by the end. Exam in the morning: nearly normal. After sustained upgaze, ptosis worsens; ice-pack improves it. No sensory loss. Reflexes normal.

Localization?

Fatigable, fluctuating ocular & bulbar weakness without sensory loss → neuromuscular junction. The clinical picture is myasthenia gravis. Confirm with acetylcholine-receptor antibodies, repetitive nerve stimulation / single-fibre EMG. Always check forced vital capacity — respiratory failure can develop quickly.
