Why this module?

Stroke is the most common life-threatening neurological diagnosis a general physician will meet. Outcomes are extraordinarily time-sensitive: every 15 minutes saved in door-to-needle time translates into a real reduction in 90-day disability. This module covers what you need to recognise, manage in the first hour, and follow up afterwards.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Distinguish ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke clinically and confirm with CT.
  • Activate a stroke pathway and run the first-hour workflow.
  • Decide whether a patient is a candidate for tPA, thrombectomy, or both.
  • Recognise the major vascular territory syndromes and lacunar patterns.
  • Run BP, anticoagulation reversal, and acute management for ICH and SAH.
  • Build an aetiology-tailored secondary prevention plan.

2026 AHA/ASA Guideline — What's New

Prabhakaran et al. Stroke. 2026;57. The fourteen practice-changing updates below replace the 2018 AIS guideline and 2019 update.

1. Tenecteplase = alteplase as a first-line thrombolytic (Class 1, LOE A)

Within 4.5 h, give either:

  • Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus (max 25 mg) — single push over 5–10 seconds.
  • Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg IV (max 90 mg) — 10% bolus then 60-min infusion.

Both have equivalent efficacy and safety across > 6000 patients in phase 3 RCTs. TNK has practical advantages: a single bolus, simpler dosing, faster administration.

Tenecteplase 0.4 mg/kg is NOT recommended — potential harm at higher dose.
2. Don't delay thrombolysis for advanced imaging (Class 1)

For patients with disabling deficits within 4.5 h, NCCT alone is sufficient to give tPA or TNK. Do not wait for CTA, CTP, or MRA — they may follow but should not delay IVT in eligible patients.

3. Mild non-disabling stroke → DAPT, not thrombolysis

For NIHSS ≤ 5 with non-disabling deficits within 4.5 h, IVT shows NO benefit (PRISMS, ARAMIS). Give aspirin + clopidogrel for 21 days then SAPT. Class 3: No Benefit for IVT in this group.

2026 update also expands DAPT recommendation for minor (NIHSS ≤ 5) noncardioembolic AIS or high-risk TIA (ABCD² ≥ 4) within 24–72 h, especially with presumed atherosclerotic cause. Class 2a.

What counts as "clearly disabling" at NIHSS 0–5? Complete hemianopsia, severe aphasia, severe hemi-inattention, weakness preventing sustained anti-gravity effort. NOT clearly disabling: isolated mild aphasia, isolated facial droop, mild cortical hand weakness, mild sensorimotor loss with preserved ambulation.
4. Extended-window IVT (4.5–9 h or wake-up) — Class 2a

For patients with salvageable penumbra on CT perfusion, or DWI–FLAIR mismatch on MRI:

  • 4.5–9 h from last known well, OR
  • wake-up stroke within 9 h of midpoint of sleep

IVT is reasonable to improve functional outcomes (EXTEND, WAKE-UP trials).

5. No adjuvant antithrombotics with IVT — Class 3: No Benefit

Argatroban and eptifibatide should NOT be added to tPA to improve outcomes. The MOST trial and other studies show no benefit.

NNT for large-core (ASPECTS 3–5) EVT is approximately 8. Functional independence achieved in ~20% vs ~8% with medical therapy alone.

7. Basilar artery occlusion → EVT (NEW Class 1)

For basilar occlusion with mRS 0–1, NIHSS ≥ 10, PC-ASPECTS ≥ 6 within 24 h, EVT is now Class 1 (ATTENTION, BAOCHE trials).

Outcome: mRS 0–3 in 46% of EVT group vs 24% medical management.

PC-ASPECTS: 10-point scale. Pons and midbrain worth 2 points each; thalami, occipital lobes, cerebellar hemispheres 1 point each.

8 & 9. Blood pressure — keep SBP 140–180 after IVT and EVT

After IVT (first 24 h): SBP 140–180 mmHg. Intensive lowering to < 140 NOT recommended — no benefit. Class 3: No Benefit.

After successful EVT (first 72 h, mTICI 2b/2c/3): Do NOT lower SBP < 140 mmHg — multiple high-quality RCTs show worse outcomes, including higher mortality. Class 3: Harm.

Aggressive BP control may be needed for other indications (tandem chronic high-grade stenosis, malignant hypertension end-organ damage) — but not as a default after reperfusion.

10. Glucose target 140–180 mg/dL (not 80–130)

The SHINE trial demonstrated no benefit of intensive control (80–130 mg/dL) over moderate control (80–180 mg/dL), with more severe hypoglycaemia in the intensive group.

  • Treat hyperglycaemia > 180 mg/dL → target 140–180.
  • Treat hypoglycaemia < 60 mg/dL.
  • Do NOT target 80–130 mg/dL.
11–12. Systems of care — Mobile Stroke Units and direct EVT routing
  • Mobile Stroke Units (CT + thrombolytics in the ambulance) — Class 1 where available; reduce onset-to-treatment time and improve functional outcomes.
  • Direct EVT-centre transport for suspected LVO is reasonable when local SSOC supports it; otherwise, prioritize door-in-door-out (DIDO) < 60 min for interhospital transfer.
  • In well-coordinated systems where the local hospital does thrombolysis well, distant transport to a TSC (45–60 min away) does NOT improve outcomes — Class 3: No Benefit.
13. Early oral anticoagulation after AF-related stroke — Class 2a

In carefully selected (eg, milder severity, smaller infarct) AIS patients with atrial fibrillation, early initiation of oral anticoagulation is low risk and reasonable compared with delayed anticoagulation (ELAN, OPTIMAS trials).

Older "wait N days according to infarct size / NIHSS" rules are no longer mandatory. Practical approach often used:

  • TIA or minor stroke → day 1–3.
  • Moderate stroke → day 3–6.
  • Severe stroke / haemorrhagic transformation → day 6–14 (case by case).
14. Pediatric stroke — first formal recommendations
  • Imaging: MRI/MRA preferred (Class 2a); CT/CTA acceptable if MRI not available within 25 min.
  • IVT (alteplase < 4.5 h): Class 2b — may be considered in age 28 days–18 y with disabling deficits; safety established, efficacy uncertain.
  • EVT, age ≥ 6 y: Class 2a within 6 h of LVO; Class 2a in 6–24 h with salvageable tissue.
  • EVT, age 28 days–6 y: Class 2b in selected patients with LVO < 24 h and salvageable tissue, at centres with pediatric neurointerventional experience.

Lessons

One panel per sitting works well.

1. Recognising stroke — the three criteria

Adams & Victor's diagnostic triad:

  1. Sudden temporal profile.
  2. Focal deficit in a vascular territory.
  3. Clinical setting consistent with vascular disease.

Both ischaemic and haemorrhagic strokes present this way; the CT scanner separates them. Approximately 85% ischaemic, 10% ICH, 5% SAH.

Useful FAST/BE-FAST tools for laypeople, but in a clinical setting any sudden focal deficit is stroke until proven otherwise.

Maximal-at-onset deficit + alert patient = think embolism. Stepwise worsening = think thrombosis (atherothrombotic in situ). Worsening over hours + headache + nausea = think haemorrhage.
2. The first hour — workflow that saves brain

Five parallel tasks (assign to different team members):

  1. ABCs and LOC.
  2. Focused history — exact time of onset / last known well; medications (anticoagulants?); witness account.
  3. Large-bore IV × 2; labs (CBC, electrolytes, glucose, INR/PTT, troponin); finger-stick glucose.
  4. NIHSS.
  5. To CT — non-contrast, ideally with CTA.

Time targets: door-to-CT < 25 min, door-to-needle < 60 min. The Helsinki model demonstrates < 20 min is feasible.

Hypoglycaemia mimics stroke. Always check finger-stick glucose first. Hypo- or hyperglycaemia can unmask old deficits or fake new ones.
3. Imaging — what each modality tells you
StudyQuestion answered
Non-contrast CTIs there blood? (Yes/no — mandatory before tPA.)
CT angiographyIs there a large-vessel occlusion? (Triggers thrombectomy pathway.)
CT perfusionHow much penumbra remains? (Extends thrombectomy and tPA windows.)
MRI / DWIConfirms infarct; distinguishes stroke from mimic; DWI–FLAIR mismatch identifies wake-up strokes eligible for tPA.

Early CT signs of ischaemia: loss of grey-white differentiation, sulcal effacement, hyperdense vessel (visible thrombus). Quantified by ASPECTS (10 minus regions affected); ASPECTS < 7 predicts haemorrhagic transformation after tPA.

4. Reperfusion — tPA

Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg IV (max 90 mg) — 10% bolus, 90% over 1 hour.

Windows: 0–3 h standard (NINDS); 3–4.5 h in selected patients (ECASS III, excludes age > 80 + NIHSS > 25 + diabetes with prior stroke); 4.5–9 h with perfusion mismatch (EXTEND).

BP rule: must be < 185/110 before tPA; keep < 180/105 for 24 h after.

After tPA: no antithrombotics for 24 h; repeat CT before any. Watch for orolingual angioedema (more common with ACEi co-treatment) and symptomatic ICH (~6%).

NNT for tPA: about 5 if given within 90 min, 8 within 3 h, 15 in the 3–4.5 h window. Speed beats almost everything.
5. Reperfusion — thrombectomy

Mechanical thrombectomy for proximal large-vessel occlusion (ICA, M1, basilar, proximal M2):

  • 0–6 h — standard (MR CLEAN, ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT-PRIME, REVASCAT).
  • 6–24 h — standard if perfusion/MRI shows small core, large penumbra (DAWN, DEFUSE-3).

tPA and thrombectomy are not mutually exclusive — give tPA if eligible, then transfer for thrombectomy ("drip-and-ship"). Best NNT for functional independence is around 2.6 in well-selected patients.

7. Lacunar syndromes — the five classics

Small subcortical infarcts from lipohyalinosis of penetrators. No cortical signs. Tightly associated with hypertension and diabetes.

  • Pure motor hemiparesis (posterior limb internal capsule, pons).
  • Pure sensory stroke (VPL thalamus).
  • Sensorimotor stroke (thalamocapsular).
  • Ataxic hemiparesis (pons, capsule).
  • Dysarthria-clumsy hand (pons, anterior internal capsule).
If the patient has aphasia or neglect, it's not lacunar. Cortical involvement = cortical-branch artery (embolic or atherothrombotic).
8. Intracerebral haemorrhage — the first hour

30-day mortality 30–40%; volume is the strongest predictor (ABC/2).

Aetiology by location: deep structures (putamen, thalamus, pons, cerebellum) → hypertensive; lobar in the elderly → amyloid angiopathy; any location in younger or atypical pattern → AVM, tumour, drugs, anticoagulant, venous infarct.

Acute priorities:

  1. Reverse anticoagulation immediately. Warfarin → 4-factor PCC + IV vitamin K. Dabigatran → idarucizumab. Xa inhibitors → andexanet alfa or 4-factor PCC.
  2. BP to systolic 130–150 (nicardipine, clevidipine, or labetalol IV).
  3. ICP measures if mass effect — head-up, mannitol or hypertonic saline, brief hyperventilation as bridge. No steroids.
  4. EVD for hydrocephalus from intraventricular extension.
  5. Cerebellar haemorrhage > 3 cm = neurosurgical emergency.
  6. Treat clinical seizures only; no prophylaxis.
Cerebellar bleed → call neurosurgery now, even if the patient looks well. Brainstem compression can kill quickly.
9. Subarachnoid haemorrhage

80% from saccular aneurysm rupture at the circle of Willis. Classic presentation:

  • Thunderclap headache — worst of life, peak in < 1 min.
  • Transient LOC (50%).
  • Meningism develops over hours.
  • Sentinel leak in 10–40% in the preceding 1–2 weeks.

Diagnosis: non-contrast CT > 99% sensitive within 6 h; LP for xanthochromia if CT negative and clinical suspicion remains. CTA / DSA to find the aneurysm.

Treatment:

  • Secure aneurysm within 24 h — coiling (ISAT favours where feasible) or clipping.
  • BP < 160 systolic until secured.
  • Nimodipine 60 mg PO q4h × 21 days.
  • Watch for vasospasm/DCI (day 4–14), hydrocephalus, hyponatraemia, rebleed, neurogenic stunned myocardium.
10. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis

Often missed. Young women on OCP / pregnant / postpartum; thrombophilia; sepsis; some COVID-19 vaccines.

Headache + seizures or focal deficits that don't respect arterial territories + papilloedema → think CVST.

Diagnose with CT venography or MR venography. Treat with anticoagulation even if haemorrhagic infarction is present. Endovascular intervention in deteriorating patients.

11. TIA and high-risk transient deficits

Tissue-based definition (AHA/ASA 2009): transient neurological dysfunction from ischaemia, without infarction on imaging. Most last < 1 hour.

Short-term stroke risk after TIA: ≈5% in 48 hours, up to 10% in 90 days. ABCD² score estimates risk but admit anyone with crescendo TIAs, AF, or carotid stenosis.

The 21-day rule (CHANCE/POINT): aspirin + clopidogrel for 21–90 days in minor non-cardioembolic stroke or high-risk TIA reduces 90-day stroke ~30% with modest bleeding risk.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 68-year-old with sudden right hemiplegia 90 min ago

BP 200/110, AF on ECG, NIHSS 18, alert with global aphasia and right hemiparesis, no headache.

Localization: dominant (left) MCA stem (M1) — face = arm = leg, aphasia, hemianesthesia.

Plan: Activate stroke pathway. Non-contrast CT + CTA. If no haemorrhage and no completed infarct: lower BP to < 185/110 (labetalol bolus), give tPA, transfer for thrombectomy because CTA almost certainly shows M1 occlusion. AF identified → after acute period, anticoagulate (DOAC unless contraindicated).

Case 2 — A 55-year-old with sudden vertigo, hoarse voice, and difficulty swallowing

Examination: right Horner syndrome, right facial pain/temperature loss, left body pain/temperature loss, right limb ataxia. Limb power normal.

Localization: right lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg). Vertebral artery atherothrombosis most likely (more common than PICA itself).

Plan: CT/CTA of head & neck — look for vertebral artery occlusion or dissection. tPA not contraindicated. Admit to stroke unit, monitor swallow, secondary prevention based on aetiology (dissection → antiplatelet or anticoagulation 3–6 months; atherosclerosis → antiplatelet + statin + risk factors).

Case 3 — A 73-year-old hypertensive woman with sudden left-sided weakness and headache, vomiting twice

BP 230/130, GCS 13, dense left hemiplegia, eyes deviate to the right.

Likely diagnosis: right basal ganglia (putamen) intracerebral haemorrhage — classic hypertensive bleed location.

Plan: Urgent CT confirms. Reverse any anticoagulation immediately. Lower BP with nicardipine to systolic 130–150. ICU admission. ABC/2 measures volume; > 30 mL is high risk. If hydrocephalus from intraventricular extension → EVD. Surgical evacuation rarely beneficial for deep supratentorial bleeds; case-by-case.

Case 4 — A 45-year-old with "worst headache of my life" while jogging, brief LOC

BP 170/100, photophobic, mild neck stiffness, no focal deficit, alert.

Likely diagnosis: aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Plan: Immediate non-contrast CT (> 99% sensitive within 6 h). If positive → CT angiography to localize aneurysm → neurosurgery / interventional neuroradiology for coiling or clipping within 24 h. If CT negative and clinical suspicion remains → LP for xanthochromia (after 12 h). Nimodipine 60 mg PO q4h × 21 d. BP < 160 systolic until aneurysm secured. ICU admission.

Case 5 — A 28-year-old woman on OCP, 5 days of worsening headache, now confused with a seizure

CT shows bilateral parasagittal hyperdensities with surrounding low attenuation.

Likely diagnosis: superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with bilateral parasagittal venous infarcts.

Plan: CT venography or MR venography to confirm. Start anticoagulation (LMWH or UFH) even though there are haemorrhagic infarcts. Stop OCP. Workup for thrombophilia, infection (look for source — otitis, sinusitis). Antiseizure medication. Long-term anticoagulation 3–12 months depending on provocation status.
