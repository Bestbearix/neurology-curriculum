1. Framework — neuro-critical care in one principle

The injured brain is a fragile organ in a fixed box. Every neurological emergency is, at heart, a struggle to keep cerebral perfusion and oxygen delivery adequate while the primary injury heals. Whether the diagnosis is traumatic brain injury, large stroke, intracerebral haemorrhage, subarachnoid haemorrhage, status epilepticus, post-cardiac-arrest brain injury, or fulminant neuromuscular disease, the central job is the prevention of secondary brain injury: hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia, hypercapnia, hyperthermia, hyponatraemia, and seizures.

The single principle: nothing the neurosurgeon, neurologist, or intensivist does inside the skull rescues a brain that has not been kept oxygenated, perfused, normoglycaemic, normocapnic, normothermic, normonatraemic, and seizure-free. Treat the body to save the brain.

3. ICP / CPP physiology — Monro-Kellie

  • The skull is a fixed box containing three compartments: brain (~80%), blood (~10%), CSF (~10%).
  • An increase in one compartment must be matched by reductions in the others, or ICP rises.
  • Normal ICP: 7–15 mmHg in adults supine.
  • Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = MAP − ICP. Normal CPP > 60 mmHg; many neuro-ICU protocols target CPP 60–70 mmHg in TBI and SAH (Brain Trauma Foundation 4th edition: avoid CPP < 60).
  • Cerebral blood flow is autoregulated between MAP ~50 and ~150 in healthy people; autoregulation is often impaired after injury, making the brain pressure-passive — small drops in MAP cause significant ischaemia, and small surges cause vasogenic oedema.
  • PaCO₂ is the strongest physiological modulator of CBF — hyperventilation reduces CBF acutely (vasoconstriction) and is used only as a brief rescue for impending herniation; sustained hyperventilation worsens ischaemia.
Cushing's triad — hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration — is a late sign of brainstem compression. Do not wait for it to act on raised ICP. Earlier warning signs: pupil asymmetry / dilation, deteriorating GCS, posturing.

4. Tiered management of raised ICP

Modern protocols use a tiered approach (Seattle International Severe TBI Consensus 2019; BTF 4th edition). Move sequentially through tiers if ICP remains > 22 mmHg (TBI target) or per indication.

Tier 0 — basic measures (all patients)

  • Head of bed up 30°; head midline (no jugular kinking).
  • Loosen cervical collars and ETT ties.
  • Adequate sedation + analgesia (propofol + opioid first-line); avoid coughing / agitation; lidocaine before suctioning.
  • Avoid hypoxia (SpO₂ ≥ 94%), maintain PaCO₂ 35–40 mmHg, MAP target.
  • Normothermia (acetaminophen, cooling); fever raises ICP and worsens outcome.
  • Glucose 6–10 mmol/L; sodium 140–145 (TBI / mass effect); seizure surveillance + treatment.
  • Treat hyponatraemia cautiously (avoid CPM — Module 13); > 10 mmol/L/24 h is the wrong direction, < 8 the safer limit.

Tier 1 — initial measures for ICP > 22 mmHg

  • Bolus sedation / analgesia.
  • External ventricular drain (EVD) — both monitors ICP and drains CSF.
  • Hyperosmolar therapy:
    • 3% hypertonic saline 250 mL over 15 min (or 23.4% 30 mL over 10 min via central line for emergency); target serum sodium 145–155, osmolality 300–320 mOsm/kg.
    • Mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg IV over 15 min; monitor osmolar gap and renal function; avoid in hypovolaemia.
    • Hypertonic saline is generally preferred in haemodynamically labile patients (mannitol causes diuresis).
  • Treat seizures (Module 3); consider continuous EEG.

Tier 2 — additional measures

  • Deeper sedation; neuromuscular blockade (cisatracurium infusion) — minimises cough, shivering, ventilator dyssynchrony.
  • Mild hyperventilation to PaCO₂ 32–35 mmHg — short-term only; monitor jugular venous saturation or PbtO₂ if available.
  • Re-bolus hyperosmolar.
  • Surgical decompression discussion (decompressive craniectomy for refractory TBI ICP — RESCUEicp trial 2016: NNT meaningful, but high rate of severe disability in survivors; preserves life at the cost of disability).

Tier 3 — refractory ICP

  • Decompressive craniectomy — bifrontal or hemicraniectomy.
  • Barbiturate coma (pentobarbital or thiopental) — burst-suppression on EEG; cardiovascular instability and immunosuppression are major risks.
  • Therapeutic hypothermia (32–34 °C) — not recommended as prophylaxis (Eurotherm 2015: no benefit and possibly harm in TBI); rarely used as rescue for refractory ICP.
Brief hyperventilation (PaCO₂ ~30 mmHg) is reserved for impending herniation as a bridge to definitive treatment. Never use it as a sustained strategy — vasoconstriction reduces CBF and worsens ischaemia.

6. Status epilepticus

Time matters: each minute of seizure activity increases neuronal injury and resistance to treatment.

PhaseTimeTreatment
0–5 min (early)Stabilise; ABCs; glucose; thiamine before glucose if at risk (Module 13)—
5–20 min (established)First-line benzodiazepine: lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg) or midazolam 10 mg IM (or 0.2 mg/kg IV) — repeat once if no responseRAMPART trial (NEJM 2012): IM midazolam non-inferior to IV lorazepam pre-hospital
20–40 min (refractory)Second-line non-benzodiazepine ASM (ESETT trial NEJM 2019: levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, and valproate equally effective ~50% efficacy each):
  • Levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV (max 4500 mg) over 10 min — usually preferred (fewer side effects, no interactions, safe in pregnancy and most comorbidities)
  • Fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg IV over 10 min (max 1500 mg PE) — caution in arrhythmia, hypotension
  • Valproate 40 mg/kg IV over 10 min — avoid in mitochondrial disease, hepatic dysfunction, pregnancy (cross-ref Module 20)
> 40 min (super-refractory)ICU; intubation; anaesthetic infusion: midazolam 0.05–0.5 mg/kg/h, propofol 30–250 µg/kg/min, or pentobarbital — titrate to seizure suppression or burst-suppression on continuous EEG; phenobarbital alternative; ketamine emerging. Treat the underlying cause; rule out NCSE; consider auto-immune encephalitis (Module 11) with empirical IV methylprednisolone + IVIG / PLEX—
Always check glucose, electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), ABG, ammonia (valproate), drug levels, toxicology, pyridoxine deficiency (especially infants — Module 16). For prolonged refractory status, continuous EEG is essential to detect non-convulsive status epilepticus. Cross-ref Module 3.

7. Malignant MCA infarct & decompressive craniectomy

  • Large MCA territory infarct → cytotoxic oedema → mass effect → midline shift → uncal herniation. Mortality up to 80% without surgery.
  • Pooled meta-analysis of DESTINY, DECIMAL, and HAMLET trials (Vahedi Lancet Neurol 2007): decompressive hemicraniectomy within 48 hours in patients < 60 years reduces mortality by ~50% and increases the proportion of patients with mRS ≤ 3 (independent or mild disability).
  • DESTINY II trial (Jüttler NEJM 2014): in patients > 60, hemicraniectomy reduces mortality but the proportion with severe disability is higher; mRS 4 (moderately severe disability) is the most common outcome. Shared decision-making with family is critical.
  • Indications: NIHSS > 15 (right) or > 20 (left), large MCA territory on imaging, decreased GCS, midline shift, < 48 h from onset.
  • Post-op: ICP / CPP management; meticulous fluid balance; avoid hypertonic saline volume overload; manage post-op CSF leak; planned cranioplasty at ~3 months.
  • Cross-ref Module 2.

8. Cerebellar emergencies

  • Cerebellar haemorrhage / large infarct: tight posterior fossa → rapid brainstem compression, fourth ventricle obstruction, obstructive hydrocephalus.
  • Clinical: occipital headache, vomiting, ataxia, declining GCS; can deteriorate within hours — high index of suspicion.
  • Image: CT brain ± MRI for infarct.
  • Management:
    • External ventricular drain for hydrocephalus (life-saving).
    • Suboccipital decompressive craniectomy for cerebellar haemorrhage > 3 cm or with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus (Surgical guidelines per AHA/ASA ICH 2022).
    • For large cerebellar infarct with mass effect, decompressive surgery is recommended within 48 hours.
  • Avoid LP if posterior-fossa mass — risk of tonsillar herniation.

9. Subarachnoid haemorrhage (cross-ref Module 4)

  • Thunderclap headache + meningism. Non-contrast CT > 99% sensitive in first 6 hours; LP for xanthochromia after 12 hours if CT normal and suspicion remains.
  • CT angiography or DSA to identify aneurysm; secure with coil or clip within 72 hours.

Acute critical care for aSAH

  • Strict BP control pre-securing: SBP < 140–160 (typically < 140 if pre-secure); avoid hypotension.
  • Nimodipine 60 mg po q4h × 21 days — reduces poor outcomes from delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI). Magnesium has not shown benefit (MASH II).
  • Bed rest, stool softeners, analgesia, anti-emetic.
  • External ventricular drain for hydrocephalus (~30% develop acute hydrocephalus).
  • Seizure prophylaxis only short-term peri-procedurally (no evidence to support routine long-term).
  • Delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI) typically days 4–14 post-bleed:
    • Daily clinical assessment + transcranial Doppler / CTA / CTP for surveillance.
    • If new neurological deficit: induced hypertension (SBP target 160–200; use vasopressors to maintain), euvolaemia, optimise haematocrit. The "triple H" therapy (hypertension + hypervolaemia + haemodilution) is now reduced to induced hypertension + euvolaemia (hypervolaemia harmful).
    • Endovascular angioplasty / intra-arterial vasodilators in selected refractory cases.
  • Cardiac complications: Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, neurogenic pulmonary oedema, troponin elevation.
  • Hyponatraemia (cerebral salt wasting vs SIADH): fluid + salt replacement; CSW shows volume depletion (treat with isotonic / hypertonic saline + fludrocortisone); SIADH shows euvolaemia (fluid restriction).

10. Intracerebral haemorrhage

  • ICH score (Hemphill 2001) — GCS + age + ICH volume + IVH + infratentorial — for risk stratification.
  • BP control: INTERACT2 / ATACH-II / INTERACT-3 (2023) support early intensive lowering — target SBP < 140 mmHg within 1 hour of presentation in spontaneous ICH; INTERACT-3 also bundled glucose, temperature, anticoagulation reversal — favourable outcome benefit.
  • Reverse anticoagulation:
    • Warfarin → 4F-PCC 25–50 IU/kg + vitamin K 10 mg IV.
    • Dabigatran → idarucizumab 5 g IV.
    • Apixaban / rivaroxaban → andexanet alfa (FDA 2018 — controversial cost/efficacy; ANNEXA-I trial 2024 NEJM showed benefit) or 4F-PCC if andexanet unavailable.
    • LMWH → protamine 1 mg per 1 mg LMWH (partial reversal); FFP for fibrinogen issues.
  • Spot sign (active contrast extravasation on CTA) → high risk of haematoma expansion.
  • Indications for surgery:
    • Cerebellar haemorrhage > 3 cm or with brainstem compression / hydrocephalus.
    • Lobar haematoma with deterioration — controversial; ENRICH (2024 NEJM): minimally invasive endoscopic surgery improved functional outcome in selected lobar ICH.
    • Decompressive craniectomy for refractory mass effect.
  • Seizure prophylaxis not routinely recommended; treat clinical or EEG-confirmed seizures.
  • VTE prophylaxis: mechanical from admission; pharmacological after 24–48 h if bleeding stable.

11. Post-cardiac-arrest care and neuroprognostication

  • Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest with ROSC → multiorgan dysfunction + brain injury from global ischaemia-reperfusion.
  • Targeted temperature management (TTM):
    • TTM 1 trial (Nielsen NEJM 2013): 33 °C equivalent to 36 °C × 24 h.
    • TTM2 trial (Dankiewicz NEJM 2021): targeted normothermia (avoid fever ≤ 37.7 °C) was equivalent to 33 °C in OHCA. Many guidelines now recommend aggressive fever avoidance ≤ 37.5 °C for at least 72 h; controlled hypothermia 32–36 °C remains an option for selected patients.
  • Targeted oxygenation (SpO₂ 94–98%, avoid hyperoxia), CO₂ 35–40 mmHg, MAP > 65 (often 80–90), glucose 7.8–10 mmol/L, avoid hyperthermia and shivering, treat seizures and myoclonic status, continuous EEG.

Multimodal neuroprognostication (≥ 72 hours after rewarming and sedation off)

  • Clinical: bilateral absent pupillary + corneal reflexes; status myoclonus on day 1.
  • SSEPs: bilateral absent N20 cortical responses.
  • EEG: burst-suppression with identical bursts; suppressed background; generalised periodic discharges > 50% of recording; status epilepticus on flat background.
  • Biomarker: NSE > 60 µg/L at 48–72 h.
  • Imaging: diffuse cortical / basal ganglia / cerebellar cytotoxic oedema on MRI DWI; loss of grey-white differentiation on CT.
  • No single feature is sufficient — combine ≥ 2 robust features (ERC/ESICM 2021).
Time and combination: do not prognosticate too early. Sedation, hypothermia, and metabolic confounders interfere with the exam. Multimodal assessment ≥ 72 hours after rewarming and after sedation withdrawal protects from premature withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment. Lance-Adams (post-anoxic action myoclonus) is a recovery state, not a poor prognostic sign.

12. Brain death determination — WBDP/BDDD 2023 + AAN 2010

The 2023 World Brain Death Project (WBDP) consensus / Brain Death/Death by Neurologic Criteria (BDDD) updated the diagnostic framework. The AAN 2010 adult guideline remains widely used in the US and many countries.

Prerequisites (all must be met)

  1. Established irreversible catastrophic brain injury of known cause.
  2. Exclude confounders:
    • Hypothermia — core temperature ≥ 36 °C.
    • Hypotension — SBP ≥ 100 mmHg (off vasoactive drugs as feasible) or MAP ≥ 75.
    • Severe metabolic disturbance — Na, glucose, urea, ammonia, ABG.
    • CNS-depressant drugs and neuromuscular blockers cleared (allow 5 half-lives; consider drug levels; train-of-four monitoring).
    • No condition mimicking brain death (Guillain-Barré, locked-in, fulminant Wernicke, toxin).
  3. Observation period: typically 6–24 hours after the inciting event (longer for HIE).

Clinical examination — coma + absent brainstem reflexes + apnoea

  1. Unresponsive coma (no eye opening, motor response, or vocalisation to noxious stimuli — supraorbital, nailbed).
  2. Absent brainstem reflexes:
    • Pupillary light reflex (round, fixed, 4–9 mm).
    • Corneal reflex (cotton swab to lateral cornea).
    • Oculocephalic (doll's eyes) — only if cervical spine cleared.
    • Oculovestibular (50 mL ice-cold water in each ear with head 30°; observe 1 min after).
    • Gag reflex (suction or tongue depressor on posterior pharynx).
    • Cough reflex (deep ETT suction).
    • No facial movement to noxious stimulus.
  3. Apnoea test — if all prerequisites and brainstem reflexes confirm:
    1. Pre-oxygenate with 100% O₂ for 10 min; PaCO₂ should be ~35–45 mmHg.
    2. Disconnect ventilator; provide passive O₂ via T-piece or insufflation catheter at 6 L/min through ETT.
    3. Observe for 8–10 minutes for any spontaneous respiration.
    4. Draw ABG.
    5. Test positive (consistent with brain death) if: PaCO₂ rises to ≥ 60 mmHg (or rises ≥ 20 mmHg above baseline) AND no respiratory effort observed.
    6. Abort if hypotension, severe hypoxia, or arrhythmia develops; consider ancillary testing.

Ancillary testing — when apnoea test cannot be done or examination confounded

  • Cerebral angiography (4-vessel) — no intracranial flow at the level of the carotid bifurcation / circle of Willis.
  • EEG — electrocerebral silence ≥ 30 min.
  • Transcranial Doppler — oscillating / reverberating flow patterns indicating no net forward flow.
  • Nuclear medicine (Tc-99m HMPAO) — no intracranial perfusion ("hollow skull" sign).
Spinal reflexes can persist — triple flexion, Lazarus sign (brief arm movement on disconnection), deep tendon reflexes, sweating, and BP variability do not exclude brain death because they originate in the spinal cord.
Brain death = death by neurological criteria. Once declared and documented per institutional protocol, life-sustaining treatment is discontinued; if organ donation is considered, organ procurement organisation is notified before withdrawal of support. Family communication is paramount. WBDP 2023 emphasises consistency, transparency, and a single declaration is sufficient for adults (some jurisdictions require two independent examinations).

13. Multimodal neuromonitoring

  • ICP monitor: EVD (also therapeutic — CSF drainage) or intraparenchymal (Camino bolt).
  • Brain tissue oxygen (PbtO₂): target > 20 mmHg; BOOST-3 trial (NEJM 2024): PbtO₂-guided care in severe TBI showed improved functional outcomes vs ICP-only.
  • Continuous EEG: detect NCSE (10–30% of comatose ICU patients); guide titration of anaesthetic infusions in super-refractory status.
  • Cerebral microdialysis: lactate / pyruvate ratio, glucose, glutamate — research / specialist centres.
  • Transcranial Doppler: vasospasm screening after SAH.
  • NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy): regional cerebral oxygen saturation — increasing use; particularly in cardiac surgery and selected neuro-ICU.
  • Pupillometer: quantitative pupillary reactivity (NPi) — early warning of neurological decline / herniation.

14. Neuromuscular crises in the ICU

  • GBS with respiratory failure (Module 9): FVC < 20 mL/kg or NIF less negative than −30 cm H₂O, or 20/30/40 rule (FVC < 20, NIF < 30, MEP < 40) — intubate. Avoid succinylcholine (hyperkalaemia from upregulated AChRs). IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days or plasma exchange 5 sessions. Autonomic dysfunction is a major mortality driver — telemetry, avoid pushed cardioactive drug boluses (Module 15).
  • Myasthenic crisis (Module 9): bulbar / respiratory failure. Avoid known crisis-precipitating drugs (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, β-blockers, magnesium — Module 20). Plasma exchange or IVIG; pyridostigmine reduced or held in crisis to reduce secretions; steroids carefully (may worsen initially). Watch for cholinergic crisis from pyridostigmine overuse.
  • Transverse myelitis / spinal cord emergencies (Module 10): high-cervical lesions can cause respiratory failure; monitor and intubate as needed.
  • Botulism, tetanus, ALS, periodic paralysis — Module 9.

15. Sedation, analgesia, and ICU-acquired weakness

  • Use lighter sedation where possible (daily sedation holds; analgesia-first approach with fentanyl / morphine for pain). For neuro-ICU patients on ICP monitoring or with status epilepticus, deeper sedation is appropriate.
  • Propofol: rapid on-off; titratable; watch for propofol-related infusion syndrome (PRIS) at > 4 mg/kg/h or > 48 h (metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac failure).
  • Midazolam: useful in status; benzo-tolerance.
  • Dexmedetomidine: α2 agonist, useful for delirium and ventilator weaning; no respiratory depression but watch bradycardia and hypotension.
  • Ketamine: emerging role in super-refractory status; raises BP (useful in hypotensive patients); old concerns about ICP largely refuted.
  • Neuromuscular blockade (cisatracurium): reserve for refractory ICP, severe ARDS, shivering during TTM, ventilator dyssynchrony.
  • ICU-acquired weakness: critical illness polyneuropathy + myopathy → flaccid quadriparesis after sepsis / multiorgan failure; reduces ventilator weaning; early mobilisation + nutrition + glycaemic control mitigate. Cross-ref Module 9.

16. When to refer / escalate

  • Same minute: suspected impending herniation (Cushing triad, deteriorating GCS, unilateral pupillary dilation) — emergency airway, hyperosmolar bolus, head up, neurosurgery on speed dial.
  • Same hour: status epilepticus → 1st-line benzodiazepine + 2nd-line ASM + ICU if persisting; suspected SAH → CT + neurosurgery / interventional neuroradiology for aneurysm securing.
  • Same shift: malignant MCA infarct candidate < 60 → decompressive hemicraniectomy discussion within 48 h; cerebellar mass > 3 cm or with hydrocephalus → suboccipital decompression + EVD.
  • Always involve: intensive care + neurology + neurosurgery + interventional neuroradiology + neurorehabilitation + clinical pharmacy + palliative care for futility discussions + organ procurement organisation if brain death suspected.
