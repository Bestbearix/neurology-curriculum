From "memory problems" to a staged diagnosis

The general-physician Module 7 teaches recognition and treatable causes. This version assumes you stage cognitive disorders, deploy the revised Alzheimer criteria and blood biomarkers, counsel on anti-amyloid therapy and its ARIA risk, and run the rapidly-progressive-dementia workup.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Separate MCI from dementia and profile the domain pattern and tempo.
  2. Exclude reversible causes before diagnosing a neurodegenerative disease.
  3. Apply the revised biological/clinical AD criteria and use plasma p-tau217 appropriately.
  4. Counsel on lecanemab/donanemab benefit and ARIA monitoring; know EVOKE was negative.
  5. Differentiate DLB, FTD, and vascular dementia; run the rapidly-progressive-dementia / CJD / autoimmune workup.
Educational use only. Confirm diagnosis, biomarker use, and drug eligibility against current guidelines and product labeling at the point of care.

The cognitive workup

Dementia = acquired decline in ≥1 domain impairing independence; MCI = decline without functional impairment. Profile domain pattern + tempo, then reach a syndromic and etiologic diagnosis.

  • Collateral history; cognitive testing (MoCA/MMSE + domain tasks); focused neuro exam.
  • Labs: TSH, B12/folate, metabolic (± HIV/syphilis); structural MRI.
  • Add CSF/blood biomarkers, FDG-PET, amyloid PET, DaTscan, EEG, genetics by syndrome.
Exclude reversible causes first: depression (pseudodementia), anticholinergic/sedative drugs, B12 deficiency, thyroid disease, normal-pressure hydrocephalus (wet-wobbly-wacky), OSA, chronic subdural, neurosyphilis/HIV, autoimmune encephalitis, alcohol.
Tempo triages: years of amnestic decline → AD; stepwise/vascular → vascular; weeks–months → prion/autoimmune/neoplastic/infectious — urgent, treatable-until-proven-otherwise.

Leading syndrome

Alzheimer disease

Pathology: amyloid-β plaques + tau tangles with neuronal loss; usually amnestic (atypical: posterior cortical atrophy, logopenic aphasia, dysexecutive). Risk: age, APOE ε4; rare autosomal-dominant APP/PSEN1/PSEN2.

Revised biological criteria. AD is defined by biomarkers and staged across preclinical → MCI → dementia. Core 1 = amyloid-β pathway (amyloid PET, CSF, blood phospho-tau); tau/neurodegeneration markers add staging (ATN logic).
Biology ≠ symptoms. A positive amyloid biomarker in a cognitively normal person = AD pathology, not dementia — don't label asymptomatic people on biomarkers alone.
Plasma p-tau217. Detects AD pathology with AUC ~0.9 (PPV ~89–95% specialty, ~82%/accuracy ~85% primary care); a blood test is now cleared for primary care — use it to answer a management question, not to screen the worried well.

Therapy

Symptomatic: cholinesterase inhibitors (mild–moderate); memantine (moderate–severe).

Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab). Remove plaques and modestly slow decline in early AD (lecanemab ~27% CDR-SB slowing over 18 mo). Require confirmed amyloid positivity and MRI surveillance.
ARIA: ARIA-E (edema) and ARIA-H (microhemorrhage), usually asymptomatic but potentially serious; highest risk in APOE ε4 homozygotes; needs serial MRI and anticoagulation caution. FDA approved; EMA more restrictive (donanemab refusal).
EVOKE / EVOKE+ (negative). Oral semaglutide (GLP-1) was not efficacious at slowing early-AD progression (CDR-SB) — epidemiologic signal ≠ trial benefit.

DLB neuroleptic sensitivity can be severe — avoid typical antipsychotics; if needed use cautious low-dose quetiapine or pimavanserin.

Rapidly progressive dementia

Decline over weeks–months = emergency; work the "VITAMINS" differential (Vascular, Infectious, Toxic-metabolic, Autoimmune, Metastatic/neoplastic, Iatrogenic, Neurodegenerative-prion, Systemic).

Creutzfeldt-Jakob (prion)

RPD with myoclonus, ataxia, mood/behavioral change (may precede cognition). Diagnostics: MRI DWI cortical ribboning + basal-ganglia signal, RT-QuIC/14-3-3 in CSF, EEG periodic sharp waves. Uniformly fatal, short survival.

Autoimmune / limbic encephalitis

Graus/Dalmau criteria. Subacute limbic encephalitis can be classed possible / probable (incl. antibody-negative) / definite, enabling empiric immunotherapy before antibodies return. Test NMDA-R, LGI1, CASPR2, GABA-B; search for tumor (NMDA-R/teratoma). Careful antibody-negative criteria avoid misdiagnosis.
Never miss a treatable dementia: send autoimmune/paraneoplastic panels and consider immunotherapy in any subacute/rapidly progressive dementia.

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A 70-year-old with 2 years of progressive short-term memory loss, MoCA 21, normal labs, and hippocampal-predominant atrophy on MRI. The family asks about anti-amyloid therapy.
How do you confirm and counsel?

Amnestic MCI/early AD. Confirm amyloid positivity (plasma p-tau217, or CSF/amyloid PET) before offering lecanemab or donanemab. Counsel on modest slowing of decline, the need for serial MRI, and ARIA risk (higher if APOE ε4 homozygote). Continue symptomatic therapy and risk-factor management.

Case 2. A 68-year-old has fluctuating attention, recurrent detailed visual hallucinations, mild parkinsonism, and dream-enactment behavior. A prior antipsychotic caused marked rigidity.
Diagnosis + management caution?

Dementia with Lewy bodies (≥2 core features). Supportive: reduced striatal DAT uptake (DaTscan), PSG REM-without-atonia. Avoid typical antipsychotics (severe neuroleptic sensitivity); use cautious low-dose quetiapine or pimavanserin; a cholinesterase inhibitor often helps cognition/hallucinations.

Case 3. A 62-year-old declines cognitively over 3 months with new myoclonus and ataxia; MRI DWI shows cortical ribboning and basal-ganglia hyperintensity.
Diagnosis + confirmatory tests — and what must you exclude?

Rapidly progressive dementia, likely Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease. Confirm with CSF RT-QuIC (and 14-3-3), EEG periodic sharp waves. Crucially, exclude autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (Graus criteria; NMDA-R/LGI1 antibodies, tumor search) and other VITAMINS causes — because those are treatable.
