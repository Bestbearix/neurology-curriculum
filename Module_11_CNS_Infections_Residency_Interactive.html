The infectious-neuro emergency mindset

The general-physician Module 11 teaches recognition and referral. This enriched version assumes you interpret the CSF, start empiric therapy before results, and run the differential across bacterial, TB, viral, fungal, parasitic, and opportunistic disease.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Read a CSF profile and name the most likely category.
  2. Give empiric bacterial-meningitis therapy (vancomycin + ceftriaxone ± ampicillin + dexamethasone) without waiting for the LP.
  3. Sequence CT-before-LP correctly while never delaying antibiotics.
  4. Start empiric aciclovir for suspected encephalitis; know DexEnceph is investigational and post-HSV NMDA-R encephalitis.
  5. Apply the TB-meningitis, brain-abscess, fungal, parasitic and PML/HIV evidence.

Bacterial meningitis

Key points
  • Adults: pneumococcus + meningococcus; add Listeria cover if >50, pregnant, or immunocompromised.
  • Empiric: vancomycin + ceftriaxone (± ampicillin) + dexamethasone with/just before first dose.
  • Dexamethasone helps chiefly pneumococcal disease; stop if Listeria.
  • Sequence: cultures → dexamethasone + antibiotics → CT (if indicated) → LP. Never delay drugs for imaging.

Adjunctive dexamethasone. Given before/with the first antibiotic dose it reduces mortality and hearing loss in pneumococcal meningitis; contemporary cohorts (Danish nationwide 2015–2023) show falling mortality with protocolized empiric therapy. Stop if Listeria.
CT before LP for focal deficit, new seizure, papilledema, ↓consciousness, or immunocompromise — but antibiotics first. A normal CT doesn't guarantee LP is safe in fulminant disease.

Tuberculous meningitis

Key points
  • Subacute basal meningitis: cranial neuropathies, hydrocephalus, basal enhancement ± infarcts/tuberculomas. CSF lymphocytic, high protein, low glucose.
  • Diagnose with Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra + AFB smear/culture (large-volume CSF); treat empirically when suspected.
  • 4-drug anti-TB + adjunctive corticosteroids; watch for hydrocephalus and paradoxical reactions/IRIS.
Genotype-stratified dexamethasone (phase-3 RCT). In LTA4H CC/CT-genotype HIV-negative adults, dexamethasone did not show the expected survival benefit — challenging uniform steroid benefit. (Dexamethasone retains an established mortality benefit in HIV-positive TBM from prior trials.)
High-dose rifampin (RCT). Standard rifampin penetrates the CNS poorly; intensified dosing is under study — no clearly superior regimen yet ("new approaches needed").

Viral encephalitis — HSV

Key points
  • HSV-1 encephalitis = the treatable can't-miss: fever, altered mentation, temporal/limbic signs, seizures.
  • Start IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg q8h empirically; confirm with CSF HSV PCR (repeat if early/negative) + MRI.
  • Adjunctive steroids (DexEnceph) are investigational; aciclovir is the established therapy. Delay worsens outcome.
DexEnceph (UK RCT). Tested IV dexamethasone + aciclovir vs aciclovir alone in PCR-confirmed HSV encephalitis; the role of adjunctive steroids remains investigational.
Post-HSV anti-NMDA-R encephalitis: new movement disorder/psychiatric decline weeks after HSE (negative repeat HSV PCR) → test NMDA-R antibodies + immunotherapy, not more aciclovir.

Weigh other viruses by epidemiology: VZV vasculopathy, enteroviruses, arboviruses (JE, West Nile), CMV, HIV. Enterovirus D68 → acute flaccid myelitis (see HIV/PML tab).

Brain abscess

Key points
  • Headache + focal deficit ± fever/seizures; ring-enhancing lesion with restricted diffusion on DWI (vs most tumours).
  • Source: contiguous (oto/sinus/dental), haematogenous (endocarditis, R→L shunt), post-neurosurgical.
  • Usually surgical aspiration/excision + prolonged targeted antibiotics; steroids only for major mass effect.
Surgery vs medical (emulated-trial cohort). Nonoperative management was associated with higher mortality and rupture risk, and ~⅓ of nonoperative patients eventually needed surgery — favouring neurosurgical drainage in most abscesses.
Corticosteroids aren't benign in brain abscess — a target-trial emulation links steroid use to higher in-hospital mortality; reserve for life-threatening edema.

Fungal CNS infection

Key points
  • Cryptococcal meningitis — very high opening pressure; cryptococcal antigen; amphotericin B + flucytosine induction, then fluconazole; manage ICP with serial LPs.
  • Mucormycosis (diabetes/immunocompromise, rhino-orbital-cerebral) & Aspergillus — angioinvasive; surgery + antifungals (isavuconazole/voriconazole; liposomal amphotericin for mucor).
  • Regional: histoplasmosis, talaromycosis (SE Asia; anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies), candida, nocardia.
In cryptococcus, ICP kills — repeated therapeutic LPs (or drainage) matter as much as the antifungal. Provided qPCR and risk-prediction tools refine diagnosis/prognosis in sub-Saharan Africa.
Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis in a neutropenic/DKA patient (facial pain, black eschar, cranial neuropathies) is a surgical emergency.

Parasitic CNS infection

Key points
  • Neurocysticercosis — leading cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic areas; albendazole ± praziquantel + steroids + antiseizure meds.
  • Cerebral toxoplasmosis — ring-enhancing lesions in advanced HIV; empiric pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine (response = diagnosis).
  • Cerebral malaria — coma + falciparum parasitaemia → IV artesunate; long-term cognitive sequelae.

Neurocysticercosis therapy is staged by cyst location/viability/burden; toxoplasmosis is distinguished from primary CNS lymphoma by therapy response, serology, and thallium-SPECT/EBV-PCR; cerebral malaria has characteristic imaging/EEG features (provided studies) and is a medical emergency.

HIV, PML & opportunistic disease

Key points
  • In advanced HIV use CD4 + imaging pattern: toxoplasmosis, primary CNS lymphoma, cryptococcus, TB, PML, CMV.
  • PML (JC virus) — subacute multifocal white-matter disease in immunosuppression (HIV, natalizumab); no specific antiviral → restore immunity, anticipate IRIS.
  • HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder persists in the ART era.
PML / JCV. ART cut HIV-related PML, but iatrogenic PML surged with natalizumab and other immunomodulators. No specific antiviral — immune restoration, tempered by IRIS risk.

Acute flaccid myelitis (AFM)

A polio-like anterior-horn syndrome of children linked to enterovirus D68 (provided causality review; MFSD6 identified as the EV-D68 entry receptor). Acute flaccid weakness ± cranial-nerve signs after a viral prodrome; MRI shows longitudinal anterior-horn (grey-matter) cord signal. Supportive care (cross-links to Module 10).

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A 62-year-old with fever, headache, neck stiffness and confusion. Focal deficit present.
Order of actions?

Suspected bacterial meningitis with a focal deficit → blood cultures, then dexamethasone + vancomycin + ceftriaxone + ampicillin (age >50 → Listeria) immediately, then CT, then LP. Do not delay antibiotics for imaging. Add aciclovir if encephalitis is also possible.

Case 2. A 40-year-old with 4 days of fever, personality change and a seizure; MRI shows left temporal T2/FLAIR signal.
Diagnosis and treatment?

HSV-1 encephalitis. Start IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg q8h empirically; send CSF HSV PCR (repeat in 3–7 days if early/negative but suspicious). Adjunctive steroids remain investigational (DexEnceph). Watch for later post-HSV NMDA-R encephalitis.

Case 3. A patient with advanced HIV (CD4 30) has headache and a very high LP opening pressure; cryptococcal antigen positive.
Management priority?

Cryptococcal meningitis. Induction amphotericin B + flucytosine then fluconazole — but the outcome-defining step is aggressive ICP control with repeated therapeutic LPs (or drainage). Time ART start to reduce IRIS risk.

Case 4. An MS patient on natalizumab develops subacute cognitive decline and hemianopia; MRI shows an expanding, non-enhancing subcortical white-matter lesion crossing to grey matter.
Diagnosis and management?

Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (JC virus). Confirm with CSF JCV PCR. There is no specific antiviral — stop natalizumab and restore immunity (e.g., plasma exchange to clear drug), and anticipate IRIS, which can transiently worsen deficits.
