Why this module?

Altered mental status accounts for up to 4–10% of emergency-department presentations, and coma is the presentation in which the gap between what the bedside can tell you and what the hospital can measure is widest. Most of the reversible causes are found with a glucose meter, a blood gas, a drug chart and a careful examination — not with a scan. This module is built so that a physician working without same-day MRI, continuous EEG or an on-call neurologist can still do the things that change outcome.

What you should be able to do after this module

  • Separate arousal from awareness, and use descriptive language instead of loose labels.
  • Run the first ten minutes: ABC, glucose, thiamine before glucose, naloxone, antimicrobials, cervical spine.
  • Ask the one question that finds locked-in syndrome.
  • Examine the four pillars and sort the patient into supratentorial, infratentorial or diffuse.
  • Recognise herniation and act before the scan is reported.
  • Decide which investigation is worth a transfer today, which can wait, and what to treat empirically meanwhile.
  • Diagnose and prevent delirium with the interventions that actually have evidence.
  • Manage sepsis-associated and hepatic encephalopathy, and recognise non-convulsive status epilepticus.
  • Deliver post-cardiac-arrest care — and understand why prognostication must be multimodal and delayed.
  • Apply the 2023 brain-death guideline correctly, including its new prerequisites.
The principle that anchors everything: localise first, then reverse what is reversible, then re-examine. The examination sorts the patient into one of three buckets and tells you whether the next call is to the neurosurgeon or the laboratory. The reversible causes are identified in the first hour and mostly without imaging. And deterioration is detected by comparing two documented examinations — not by impression.

Lessons

1 · Arousal, awareness, and the states in between

Arousal depends on the ascending reticular activating system — brainstem tegmentum, locus coeruleus, raphe, pedunculopontine nuclei — projecting through the thalamic intralaminar nuclei to cortex. Awareness depends on recurrent thalamocortical activity, with at least fleeting working memory required for a conscious percept. Every state in this module is a combination of the two.

Always test for locked-in syndrome before concluding a patient is unresponsive. Say: "Look up at the ceiling" and "Blink twice if you can hear me." Vertical eye movement and blinking may be the only voluntary responses available. Patients have been managed as comatose for days while fully aware.
2 · The first ten minutes
  • Airway, breathing, circulation. Intubate for GCS ≤ 8 or airway at risk. Hypoxia and hypotension worsen every cause on the list.
  • Capillary glucose — and thiamine before glucose in anyone at nutritional risk.
  • Naloxone 0.4 mg IV titrated if opioids are plausible; expect resedation.
  • Flumazenil is not part of the modern coma cocktail — seizure risk in mixed overdose and chronic use.
  • Antibiotics + dexamethasone + aciclovir if meningoencephalitis is plausible — before the CT and before the LP if there is any delay.
  • Cervical spine precautions in any unwitnessed collapse; no doll's-eye testing until cleared.
  • Vital signs tell a story: Cushing response = raised ICP; fever = infection, heatstroke or toxidrome; hypothermia = exposure, sepsis, hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia — and it can mimic brain death.
The history comes from someone else, and it beats the scan. Telephone the family, the nursing home or the ambulance crew before they disperse. The detail that explains the coma — a missed dialysis, a new tramadol prescription, a charcoal stove in a closed room — is obtainable in one call now and unobtainable in an hour.
3 · The four-pillar examination

Pillar 1 — Motor. Follows commands → localises → withdraws → decorticate (lesion above the red nucleus) → decerebrate (midbrain/upper pons) → flaccid. Asymmetry means structural until proven otherwise. Absent response may also be locked-in syndrome, GBS, neuromuscular blockade or cord injury.

Pillar 2 — Respiration. Cheyne–Stokes (bilateral hemispheric or metabolic) · hyperventilation (acidosis, sepsis, hepatic) · apneustic (pons) · cluster (low pons/high medulla) · ataxic (medulla — pre-terminal).

Pillar 3 — Pupils. The single most useful sign, because pupillary pathways run near but distinct from those for consciousness. Metabolic coma spares reactivity — except anticholinergics, severe anoxia, profound barbiturate intoxication and hypothermia. Pinpoint reactive = pons or opioid; mid-position fixed = midbrain; unilateral fixed dilated = uncal herniation; bilateral fixed dilated = brainstem catastrophe or a reversible mimic.

Pillar 4 — Eye movements. Roving conjugate movements mean an intact brainstem. Conjugate deviation towards a destructive cortical lesion, away from a pontine one. Ocular bobbing = pontine destruction. Doll's eyes and cold calorics test the brainstem — 50 mL ice water, head at 30°.

COWS describes the awake patient. In coma there is no cortical fast phase, so you see only tonic deviation towards the cold ear. Not seeing nystagmus in a comatose patient is not an abnormal result.
4 · Localisation and herniation

Supratentorial: coma only after a contralateral deficit, then orderly rostro-caudal deterioration. Infratentorial: sudden coma with brainstem signs from the outset, no orderly sequence. Diffuse/metabolic: reactive pupils, symmetric findings, asterixis and multifocal myoclonus.

Act before the scan is reported: head up 30° and midline · normoxia and normocapnia (brief hyperventilation only as a bridge) · hypertonic saline or mannitol · reverse anticoagulation · treat seizures and fever · telephone the neurosurgical centre now. Cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct with declining consciousness needs decompression, and outcome tracks how fast it happens.
5 · Investigating when the tests are not on site new
In coma the bedside localises and the tests confirm — but the treatable causes are mostly found by cheap immediate tests and by the history. The scan's main job is to find the lesion that needs a neurosurgeon or that contraindicates an LP.
The yield data should change your practice. ACR 2024: in a meta-analysis of 25 studies and 79 201 patients with atraumatic AMS, 94% had a head CT but relevant abnormalities in only 11%. Across 708 145 ED encounters, CT for AMS gave a 9.8% critical-result yield. In febrile elderly patients, abnormal imaging was 16.5% — predicted by lower GCS, a lateralising sign, higher systolic BP and lower temperature.

Know the limits of your CT. A normal non-contrast CT does not exclude early ischaemic stroke or basilar occlusion, encephalitis, non-convulsive status epilepticus, meningitis, early hypoxic injury, PRES, CVST, diffuse axonal injury, or any metabolic or toxic cause. If the CT is normal and the patient is still comatose, the useful next tests are EEG, lumbar puncture and MRI.

EEG is the most under-used test in unexplained coma. Non-convulsive status epilepticus is the classic missed diagnosis — suspect it with unexplained coma, subtle facial or limb twitching, nystagmoid eye movements, or failure to wake after a seizure or after sepsis is treated. Where EEG is unavailable, a monitored benzodiazepine trial with observation for clinical improvement is a legitimate diagnostic manoeuvre.

Never let a pending antibody result delay immunotherapy in a deteriorating patient. Autoimmune encephalitis is diagnosed on clinical criteria supported by MRI, EEG and CSF, and antibody-negative disease is real. Take paired serum and CSF, freeze a pre-treatment aliquot, cover infection, and start steroids, IVIg or plasma exchange on clinical grounds.

6 · Delirium — and the interventions that actually work

CAM: features 1 and 2, plus either 3 or 4 — (1) acute onset and fluctuating course · (2) inattention (months backwards, days backwards, serial sevens) · (3) disorganised thinking · (4) altered level of consciousness. Hypoactive delirium is commoner and more often missed. A RASS of −4 or −5 means too sedated to assess — lighten sedation and reassess.

I WATCH DEATH: Infection · Withdrawal · Acute metabolic · Trauma · CNS pathology · Hypoxia · Deficiencies · Endocrinopathies · Acute vascular · Toxins · Heavy metals. In practice: infection, drugs, metabolic derangement and pain account for most.

A meta-review of 32 systematic reviews covering 335 studies of 16 non-pharmacological interventions found that the ABCDEF bundle, multicomponent packages and early mobilisation significantly reduced delirium — and that strategies including early mobilisation and family participation were most effective.

ABCDEF: Assess and manage pain · Both awakening and breathing trials · Choice of sedation (avoid benzodiazepines) · Delirium monitoring · Early mobility · Family engagement.

Drugs only for severe agitation that threatens safety. They shorten neither the delirium nor the admission. Avoid antipsychotics in parkinsonism and Lewy body dementia; benzodiazepines are first-line only for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal.

Four things not to miss behind a "delirium" label: non-convulsive status epilepticus · Wernicke encephalopathy · HSV encephalitis · a toxidrome (serotonin syndrome, NMS, anticholinergic).
7 · Sepsis-associated encephalopathy new

The leading cause of encephalopathy in critically ill patients — diffuse brain dysfunction from systemic infection without direct CNS infection, ranging from inattention to deep coma. Encephalopathy in sepsis independently predicts mortality, and survivors carry long-term cognitive impairment.

Mechanism: blood–brain barrier breakdown permitting peripheral-to-central immune signalling (the pivotal step), neuroinflammation with glial activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, neurotransmitter imbalance, and impaired autoregulation. Distinct subphenotypes are emerging — ischaemic-hypoxic, inflammatory-dominant and metabolic-degenerative.

There is no diagnostic test and no evidence-based standard. SAE is a diagnosis of exclusion — never apply the label before excluding CNS infection, non-convulsive seizures, drug effect, metabolic derangement and stroke, all of which occur in septic patients and all of which are treatable.

Seizures matter here. Non-convulsive seizures are prevalent in SAE and frequently misdiagnosed, because the only sign is a conscious level that does not improve. Continuous EEG is ideal but not widespread — a routine EEG in the septic patient who is not waking up is the realistic and worthwhile test.

Management is treatment of the sepsis plus prevention of secondary brain injury: source control, cerebral perfusion, avoidance of hypoxia, hypercapnia and glycaemic extremes, avoidance of neurotoxic and sedating drugs, the delirium bundle, and early seizure recognition.

8 · Hepatic encephalopathy new

West Haven grades: I — trivial unawareness, shortened attention, altered sleep rhythm · II — lethargy, disorientation for time, personality change, asterixis · III — somnolence to semi-stupor, gross disorientation · IV — coma. Minimal HE is detectable only on psychometric testing (relevant to driving); covert HE unifies minimal HE and grade I.

Ammonia is a poor diagnostic test. A normal level does not exclude HE and a high one does not confirm it. It needs a free-flowing sample, on ice, analysed immediately. Clinical examination and judgement establish overt HE — and HE remains a diagnosis of exclusion in a cirrhotic patient who may equally have an intracranial bleed, SBP, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal, Wernicke encephalopathy or non-convulsive seizures.

Find the precipitant — that is the treatment: GI bleeding · infection including SBP · constipation · dehydration and over-diuresis · hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia · renal impairment · sedatives and opioids · portosystemic shunt · alcohol · non-adherence to lactulose.

Treatment: correct the precipitant · lactulose titrated to 2–3 soft bowel movements daily · rifaximin as add-on · airway protection in grade III–IV · do not restrict protein · continue secondary prophylaxis after discharge · primary prophylaxis after GI bleeding · and consider transplantation, because recurrent HE marks decompensation.

9 · Thiamine — beyond alcohol new
Thiamine deficiency is linked to medical, surgical, oncological, obstetric and critically ill populations — not just alcohol. Altered mental status may be the earliest manifestation, while MRI and serum thiamine levels frequently lack sensitivity early. Delayed recognition causes persistent deficits, despite the safety, low cost and availability of parenteral thiamine.

Risk beyond alcohol: bariatric surgery (often years later) · gastrectomy · hyperemesis gravidarum and persistent vomiting · malignancy and chemotherapy · dialysis · diuretics · refeeding · sepsis · prolonged IV fluids or parenteral nutrition without vitamins · anorexia nervosa and restrictive diets · critical illness generally.

The classic triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia is present in a minority. Suspect Wernicke encephalopathy on any of confusion, an ocular abnormality, gait ataxia, or unexplained hypotension and hypothermia, in a patient with any risk factor.

Do not use tests to decide. Serum thiamine is slow, often unavailable and unreliable after treatment; MRI is insensitive early. Give thiamine before glucose, parenterally, at a high dose, and replace magnesium too — hypomagnesaemia causes apparent thiamine resistance.
10 · After cardiac arrest new

Most deaths after cardiac arrest follow withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy based on a judgement that recovery is impossible — which is why the accuracy of prognostication is a life-and-death matter in a direct sense.

TrialResult
HACA (2002)32–34 °C after VF arrest: favourable neurological outcome 55% vs 39%; 6-month mortality 41% vs 55%
TTM (2013, n=950)33 °C vs 36 °C: no difference in mortality (50% vs 48%) or in death/poor outcome at 180 days (54% vs 52%)

What this means where cooling equipment is unavailable: the achievable, evidence-supported intervention is controlled normothermia with aggressive fever prevention — antipyretics, exposure, fans, cooled fluids. Plus normoxia, normocapnia, adequate perfusion pressure, normoglycaemia and seizure control.

Do not prognosticate in the first 72 hours, and not until sedation, paralysis, hypothermia and metabolic derangement are excluded. No single test is sufficient — combine at least two concordant indicators. And distinguish status myoclonus within 48 h (unfavourable) from Lance-Adams syndrome — action myoclonus in a patient who regains consciousness, compatible with good recovery.

Delayed post-hypoxic leukoencephalopathy: in 73 pooled cases, deterioration appeared days to weeks after benzodiazepine, opioid or polysubstance overdose or carbon monoxide poisoning — decreased consciousness, agitation, cognitive decline, parkinsonism — with diffuse white-matter T2 hyperintensity. Most recovered substantially over months. A patient deteriorating a week after an overdose has not necessarily relapsed or had a stroke.

11 · Chronic disorders of consciousness

The CRS-R is the gold standard and — importantly — remains more sensitive for detecting consciousness in routine practice than fMRI or EEG. Assess repeatedly: arousal fluctuates and a single assessment misclassifies. Optimise conditions first — remove sedating drugs, treat pain, infection, hydrocephalus, spasticity and seizures, correct sensory deficits, sit the patient up.

Research-grade markers (useful to know, not yet routine): heartbeat-evoked EEG responses separated UWS from MCS with 87% accuracy without any patient cooperation; frontoparietal effective connectivity discriminates UWS from MCS+; EEG meta-state dynamics are faster and less stable in UWS; FDG-PET helps differentiate states and predict outcome. The AAN systematic review is a useful corrective: no diagnostic assessment procedure had moderate or strong evidence for use.

Treatment: amantadine for traumatic disorders of consciousness — cheap, available, guideline-supported. Vagal nerve stimulation: individual-patient meta-analysis of 112 patients found a mean CRS-R gain of 2.78 points, with 40.2% exceeding the MCID; early evidence, moderate risk of bias. Otherwise: prevent complications, nourish, rehabilitate, and communicate structuredly with the family.

The ethical weight. With up to 40% of behaviourally unresponsive patients showing covert awareness, the obligations are concrete: assess repeatedly with a structured tool, optimise conditions first, involve more than one assessor, document the evidence rather than the label, and be explicit with families about uncertainty and about a recovery time course that can extend over months to years.
12 · Brain death — the 2023 guideline
The 2023 AAN/AAP/CNS/SCCM guideline replaces the 2010 adult and 2011 paediatric guidelines with a single document and 85 recommendations, deliberately conservative — emphasising when determination should be delayed or deferred, to minimise any risk of a false positive. Studies of hospital policies found wide variability and incomplete concordance with national guidance, improving but still incomplete.

New or changed prerequisites: a catastrophic permanent injury with an identified mechanism known to cause brain death · a defined observation period (≥24 h after hypoxic-ischaemic injury in patients ≥24 months; ≥48 h under 24 months) · neuroimaging consistent with the mechanism and severity · SBP ≥100 AND MAP ≥75 in adults (previously either) · normothermia · no drug effect (pentobarbital <5 µg/mL) · no neuromuscular blockade · no severe metabolic derangement. Pregnancy is not a contraindication, and preserved neuroendocrine function does not preclude evaluation.

Examination: coma with no motor response, and absent pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, oculovestibular, facial, gag and cough reflexes. Minimum one examination in adults, two in children.

Apnoea test: PaCO₂ 35–45 and pH 7.35–7.45 at baseline, preoxygenate ≥10 min to PaO₂ >200, then apnoeic oxygenation. Positive if PaCO₂ ≥60 mmHg or ≥20 above baseline with no respiratory effort. Abort for instability and use an ancillary test.

Spinal reflexes do not invalidate brain death — triple flexion, the Lazarus sign, and false ventilator triggering all occur. Know this before you are at the bedside with the family watching, and explain it to them in advance.

Ancillary tests are for when the clinical examination or apnoea test cannot be completed — not to shorten the process. Check your own hospital's written policy against the current guideline: the published variability is precisely in institutional policies.

Investigation Planner new

For hospitals without same-day MRI, continuous EEG, or a neuroimmunology laboratory. The question is never just which test — it is whether the result changes what you do in the next hour, today or this week, and what you treat empirically meanwhile.

Five urgency tiers. A — Now: the first ten minutes, at the bedside. B — Today: the result determines an emergency intervention; if you cannot do it here, the patient travels today. C — This week: treatable disease is plausible; days are acceptable, weeks are not. D — This month: refines diagnosis or prognosis without changing acute management. E — Elective: must never delay treatment or a transfer decision.
The imaging-yield data (ACR 2024). In a meta-analysis of 25 studies and 79 201 patients with atraumatic altered mental status, 94% underwent head CT but relevant abnormal findings appeared in only 11%. Across 708 145 adult ED encounters, CT ordered for AMS had a critical-result yield of 9.8%. Among 285 febrile elderly patients with AMS, abnormal imaging was 16.5% — predicted by lower GCS, a lateralising sign, higher systolic blood pressure and lower body temperature. Commonest findings: intracranial haemorrhage and ischaemic stroke.

Making a transfer count. Telephone before you move anyone in tier B. Send your results on paper — bloods, gases, ECG, CT images, drug chart, with times. Document your examination with times — GCS components, pupils, brainstem reflexes, motor response, respiratory pattern, temperature — because in a patient who later becomes a prognostication or brain-death question, your early examination is irreplaceable data nobody can reconstruct. Record what you have already given, especially sedatives and paralytics, which are the commonest confounders in later neurological assessment. And stabilise before moving: airway, blood pressure, oxygenation, seizures, glucose, temperature.
