Epilepsy & Seizure Disorders

Residency deep-dive · PGY-2 core · classify, treat, run the status protocol, refer for surgery
Resident level ILAE 2017 ASM selection Status epilepticus Surgery

From "had a seizure" to a management plan

The general-physician Module 3 teaches recognition and when to refer. This version assumes you classify with the ILAE 2017 framework, choose the right antiseizure medication on trial evidence, run the status-epilepticus protocol from minute five, and identify who belongs at an epilepsy surgery center.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Classify by the ILAE 2017 three levels (seizure type → epilepsy type → syndrome) with etiology at each step.
  2. Separate provoked from unprovoked seizures and decide when to start treatment.
  3. Select first-line therapy by epilepsy type, citing SANAD and the monotherapy meta-analysis.
  4. Run the status-epilepticus protocol with correct drugs, doses, and timing (ESETT).
  5. Recognize drug-resistant epilepsy and refer for surgical evaluation without delay.
Treat status from 5 minutes. The ILAE 2015 definition sets t1 = 5 min (start treatment) and t2 = 30 min (long-term injury begins) for convulsive seizures. Don't wait.
Educational use only. Verify drugs, doses, and protocols against current guidelines and institutional policy at the point of care.

ILAE 2017 — three levels, etiology throughout

LevelCategories
1 · Seizure typeFocal onset · Generalized onset · Unknown onset (motor / non-motor; focal by awareness)
2 · Epilepsy typeFocal · Generalized · Combined generalized & focal · Unknown
3 · Epilepsy syndromeSpecific syndrome (JME, CAE, mesial TLE, Lennox-Gastaut, …)

The six etiologic groups — structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown — are considered at every level because each has therapeutic consequences. The term developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) captures impairment from both the underlying disease and the epileptic activity.

2017 terminology updates: "partial" → focal; "simple/complex partial" → focal aware / focal impaired awareness; "secondarily generalized" → focal to bilateral tonic–clonic.

Mimics: convulsive syncope, PNES (closed eyes, asynchronous movements, fluctuating course, preserved awareness during bilateral motor activity), migraine/TIA. Video-EEG is the arbiter.

Antiseizure-medication selection

SANAD I (Lancet 2007). Arm A (focal/unclassified): lamotrigine clinically better than carbamazepine for time to treatment failure → first-line for focal. Arm B (generalized/unclassified): valproate superior (treatment failure & 12-month remission) → first-line for generalized where appropriate.
Monotherapy network meta-analysis (IPD). Focal: lamotrigine best for treatment failure, levetiracetam comparable (HR LTG vs LEV ≈ 1.01); CBZ/OXC/VPA/PHT/TPM worse on failure-for-any-reason. Generalized: valproate most effective. NICE-type first-line: LTG/LEV for focal; valproate for generalized (teratogenicity caveat).
ScenarioPreferredNotes
Focal epilepsyLamotrigine or levetiracetamLTG titrate slowly (rash/SJS)
Generalized epilepsyValproate (if not childbearing potential)Else LEV/LTG
AbsenceEthosuximide or valproateEthosuximide for absence-only
Childbearing potentialLevetiracetam, lamotrigineAvoid valproate; folate
Pitfalls: carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin can worsen absence/myoclonic seizures. Valproate is teratogenic + neurodevelopmental risk. Lamotrigine needs slow titration. Enzyme inducers (CBZ/PHT) cause interactions (incl. contraceptives). Screen HLA-B*15:02 before carbamazepine in at-risk Asian populations.

Status epilepticus — stepwise protocol

ILAE 2015 time points: t1 = 5 min (start treatment) and t2 = 30 min (long-term injury) for convulsive status.

0–5 min · Stabilize
ABCs, oxygen, IV access, fingerstick glucose, thiamine if indicated, cardiac/EEG monitoring, time the seizure.
5–20 min · First-line (benzodiazepine, full dose)
IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg/dose) · or IM midazolam 10 mg · or IV diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg. Under-dosing is the commonest error.
20–40 min · Second-line (IV ASM)
Levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, OR valproate — all comparable per ESETT. Choose by patient factors (avoid VPA in pregnancy/hepatic disease; FOS cautions in cardiac disease).
>40 min · Refractory
Anesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital) with continuous EEG. Search for and treat the cause (incl. autoimmune/NORSE).
ESETT. Double-blind 3-arm RCT — levetiracetam vs fosphenytoin vs valproate as second-line: no significant difference; each stopped status in roughly half. Pick by availability and contraindications.
Refractory = fails first + second line; super-refractory = ≥24 h despite anesthesia. Ongoing seizures drive pharmacoresistance (receptor trafficking) — treat early and adequately.

Drug-resistant epilepsy & surgery

Drug-resistant epilepsy (ILAE): failure of two appropriately chosen, adequately dosed, tolerated ASM regimens to achieve sustained seizure freedom → refer to a comprehensive epilepsy center (video-EEG, MRI, neuropsychology, ± PET/SPECT, intracranial EEG).

Surgery evidence. Controlled data support anterior temporal lobectomy for drug-resistant mesial temporal lobe epilepsy as superior to continued medical therapy for seizure freedom (RCT assessments + AAN practice parameter in the resource set). SEEG-guided thermocoagulation vs ATL was tested in a pilot RCT; laser interstitial thermal therapy is an additional minimally invasive option. Palliative: VNS, responsive neurostimulation, DBS, callosotomy.
Refer early. MTLE with hippocampal sclerosis is surgically remediable but often left on years of escalating polytherapy — referral after two failed drugs improves seizure-freedom odds.

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A convulsing patient has been seizing for 8 minutes in the ED. IV access is in; glucose normal.
Next drug and dose?

This is status epilepticus (>5 min). Give a full-dose benzodiazepine: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (up to 4 mg/dose). If still seizing at ~20 min, move to a second-line IV ASM — levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate (all comparable, ESETT). Avoid the common error of under-dosing the benzodiazepine.

Case 2. A 19-year-old woman has early-morning jerks, two GTCs on waking after sleep deprivation, and a photoparoxysmal EEG response.
Diagnosis and drug choice?

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (generalized). Valproate is most effective but avoid in a person of childbearing potential — choose levetiracetam or lamotrigine, add folate, and avoid carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine (can worsen myoclonus/absence).

Case 3. A 34-year-old with focal impaired-awareness seizures and right hippocampal sclerosis on MRI continues to seize despite adequate trials of two well-chosen ASMs.
What now?

This is drug-resistant epilepsy (two failed appropriate ASMs) in a surgically remediable syndrome (MTLE with hippocampal sclerosis). Refer to a comprehensive epilepsy center for pre-surgical evaluation — anterior temporal lobectomy is superior to continued medical therapy for seizure freedom.
