Module 3 — Epilepsy & Seizure Disorders

Interactive residency module · classify → treat → escalate. Reference version: printable reference ↗

The through-line

▎Core loop
  • Classify precisely with ILAE 2017 (seizure type → epilepsy type → syndrome, etiology at every step).
  • Match the drug to the epilepsy type — lamotrigine/levetiracetam for focal, valproate for generalized (but not in childbearing potential).
  • Treat status from minute 5 — full-dose benzodiazepine first, then LEV/fosphenytoin/valproate.
Two definitions to have cold: Epilepsy = 2 unprovoked seizures >24 h apart, OR 1 unprovoked seizure with ≥60% 10-yr recurrence risk, OR an epilepsy syndrome. Drug-resistant epilepsy = failure of 2 tolerated, appropriate, adequately-dosed ASM regimens.
Timepoints: t1 = 5 min → start treating convulsive SE. t2 = 30 min → neuronal injury begins. Never wait for 30.

ILAE 2017 — three levels

▎Key points
  • Three sequential levels: seizure type → epilepsy type → epilepsy syndrome.
  • Six etiologies: structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown — considered at every level.
  • New words: focal (not partial); focal aware / focal impaired awareness; focal to bilateral tonic–clonic.

Seizure types & semiology

▎Key points
  • Read onset first, then awareness, then first prominent sign.
  • Distinguish primary generalized from focal-to-bilateral — it changes the drug.
  • Video-EEG settles mimics: syncope, PNES, migraine/TIA.

PNES flags: closed eyes, asynchronous/pelvic-thrust movements, fluctuating course, preserved awareness during bilateral motor activity, long duration. Confirm with video-EEG — don't escalate ASMs blindly.

Choosing the first drug

▎Key points
  • Focal: lamotrigine or levetiracetam first-line.
  • Generalized: valproate most effective — avoid in childbearing potential.
  • Some drugs worsen generalized seizures (see amber box).
ScenarioPreferredNote
Focal epilepsyLamotrigine / levetiracetamLTG better tolerated; titrate slow (rash)
GeneralizedValproateMost effective if not childbearing potential
AbsenceEthosuximide / valproateEthosuximide for absence-only
Childbearing potentialLevetiracetam / lamotrigineAvoid valproate; folate
SANAD: lamotrigine beat carbamazepine for focal (Arm A); valproate beat LTG/TPM for generalized (Arm B). Monotherapy meta-analysis: LTG ≈ LEV for focal; valproate best for generalized.
Avoid in generalized epilepsy (can worsen absence/myoclonus): carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, tiagabine. Also: HLA-B*15:02 screen before CBZ in at-risk Asian populations; valproate is teratogenic + neurodevelopmentally harmful; CBZ/PHT are enzyme inducers.

Status epilepticus — the ladder

▎Key points
  • Full-dose benzodiazepine first — under-dosing is the classic error.
  • Second-line: levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or valproate — all comparable (ESETT).
  • Refractory (>40 min) → anesthetic infusion + continuous EEG.

ESETT: LEV vs fosphenytoin vs valproate — no significant difference (~47% each). Pick by availability & contraindications.
Under-dosing the benzo is the most common mistake — give the full weight-based dose before escalating. IM midazolam works pre-hospital when IV access is delayed.

Interactive: status epilepticus stepper

Walk the ladder for a convulsing adult. Advance as the clock runs.

t = 0 min

0–5 min · Stabilize

  • Airway, breathing, circulation; oxygen.
  • IV access; fingerstick glucose (thiamine before glucose if malnourished/alcohol).
  • Cardiac + pulse-ox monitoring; note seizure start time.
Don't leave this phase without the time the seizure started — it drives every downstream decision.

5–20 min · First-line benzodiazepine (FULL dose)

  • IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 4 mg/dose), may repeat once; OR
  • IM midazolam 10 mg (≥40 kg) if no IV; OR
  • IV diazepam 0.15–0.2 mg/kg (max 10 mg).
The commonest error is here: under-dosing and escalating too early.

20–40 min · Second-line IV ASM

  • Levetiracetam 60 mg/kg (max 4500 mg); OR
  • Fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg (max 1500 mg PE) — cardiac monitoring; OR
  • Valproate 40 mg/kg (max 3000 mg) — avoid in pregnancy/hepatic disease.
ESETT: the three are equivalent — choose on contraindications and availability.

>40 min · Refractory SE

  • Continuous infusion: midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital.
  • Continuous EEG — titrate to seizure suppression / burst-suppression.
  • Hunt the cause: imaging, CSF, autoimmune panel (consider NORSE/new-onset refractory SE).
Super-refractory = seizures continuing/recurring ≥24 h after anesthesia started.

Drug-resistant epilepsy & surgery

▎Key points
  • Drug-resistant = 2 tolerated, appropriate, adequately-dosed regimens fail → refer to an epilepsy center.
  • Anterior temporal lobectomy beats continued medical therapy for drug-resistant MTLE (RCT-supported).
  • Refer early — surgically remediable syndromes are often left on years of polytherapy.
OptionRole
Anterior temporal lobectomyCurative for MTLE + hippocampal sclerosis

Who was included & excluded

▎Why it matters
  • The eligibility criteria are the indication — they tell you where the result applies.
  • ESETT: established (benzo-refractory) convulsive SE. SANAD: newly diagnosed epilepsy, by seizure type.
ESETT — established status epilepticus, 2nd-line drug
Included: age ≥2 y; established convulsive SE (seizures persisting despite an adequate benzodiazepine dose). Randomized to one weight-based dose of LEV / fosphenytoin / valproate over 10 min, double-blind.

Excluded / design: seizure already stopped by benzo (not "established"); known contraindication/allergy to a study drug; specific precipitants needing dedicated therapy at discretion. Primary endpoint = clinical seizure cessation + improving responsiveness at 60 min with no added ASM.

Result: ~47% cessation with each drug — no difference. → choose by availability/contraindications.
Bedside caveat: ESETT applies only after an adequate benzo has failed — it does not license skipping or under-dosing the benzodiazepine.
SANAD Arm A — focal / unclassified epilepsy
Included: newly diagnosed patients for whom carbamazepine would be standard — 88% focal (partial), 10% unclassified; clinician uncertain of best drug.
Comparison: CBZ vs gabapentin vs lamotrigine vs oxcarbazepine vs topiramate.
Result: lamotrigine best for time to treatment failure → first-line focal.
SANAD Arm B — generalized / unclassifiable epilepsy
Included: newly diagnosed patients for whom valproate would be standard — 63% idiopathic generalized, 25% unclassified.
Comparison: valproate vs lamotrigine vs topiramate.
Result: valproate best for treatment failure and 12-month remission.
Bedside caveat: map the arm to seizure type — don't apply "valproate wins" to focal epilepsy or "lamotrigine wins" to generalized.
Excluded across SANAD
Patients where the clinician was already confident of the best drug, or with a clear contraindication to a study drug. SANAD II later reaffirmed lamotrigine (focal) and valproate (generalized).
