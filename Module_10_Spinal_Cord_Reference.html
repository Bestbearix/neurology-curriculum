1 · Big picture: time is cord

The spinal cord has minimal redundancy. An acute compressive or vascular myelopathy that is left untreated for hours can convert an ambulatory patient into a permanently paraplegic one. The single most important principle in this module is: treat the patient before completing the workup when compression is suspected.

The diagnostic task is to answer three questions in rapid sequence:

  1. Is this a cord lesion? A sensory level on the trunk, bilateral long-tract signs, and sphincter dysfunction give it away. UMN signs below the level (often after a brief flaccid "spinal shock" phase) seal it.
  2. At what level? Sensory level on the trunk, motor level, and reflex pattern localise the segmental level. Image at least one level above the suspected level.
  3. What is the mechanism — compressive or not? Compression is the emergency. MRI with and without gadolinium of the whole spine is the answer in almost every case.
Three things must happen within 6 hours of a suspected acute compressive myelopathy: (1) urgent MRI — do not delay for sequential imaging or other tests; (2) IV dexamethasone if metastatic compression is plausible; (3) neurosurgical / spine surgery referral. Outcome is dictated by the neurological state at the time of decompression, not the time of presentation.

2 · Functional anatomy & vertebral mismatch

Length-axis — the vertebra is NOT the cord segment

The vertebral column is longer than the cord. Cord ends at roughly L1–L2 in adults; below that is the cauda equina. A patient with a "T12 fracture" actually has injury to the lumbosacral cord (conus); an "L2 fracture" damages the cauda equina. Always document both vertebral level (the bone) and neurological level (the cord segment).

Cross-section anatomy — the four tracts the GP must know

  • Lateral corticospinal tract (descending motor) — crosses in the medulla; ipsilateral weakness below the cord lesion.
  • Dorsal columns (vibration, proprioception, fine touch) — ascend uncrossed; cross in the medulla; ipsilateral loss below a cord lesion.
  • Lateral spinothalamic tract (pain, temperature) — crosses within 1–2 cord segments after entry; contralateral loss below the lesion (and 1–2 levels below the lesion on the side opposite the cord lesion).
  • Autonomic outflow — sympathetic (T1–L2 lateral horn) and parasympathetic (S2–S4 sacral). Above T6 → risk of autonomic dysreflexia.

Lamination matters: in the corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts, cervical fibres are medial and sacral fibres are lateral. Extrinsic (extramedullary) compression hits sacral fibres first → "sacral sparing" suggests an intramedullary lesion.

Vascular supply

  • Anterior spinal artery (single, midline) — supplies the anterior two-thirds of the cord (corticospinal, spinothalamic, autonomic, anterior grey matter). One artery, no redundancy → anterior cord ischaemia is common and devastating.
  • Posterior spinal arteries (paired) — supply the posterior third (dorsal columns).
  • The cord is fed segmentally by radicular arteries. The largest, the artery of Adamkiewicz, usually arises from a left intercostal artery between T9 and T12 and supplies the lower two-thirds of the anterior cord. Highly susceptible to occlusion by aortic dissection, aortic surgery, and severe hypotension.
  • The mid-thoracic watershed zone (around T4–T6) is the most vulnerable to hypotensive ischaemia.

4 · Approach to the suspected myelopathy

History — tempo first

Time to nadirLikely category
Seconds → minutesVascular (spinal cord stroke, dural AVF rupture); rarely cord haemorrhage; rarely trauma without obvious context
Hours → daysCompressive (haematoma, abscess, tumour with sudden decompensation, disc), severe transverse myelitis
Days → 4 weeksTransverse myelitis (most common 4–21 days to nadir), MS, NMOSD, MOGAD, sarcoid, infectious, paraneoplastic
Weeks → monthsTumour (metastatic or primary), spondylotic myelopathy, B12/copper deficiency, syringomyelia, HTLV-1, hereditary spastic paraparesis
YearsSpondylotic, hereditary spastic paraparesis, slow-growing tumour, MS progressive form

Key questions

  • Sensory level on the trunk? At what dermatome (T4 = nipple line; T10 = umbilicus; L1 = inguinal ligament; S2–4 = perineum)?
  • Bilateral leg weakness with spasticity or flaccidity (think tempo)?
  • Bowel/bladder/sexual dysfunction? New-onset urinary retention in someone with leg symptoms is a red flag.
  • Back pain at the level (compressive)?
  • Recent trauma? Anticoagulation? Coagulopathy?
  • Recent infection or vaccination (parainfectious myelitis)?
  • Cancer history? Weight loss? Night sweats?
  • Optic neuritis or bilateral severe ON in past? (NMOSD)
  • IV drug use, dental work, immunosuppression? (Abscess)
  • Aortic surgery, aortic dissection, hypotension? (Spinal cord stroke)
  • Diet (B12 from vegan diet, gastric bypass), nitrous oxide exposure?
  • Family history (HSP)?
  • Lhermitte sign / Uhthoff phenomenon (MS, B12)?

Examination

  • Motor: tone (flaccid early, then spastic), pyramidal weakness pattern, atrophy/fasciculations (LMN at the level of the lesion if grey matter involved).
  • Reflexes: brisk below the level (after spinal shock); absent at and below for acute SCI initially.
  • Sensory: test pinprick (spinothalamic), vibration and proprioception (dorsal columns), light touch — mapping the sensory level. Examine on the back as well as the front; the sensory level on the back is often clearer.
  • Sphincter: anal tone, bulbocavernosus reflex, post-void residual (catheter or bladder scan), saddle sensation (S2–S4).
  • Babinski; clonus; Hoffman sign (for cervical UMN).
  • Look for stigmata of the cause: dysraphism, neurofibromatosis, kyphoscoliosis, skin signs of vasculitis, abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Investigations

  • MRI of the whole spine with and without gadolinium — the single most important investigation. Image at least one level above the suspected sensory level.
  • CT myelogram if MRI contraindicated (pacemaker, metallic implant in cord region).
  • Plain films and CT of vertebrae for fracture or destructive lesion.
  • Lumbar puncture (after MRI rules out mass effect): cell count, protein, glucose, oligoclonal bands, IgG index, AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG, viral PCR (HSV, VZV, enterovirus, EBV, CMV, JCV, HIV, West Nile if relevant), VDRL, cytology.
  • Serum: vitamin B12 (+ methylmalonic acid & homocysteine), copper, zinc, HIV, HTLV-1 if exposure, VDRL/RPR, FTA-ABS, ANA, ANCA, anti-Ro/La, ESR, ACE, AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG, paraneoplastic panel (anti-CRMP5, anti-Hu, anti-amphiphysin, anti-GFAP).
  • Imaging for systemic disease if needed: chest CT for sarcoidosis or metastasis; PET-CT if paraneoplastic suspected.

5 · Compressive myelopathies

Metastatic epidural cord compression (MESCC) — the most common neurosurgical emergency in cancer

  • 5–10% of cancer patients; first presentation of unknown cancer in ~20%.
  • Top primary sources: breast, lung, prostate, kidney, multiple myeloma, lymphoma, colon.
  • Thoracic spine in 70%; lumbosacral 20%; cervical 10%.
  • Progressive back pain (weeks-months) preceding neurological symptoms in 90%; pain often worse with recumbency (a key clue distinguishing it from mechanical back pain).
  • Once weakness or bowel/bladder symptoms develop, deterioration to non-ambulatory status can occur within hours-days.

Acute management of suspected MESCC

  1. Stat MRI whole spine with gadolinium (not just the symptomatic level — multiple-level disease in 30–50%).
  2. Dexamethasone — 10 mg IV loading then 4 mg PO/IV every 6 hours (a higher 100 mg loading then 24 mg q6h regimen has been used but the standard moderate dose has equal efficacy and fewer complications).
  3. Neurosurgery / spine surgery referral — surgical decompression within 24 hours, followed by post-op radiotherapy, gives better ambulation outcomes than RT alone (Patchell trial 2005). Reserved for ambulatory or recently non-ambulatory patients with reasonable life expectancy > 3 months.
  4. Radiotherapy alone for patients who are not surgical candidates, radiosensitive tumours (lymphoma, myeloma, germ cell, small-cell), or multilevel disease.
  5. Treat underlying cancer (hormonal, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, bisphosphonates / denosumab).
  6. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) increasingly used.

Spondylotic myelopathy

  • Most common cause of myelopathy in adults > 55.
  • Slowly progressive cord compression from degenerative cervical (most common) or thoracic spinal stenosis: osteophytes, disc protrusion, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy.
  • Mixed UMN (corticospinal compromise) + LMN (root compromise at the level) signs — classic "myeloradiculopathy."
  • Hand clumsiness, gait stiffness, urinary urgency, Lhermitte sign, hyperreflexia, Hoffman sign, brisk jaw jerk preserved (clinches cervical not brainstem).
  • MRI shows multi-level stenosis with cord T2 hyperintensity at the worst level (myelomalacia).
  • Treatment: conservative (PT, activity modification, neck collar in selective cases) for mild disease; surgical decompression (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion, posterior laminoplasty or laminectomy ± fusion) for moderate-to-severe or progressive deficit.

Disc herniation, abscess, haematoma

  • Acute disc herniation — severe radicular pain at root level; cauda equina syndrome with large central lumbar disc.
  • Epidural abscess — back pain, fever, focal tenderness; risk factors include IV drug use, diabetes, dental procedures, recent spinal procedure, mechanical heart valve. Staph aureus most common. Stat MRI + blood cultures + emergency neurosurgical drainage + IV antibiotics covering S. aureus (vancomycin + ceftriaxone, adjust to culture).
  • Epidural haematoma — spontaneous in patients on anticoagulants; or after spinal procedure (LP, epidural). Sudden severe back pain followed by progressive neurological deficit. Stat MRI; reverse anticoagulation; emergent surgical evacuation.
Compressive cord lesion checklist (each must happen in the first 6 h):
  • Whole-spine MRI with gadolinium.
  • Dexamethasone if cancer plausible (no contraindication).
  • Neurosurgery / spine surgery referral.
  • Antibiotic if abscess suspected (vancomycin + ceftriaxone).
  • Reverse anticoagulation if haematoma suspected.

6 · Vascular cord syndromes

Spinal cord infarction (anterior spinal artery syndrome most common)

  • Sudden onset (often with pain at the level) → bilateral leg weakness + pain/temperature loss + sphincter dysfunction; dorsal columns preserved.
  • Causes: aortic dissection or aortic aneurysm repair (Adamkiewicz interruption), severe hypotension (cardiac arrest, sepsis), atherosclerosis, fibrocartilaginous embolism (Valsalva or trauma-induced), vasculitis, decompression sickness, cocaine use.
  • MRI may be normal in the first hours; DWI sequence helpful; pencil-shaped T2 hyperintensity over multiple segments on sagittal; "owl eyes" or "snake eyes" on axial (anterior horn cells).
  • Management: permissive hypertension (CSF drainage and MAP elevation are used post-aortic surgery); search for and treat the underlying cause. No proven role for thrombolysis. Outcomes generally poor — ~30% remain non-ambulatory.

Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (DAVF)

  • Most common vascular malformation of the spinal cord.
  • Typically older men; slowly progressive lower-limb spastic-flaccid weakness with sphincter dysfunction; symptoms exacerbated by exercise (venous hypertension steals from cord).
  • MRI: dilated tortuous serpentine vessels on dorsal cord surface; central cord T2 hyperintensity.
  • Diagnosis confirmed with spinal angiography.
  • Treatment: endovascular embolisation or surgical interruption. A treatable cause of progressive myelopathy that is often missed.

7 · Transverse myelitis (inflammatory / autoimmune)

Transverse myelitis is a syndrome label, not a diagnosis. After thorough workup, ~70% have an identifiable cause; ~30% remain "idiopathic". Cross-reference with Module 8 for MS / NMOSD / MOGAD.

Causes

  • MS: usually short (< 2 vertebral segments), peripheral cord lesion; partial; recovery often good.
  • NMOSD: longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (LETM, ≥ 3 vertebral segments), often central; severe; AQP4-IgG positive in 75%.
  • MOGAD: conus often involved; LETM possible; MOG-IgG positive.
  • Sarcoid myelopathy: longitudinally extensive enhancing lesion, often with dorsal subpial enhancement ("trident sign"); elevated ACE; CT chest may show lymphadenopathy.
  • Connective tissue disease: lupus, Sjögren, antiphospholipid, mixed CTD.
  • Behçet: brainstem and cord; oral and genital ulcers.
  • Paraneoplastic: anti-CRMP5 (small-cell lung cancer), anti-amphiphysin, anti-GFAP.
  • Parainfectious / post-vaccinal: most common after a viral illness or vaccine; usually monophasic.
  • Idiopathic transverse myelitis: after exhaustive workup negative.

Diagnostic workup

  • MRI whole spine ± brain with gadolinium.
  • LP: cells, protein, oligoclonal bands, IgG index, AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG, viral PCR.
  • Serum: AQP4-IgG, MOG-IgG (cell-based assays); ACE; ANA/ENA; anti-Ro/La; ANCA; cardiolipin/β2GP1; HIV, syphilis, HTLV-1 if exposure; B12, copper; paraneoplastic panel.
  • Chest CT for sarcoidosis or occult malignancy; whole-body PET-CT if paraneoplastic strongly suspected.

Treatment

  • IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 5 days.
  • Plasma exchange for severe / steroid-refractory disease (especially in NMOSD — lower threshold; better outcomes if started early).
  • Long-term immunotherapy depends on aetiology: NMOSD-specific mAbs (eculizumab/inebilizumab/satralizumab), MOGAD off-label rituximab/IVIG, sarcoid steroids + steroid-sparing, MS DMTs for MS.

9 · Metabolic, toxic & nutritional myelopathies

Vitamin B12 deficiency — subacute combined degeneration (SCD)

  • Symmetric loss of vibration and proprioception (sensory ataxia, positive Romberg) + spastic paraparesis + sometimes peripheral neuropathy + sometimes cognitive change.
  • Macrocytosis often absent — never rely on MCV to rule out B12.
  • Confirm: low B12 + elevated methylmalonic acid + elevated homocysteine.
  • Causes: pernicious anaemia, gastric bypass, ileal resection, vegan diet, metformin (mild), nitrous oxide abuse (recreational, dentists, healthcare workers using whippets) — inactivates B12 within hours.
  • Treat: cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM daily × 1 week, weekly × 4 weeks, then monthly; or high-dose oral 1–2 mg daily. Recovery often partial if treatment delayed > 1 year.

Copper deficiency myelopathy

  • Mimics B12 SCD — sensory ataxia + spastic paraparesis.
  • Causes: bariatric surgery, zinc excess (denture cream, supplements), malabsorption.
  • Confirm: low serum copper, low ceruloplasmin; elevated zinc if exogenous.
  • Treat: oral or IV copper replacement; remove zinc source. Recovery may be incomplete.

Folate deficiency

Rarely causes myelopathy alone; mainly neuropsychiatric and peripheral.

Heroin and other toxins

Heroin myelopathy (rare); chemotherapy intrathecal toxicity (methotrexate, cytarabine); decompression sickness ("caisson disease"); arsenic, lead; konzo (cyanide from cassava — tropical spastic paraparesis in malnourished children).

Radiation myelopathy

Two forms: early transient (1–6 months after RT, Lhermitte sign, resolves) and late delayed (6–18 months, progressive). MRI may show cord swelling acutely, then atrophy. Reduce risk by limiting cord dose. Treatment is supportive; case reports of bevacizumab for delayed form.

10 · Degenerative & hereditary myelopathies

Hereditary spastic paraparesis (HSP)

  • Group of genetic disorders with pure or complicated spastic paraparesis. Most common autosomal dominant gene: SPAST (SPG4).
  • Insidious progressive lower-extremity spasticity, gait stiffness, urinary urgency. Sensation relatively spared.
  • Complicated forms: + ataxia, neuropathy, cognitive impairment, retinopathy, etc.
  • Diagnosis: family history, genetic testing. Differentiate from MS (no MRI lesions), B12 deficiency (normal labs), HTLV-1 (negative serology), dopa-responsive dystonia.
  • Treat: baclofen, tizanidine, botulinum toxin, intrathecal baclofen; PT/OT.

Adrenoleukodystrophy / adrenomyeloneuropathy (X-linked)

  • ABCD1 gene mutation; very-long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA) accumulation.
  • Adrenomyeloneuropathy (AMN) in young men: progressive spastic paraparesis + adrenal insufficiency + peripheral neuropathy; cognitive features later.
  • Serum VLCFAs elevated.
  • Treat: adrenal hormone replacement; HSCT in early childhood cerebral form (not effective for AMN).

Spinocerebellar ataxias and Friedreich ataxia

Combined cord + cerebellar involvement; covered in cerebellar disorders typically. Friedreich: GAA expansion in FXN; childhood/adolescent onset; areflexia + dorsal column loss + cerebellar ataxia + cardiomyopathy + diabetes; omaveloxolone (FDA-approved 2023) is the first DMT.

11 · Syringomyelia

  • Fluid-filled cavity within the spinal cord, usually central / cervical.
  • Classic presentation: dissociated sensory loss in a "cape" or "shawl" distribution (loss of pain/temperature, preserved touch and proprioception) over the shoulders and upper arms; followed by LMN weakness in arms (anterior horn involvement at expanding cord level); UMN signs in legs (corticospinal involvement) develop later.
  • Often associated with Chiari I malformation; also post-traumatic, post-myelitic, or with spinal cord tumour.
  • MRI confirms the syrinx and identifies underlying cause.
  • Treat: suboccipital decompression for Chiari-associated syrinx; syringosubarachnoid shunt for refractory cases.

12 · Traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI)

Epidemiology

~17,000 new cases per year in the US; motor vehicle accidents and falls leading causes. Median age has shifted upward over recent decades (elderly falls with pre-existing cervical stenosis).

Acute care

  1. ABCDE per ATLS; cervical-spine immobilisation until cleared clinically (NEXUS / Canadian C-spine rule) and radiographically.
  2. Maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 85 mmHg for 5–7 days to optimise cord perfusion (consensus guideline; weak evidence). Use vasopressors as needed.
  3. Stat CT cervical / thoracic / lumbar spine for fracture detail; MRI for cord assessment, ligamentous injury, and disc herniation.
  4. Surgical decompression: timing remains debated, but most evidence favours early decompression (< 24 h) for incomplete cervical SCI with persistent cord compression (STASCIS, AOSpine guidelines).
  5. High-dose methylprednisolone for SCI is controversial. The NASCIS protocols showed marginal benefit at considerable cost (infection, GI bleed, mortality). Current AANS/CNS guidelines do not recommend routine high-dose methylprednisolone; AOSpine 2017 guideline allows it as an option in patients within 8 h of blunt SCI but acknowledges low quality of evidence. Many centres no longer use it.
  6. DVT prophylaxis (mechanical immediately, pharmacological after 24–72 h if no haemorrhagic concern).
  7. Pressure-area care; bladder management (intermittent catheterisation).

Spinal shock vs neurogenic shock

  • Spinal shock — transient flaccid areflexic state below the SCI level lasting days to weeks; bulbocavernosus reflex returns first; defined neurologically.
  • Neurogenic shock — hypotension + bradycardia due to loss of sympathetic outflow (lesions above T6); requires careful fluid + vasopressor (norepinephrine) management. Distinguish from haemorrhagic shock; trauma patients often have both.

Autonomic dysreflexia

  • Occurs in chronic SCI above T6 level when a noxious stimulus below the lesion triggers unregulated sympathetic outflow.
  • Trigger: bladder distension (most common — blocked catheter), bowel impaction, pressure ulcer, ingrown toenail, urinary tract infection, sexual activity, labour.
  • Symptoms: pounding headache, severe paroxysmal hypertension (systolic often > 200), flushing/sweating above the level, pallor and pilo-erection below, bradycardia (reflex).
  • Management: sit the patient up; loosen clothing; find and remove the trigger immediately (catheter for distended bladder, manual disimpaction for stool). Antihypertensive (nifedipine bite-and-swallow, nitroglycerin, captopril) only if BP severely elevated and trigger not quickly removable. Untreated → stroke, seizure, MI, death.
Autonomic dysreflexia is a true neurological emergency. If a chronic SCI patient with a high lesion presents with severe headache and hypertension, do not give labetalol or other long-acting agents reflexively — check for blocked catheter or impacted bowel first; remove the trigger; the BP will often normalise within minutes.

14 · Neurogenic bladder & bowel

Bladder patterns

Lesion sitePattern
Cord above sacral level (suprasacral)Upper-motor-neuron / "spastic" bladder — detrusor hyperreflexia, often with detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia; urgency, incontinence, incomplete emptying.
Conus or caudaLower-motor-neuron / "flaccid" bladder — detrusor areflexia, overflow incontinence, retention.

Management principles

  • Urodynamic assessment.
  • Clean intermittent self-catheterisation (preferred) over indwelling Foley.
  • Antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, solifenacin, tolterodine) or β3 agonist (mirabegron — avoids anticholinergic burden) for overactive detrusor.
  • Onabotulinum toxin A into detrusor for refractory neurogenic detrusor overactivity.
  • α-blockers (tamsulosin) for sphincter dyssynergia.
  • Cathetersed bladder — high-risk for UTI, bladder cancer (long-term); urological follow-up essential.
  • Bowel: routine bowel programme, fibre, suppositories, enemas, manual evacuation; surveillance for impaction (autonomic dysreflexia trigger).

15 · When to refer

Emergency — same-day spine surgery / neurosurgery:
  • Suspected compressive cord lesion (MESCC, epidural abscess, epidural haematoma).
  • Cauda equina syndrome (bilateral leg pain + saddle anaesthesia + new bowel/bladder dysfunction).
  • Conus medullaris syndrome.
  • Traumatic SCI.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia in chronic SCI patient with high cord lesion.
Urgent neurology referral: transverse myelitis (start steroids; refer for full demyelinating workup); spinal cord stroke; suspected spondylotic myelopathy with progressive symptoms or significant disability; B12 / copper deficiency myelopathy that does not improve with replacement; suspected hereditary myelopathy for genetic testing.
