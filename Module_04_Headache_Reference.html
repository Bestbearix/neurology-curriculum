1 · Big picture: primary vs secondary

The very first task with any patient who says "headache" is to decide whether you are dealing with a primary headache disorder (the headache is the disease — migraine, tension, cluster) or a secondary headache (the headache is a symptom of something else — SAH, meningitis, tumor, GCA, dissection, CVST, glaucoma, etc.).

PrimaryLifetime prevalenceHallmarks
Tension-type headache70–90%Bilateral, pressing/tightening, mild–moderate, no nausea, no aggravation by activity. Self-treated.
Migraine~12–15% (17% women, 6% men)Recurrent disabling attacks, lasting 4–72 h, with photo/phonophobia, nausea; often unilateral and throbbing.
Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (TACs)< 1%Strictly unilateral, severe, with prominent ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms (lacrimation, conjunctival injection, ptosis, miosis, rhinorrhoea, eyelid oedema).

Most patients with disabling recurrent headache who reach a clinician have migraine, regardless of how they label it. Tension-type is more prevalent but rarely brings patients to medical attention.

Foundational rule: the differential for a new-onset severe headache is very different from that of a chronic, recurrent headache. Always ask: "Is this new, or different from your usual headaches?"

Any one SNOOP feature warrants imaging — usually MRI brain with and without contrast (or non-contrast CT if SAH is the worry).

3 · Initial workup & when to image

History — the heart of headache medicine

  • Onset — abrupt vs gradual; age at first attack; context (trauma, post-partum).
  • Timing — frequency, duration, time to peak, diurnal pattern (wakening from sleep is a red flag).
  • Quality — throbbing, pressing, sharp, lancinating, burning.
  • Laterality & location — unilateral (side-locked vs alternating) vs bilateral; retro-orbital, temporal, occipital.
  • Severity & disability — VAS, ability to function.
  • Pattern change — the single most powerful red flag in a known headache patient.
  • Associated features — photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, autonomic features, aura, neurologic deficit.
  • Premonitory & postdrome — yawning, sleepiness, neck stiffness, cognitive sluggishness; typical of migraine.
  • Triggers — menses, sleep, stress, alcohol, altitude, Valsalva, position.
  • Family history — migraine, aneurysm, malignancy.
  • Medications — analgesic use frequency (MOH risk), nitrates, vasodilators, oestrogens, OCP/HRT.

Examination essentials

BP, fundoscopy (papilloedema, venous pulsations), temporal artery palpation, neck ROM & bruits, pericranial nerve tenderness, cranial nerves and pupils, full neuro exam.

Choosing wisely: when to image

The American Headache Society advises not to image stable migraine without red flags and not to use CT when MRI is available (except in the ED).

ScenarioImaging of choice
Thunderclap headacheNon-contrast CT first; if negative within 6 h and suspicion remains → LP for xanthochromia or CTA
New headache + focal deficit, altered mentation, fever, or papilloedemaMRI brain ± contrast; CT if MR not available
New headache > 50 y with jaw claudication / scalp tendernessESR / CRP + temporal artery biopsy; treat as GCA
Headache + raised ICP suspicion (positional, Valsalva, nocturnal)MRI + MR venography (rule out CVST and lesion)
Pregnant / postpartum new headacheMRI / MR venography (no gadolinium if avoidable)
Stable primary headache pattern, no red flagsNo imaging needed

4 · Migraine

Diagnosis (ICHD-3 simplified)

Repeated attacks of headache lasting 4–72 h with normal examination and no other cause, plus:

At least 2 of:Plus at least 1 of:
UnilateralNausea / vomiting
PulsatingPhotophobia and phonophobia
Moderate or severe intensity
Aggravated by routine activity

Adapted from Merritt's Table 56.2 / ICHD-3.

POUND mnemonic (4 of 5 = migraine highly likely): Pulsatile, One-day duration (4–72 h), Unilateral, Nausea/vomiting, Disabling. ID Migraine / PIN: photophobia, inability to function, nausea (2 of 3 → migraine likely).

Migraine aura

20–25% have aura: fully reversible neurological symptoms developing over 5–20 min, lasting < 60 min, immediately followed (or accompanied) by headache. Most often visual (scintillating zigzag, fortification spectra, scotoma); also sensory (slow tingling march), aphasic, or motor (hemiplegic migraine — FHM gene mutations in CACNA1A, ATP1A2, SCN1A).

Distinguish aura from TIA: migraine aura builds slowly with positive symptoms (scintillations, tingling), then resolves over minutes. TIA produces sudden negative symptoms (visual loss, weakness) and usually has no headache progression.

Chronic migraine

≥ 15 headache days/month for > 3 months, of which ≥ 8 have migrainous features. Often coexists with medication overuse and depression. Refer.

Triptan and ergot contraindications: coronary or cerebrovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy (relative), Prinzmetal angina, < 24 h after another triptan or ergot. Gepants and ditans are safer in vascular disease.

Preventive treatment — when and what

Offer prevention when migraine attacks are ≥ 4 per month, prolonged, disabling, refractory to acute treatment, contraindications to acute drugs, or rare disabling subtypes (hemiplegic, migraine with brainstem aura).

5 · Tension-type headache

The most prevalent but least disabling primary headache. Bilateral pressing / tightening pain, mild–moderate, no nausea, not aggravated by routine activity, ± mild photo- or phonophobia (not both). Pericranial muscle tenderness common.

Acute: simple analgesics (acetaminophen, NSAID). Prevention when frequent: tricyclic (amitriptyline 25–75 mg nightly is first-line). Address triggers (stress, sleep, posture, caffeine excess); physical therapy, biofeedback, CBT have evidence.

6 · Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias

All TACs share strictly unilateral head pain with ipsilateral cranial autonomic features. They are classified by attack duration and frequency.

SUNCT / SUNA1–600 sDozens to hundreds/dayStab-like; SUNCT has conjunctival injection + tearing; SUNA has either alone.
Hemicrania continuaContinuous, fluctuating—Continuous unilateral pain with exacerbations; absolute response to indomethacin.

Cluster headache — what every clinician should know

Periorbital "ice pick / hot poker" pain lasting 15–180 min, often nocturnal, with ipsilateral lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion/rhinorrhoea, ptosis, miosis, eyelid oedema, sweating. Attacks group into clusters lasting weeks-months with remission between (episodic) or do not remit (chronic). Patient is restless — pacing, banging head — in striking contrast to the migraineur who lies still in a dark room.

Acute treatment:

  • 100% oxygen by non-rebreather mask 12–15 L/min for 15–20 min — first-line, abortive in > 70%.
  • Sumatriptan 6 mg SC or zolmitriptan 5–10 mg nasal spray.
  • Intranasal lidocaine 4% as adjunct.

Preventive (within a cluster):

  • Verapamil 240–960 mg/d (titrate with ECG monitoring for PR prolongation) — first-line.
  • Bridge with prednisone taper (60 mg → off over 2–3 weeks) or occipital nerve block while verapamil is titrated.
  • Galcanezumab (CGRP mAb) approved for episodic cluster.
  • Lithium for chronic cluster (close monitoring).
Indomethacin trial — if a TAC seems to have features of paroxysmal hemicrania or hemicrania continua, a brief course of indomethacin (e.g. 25–75 mg TID) confirms the diagnosis through complete response. With GI protection (PPI) for the trial.

Giant cell arteritis is a "treat first, biopsy later" emergency. Visual loss can be permanent and bilateral within days. Start high-dose prednisone (40–60 mg/d, or IV methylprednisolone if visual symptoms) immediately on suspicion; biopsy can still be diagnostic up to 2 weeks after starting steroids.

8 · Trigeminal neuralgia & facial pain

Classical trigeminal neuralgia

Brief (< 2 s to 2 min), paroxysmal, lancinating "electric shock" pain in the distribution of one or more trigeminal branches (V2 > V3 >> V1), triggered by light touch, chewing, talking, tooth brushing, or a cold breeze. Pain-free intervals between paroxysms. Examination is normal between attacks. Most commonly from vascular compression of the trigeminal root entry zone (superior cerebellar artery loop). Onset usually > 50 y.

Symptomatic / painful trigeminal neuropathy = caused by an identifiable lesion (tumour, MS plaque, vascular malformation, post-herpetic). MRI mandatory in patients < 40, those with V1 involvement, sensory deficit, or bilateral pain.

TreatmentDetail
First-lineCarbamazepine 200–1200 mg/d (start 100 mg BID, titrate); response often > 70%. Or oxcarbazepine 300–1800 mg/d (better tolerated).
Add-on / alternativeGabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, baclofen.
Surgical (refractory)Microvascular decompression (Jannetta), percutaneous balloon compression or glycerol rhizotomy, radiofrequency thermocoagulation, Gamma Knife stereotactic radiosurgery.

Other facial pain disorders

  • Postherpetic trigeminal neuropathy — after V1 zoster; constant burning + allodynia. Treat with tricyclics, gabapentin/pregabalin, topical lidocaine; consider early antiviral + vaccination to prevent.
  • Glossopharyngeal neuralgia — ear/throat pain triggered by swallowing.
  • Persistent idiopathic facial pain — constant, dull, poorly localised; previously "atypical facial pain". Treat as neuropathic pain; coordinate care with dentistry/psychiatry.
  • Burning mouth syndrome.

9 · Medication overuse headache

Regular intake of acute headache drugs ≥ 10 days/month (triptans, ergots, opioids, combination analgesics) or ≥ 15 days/month for simple analgesics over > 3 months can produce a daily refractory headache — medication overuse headache (MOH). MOH is the most common cause of "transformation" from episodic to chronic daily headache.

Management: stop the overused drug (cold turkey usually for triptans / simple analgesics; taper for opioids and barbiturates), bridge with a preventive (topiramate, amitriptyline, CGRP mAb) and a short course of bridge therapy (e.g. naproxen 500 mg BID for 1–2 weeks, or a steroid taper), warn the patient that headaches will worsen for 2–6 weeks before improving.

Counsel from day one: with any acute migraine drug, limit use to < 2 days/week. Frequent acute use predicts MOH and progression to chronic migraine.

10 · When to refer

Same-day or emergency:
  • Thunderclap headache.
  • New headache with focal deficit, seizure, altered mentation, or fever.
  • Sudden visual loss + headache (suspect GCA or pituitary apoplexy).
  • Eye pain + halos + red eye + nausea (angle-closure glaucoma).
  • New severe headache in pregnancy/postpartum (CVST, PRES, RCVS).
  • Acute Horner syndrome with neck pain (carotid dissection).
Routine neurology / headache clinic: chronic migraine, refractory cluster, suspected hemicrania continua / paroxysmal hemicrania for indomethacin trial, drug-resistant trigeminal neuralgia, suspected IIH for ophthalmology coordination, medication overuse headache requiring inpatient withdrawal.
