Why this module?

Headache is one of the most common reasons patients come to medical attention, and globally the leading neurological cause of disability. Most are primary disorders that respond to good diagnosis and a structured treatment plan. The danger is missing the small fraction that are secondary — SAH, GCA, dissection, CVST, glaucoma, tumour.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Run a structured headache history that captures red flags.
  • Apply SNOOP to triage urgency.
  • Diagnose migraine, tension-type, and cluster confidently.
  • Choose a stratified acute and preventive plan in migraine.
  • Recognise cluster and offer first-line treatment (100% O₂, SC sumatriptan, verapamil).
  • Identify and refer the secondary headaches that must not be missed.
  • Diagnose and start treatment for classical trigeminal neuralgia.
  • Recognise medication overuse headache and counsel about it.
The single sentence to remember: in headache, the dangerous diagnoses are usually new, sudden, or pattern-changing. Run SNOOP every time.

Lessons

1. Primary vs secondary headache

Primary: migraine, tension-type, TACs (cluster, paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT/SUNA, hemicrania continua), other primary types (cough, exertional, sex, thunderclap, cold-stimulus, hypnic, NDPH).

Secondary: vascular (SAH, ICH, CVST, dissection, RCVS, PRES), infection (meningitis, encephalitis, abscess), raised or low ICP (tumour, IIH, CSF leak), inflammation (GCA), ocular (acute angle-closure glaucoma), sinusitis, post-traumatic, MOH.

Most patients reaching a clinician with disabling recurrent headache have migraine — even when they don't call it that.

2. SNOOP — the red-flag mnemonic
  • Systemic — fever, weight loss, malignancy, HIV, pregnancy.
  • Neurologic — focal deficit, altered mentation, seizure, papilloedema, Valsalva-induced.
  • Onset — thunderclap (peak < 1 min).
  • Older — first headache after 50.
  • Pattern change — character/frequency/severity different from usual headaches.

Use the SNOOP Checker tab to walk through any patient encounter.

Any single SNOOP feature warrants imaging — MRI brain (± contrast / MRV) unless thunderclap when NCCT goes first.
3. Migraine — diagnosis

ICHD-3 criteria (simplified): 4–72 h attacks, normal exam, no other cause, plus ≥ 2 of (unilateral, throbbing, moderate-severe, aggravated by activity) plus ≥ 1 of (nausea/vomiting OR photo+phonophobia).

POUND (4 of 5 = migraine likely): Pulsatile, One-day, Unilateral, Nausea, Disabling.

Aura: in 20–25%. Visual most common — scintillating zigzag spreading over 5–20 min, lasting < 60 min, followed (or accompanied) by headache.

Migraine vs TIA: migraine aura builds slowly with positive symptoms then resolves. TIA is sudden negative symptoms, no headache build-up.
4. Migraine — acute treatment
SeverityFirst-line
MildNSAID (ibuprofen, naproxen) ± metoclopramide
ModerateOral triptan (sumatriptan 50–100 mg, rizatriptan 10 mg, eletriptan 40 mg) ± naproxen
Severe / nauseaSC sumatriptan 6 mg, intranasal zolmitriptan 5 mg, IV dopamine antagonist + ketorolac
Vascular contraindicationGepants (rimegepant, ubrogepant) or ditans (lasmiditan)

Take acute drugs early. Combine triptan + naproxen for better outcomes and lower recurrence. Limit acute drug days to < 2/week to avoid MOH.

Avoid opioids and butalbital combinations for migraine — they cause MOH and worsen migraine progression.
5. Migraine — preventive treatment

Indications: ≥ 4 attacks/month, disabling, prolonged, refractory, or with hemiplegic / brainstem aura.

6. Cluster headache and other TACs

Strictly unilateral, severe peri-orbital pain with ipsilateral cranial autonomic features. Patient is restless / paces (vs migraine who lies still).

Acute: 100% O₂ via non-rebreather 12–15 L/min for 15–20 min · SC sumatriptan 6 mg · intranasal zolmitriptan 5–10 mg.

Prevention: verapamil 240–960 mg/d (titrate with ECG) · bridge with prednisone taper or occipital nerve block · galcanezumab for episodic cluster · lithium for chronic.

Indomethacin trial: if features suggest paroxysmal hemicrania or hemicrania continua (more frequent attacks, shorter duration), a brief course of indomethacin (with PPI cover) is both diagnostic and therapeutic.

8. Trigeminal neuralgia

Brief lancinating "electric" pain in V2 > V3 distribution, triggered by light touch, chewing, cold air. Examination normal between attacks. Most often from vascular loop compression at root entry zone.

MRI mandatory in: age < 40, V1 involvement, sensory deficit, bilateral pain — rule out MS plaque or tumour.

First-line: carbamazepine 200–1200 mg/d (or oxcarbazepine 300–1800 mg/d — better tolerated).

Add-on: gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, baclofen.

Refractory: microvascular decompression (Jannetta), percutaneous procedures, Gamma Knife.

9. Medication overuse headache (MOH)

Acute drug days > 10/month for triptans, ergots, opioids, combinations; or > 15/month for simple analgesics, over > 3 months → daily refractory headache. The most common cause of "transformation" to chronic daily headache.

Management: stop the overused drug (taper opioids/barbiturates), bridge with a preventive (topiramate, amitriptyline, or CGRP mAb) and a short course of bridge therapy (naproxen 500 mg BID × 1–2 weeks, or steroid taper). Warn the patient: headaches will worsen for 2–6 weeks before improving.

Counsel from day one: < 2 acute drug days per week. Frequent acute use predicts MOH.
10. Red flags & when to refer
Same-day / emergency referral: thunderclap headache · new headache + focal deficit/seizure/fever · sudden vision loss + headache · acute angle-closure glaucoma · acute Horner + neck pain · new headache in pregnancy/postpartum.
Routine neurology / headache clinic: chronic migraine, refractory cluster, suspected hemicrania continua/paroxysmal hemicrania for indomethacin trial, refractory TN, IIH for ophthalmology coordination, MOH requiring inpatient withdrawal.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 32-year-old woman with monthly throbbing headaches

For 10 years, ~3 days/month she has right-sided throbbing headache for 12–24 h with nausea, light and sound sensitivity, and disability that keeps her in bed. No aura, no fever, no neurological symptoms.

Diagnosis: migraine without aura.

Plan: education + headache diary; acute treatment: NSAID + metoclopramide OR oral triptan; review pattern in 3 months. Counsel < 2 days/week acute drug use. Preventive not yet needed (3 attacks/month with good response to acute).

Case 2 — A 47-year-old man with nightly right peri-orbital pain

For 3 weeks, every night around 1 AM he wakes with 30–90 min of severe right peri-orbital "ice-pick" pain, right eye watery and red, right nostril running, right lid drooping. Paces the room during the attack. Family thinks "he's gone crazy" with the pain.

Diagnosis: episodic cluster headache.

Plan: acute — 100% O₂ via non-rebreather 12 L/min at attack onset; SC sumatriptan 6 mg as backup. Preventive — start verapamil and bridge with a 2-week prednisone taper while titrating. Get baseline ECG. Brain MRI if any atypical features. Counsel about avoiding alcohol during a cluster bout.

Case 3 — A 72-year-old woman with new headache and fatigue

Three weeks of right temporal headache, scalp tenderness when combing hair, jaw aching while chewing tough food, weight loss. ESR 88, CRP elevated.

Diagnosis: giant cell arteritis.

Plan: Start prednisone 60 mg PO daily immediately (or IV methylprednisolone if any visual symptoms). Arrange temporal artery biopsy within 1–2 weeks (still diagnostic up to 2 weeks on steroids). PPI cover + bone protection + ophthalmology review.

Visual loss in untreated GCA can be permanent and bilateral within days. Steroids first, biopsy later.
Case 4 — A 28-year-old woman, postpartum 2 weeks, with worsening headache and a seizure

Five days of worsening headache, now with confusion and a focal motor seizure. BP 130/80. CT shows parasagittal hyperdensities.

Diagnosis: cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (postpartum).

Plan: CT or MR venography confirms. Start anticoagulation (LMWH then warfarin or DOAC) even with haemorrhagic infarct. Antiseizure drug. Investigate for thrombophilia. Discuss future contraception (avoid combined OCP).

Case 5 — A 60-year-old with brief electric-shock right cheek pain

For 6 weeks, 5–30 second bursts of right cheek shooting pain triggered by tooth brushing, chewing, cold wind. Examination normal between attacks.

Diagnosis: classical trigeminal neuralgia (V2 distribution).

Plan: Start carbamazepine 100 mg BID, titrate to effect (monitor sodium, LFTs, CBC; HLA-B*1502 screening for Asian patients). MRI brain to look for vascular loop, MS plaque, tumour (mandatory if age < 40, sensory deficit, V1, bilateral). If refractory or intolerant: oxcarbazepine, gabapentin, baclofen, or surgical options (microvascular decompression best evidence).
