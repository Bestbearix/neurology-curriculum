1. Framework — recovery, the rehabilitation team, and ICF

Neurorehabilitation is the discipline of maximising function, participation, and quality of life after neurological injury. It is not a synonym for "physiotherapy" — it is a multidisciplinary, goal-oriented, time-limited process that takes a patient from impairment through rehabilitation hospital and out-patient programmes to community living, return to work, and independent participation.

The WHO ICF model (International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, 2001)

  • Body structure / function: anatomy (e.g., MCA infarct) and impairment (e.g., right hemiparesis).
  • Activity: what the person can do (e.g., walk 50 m with a stick).
  • Participation: role engagement (e.g., back at work as an accountant).
  • Environmental factors: built environment, family, services, technology.
  • Personal factors: age, education, motivation, comorbidity, premorbid function.
The single principle: rehabilitation goals are set at the activity and participation levels, not at the impairment level. Restoring full strength may be impossible after a large stroke; restoring independent walking, returning to work, and resuming family roles are usually possible with structured rehabilitation. The patient and family define the goals; the team finds the route.

2. Mechanisms of recovery — neuroplasticity

  • Acute (hours–days): resolution of oedema, reperfusion, reversal of metabolic depression in penumbra (diaschisis).
  • Subacute (days–weeks): unmasking of latent connections; axonal sprouting; synaptogenesis; cortical map reorganisation; contralesional hemisphere takes on function.
  • Chronic (months–years): continued cortical map reorganisation; compensation strategies; experience-dependent plasticity; learning-based recovery (use-dependent).

Principles of experience-dependent plasticity (Kleim & Jones)

  1. Use it or lose it — unused circuits regress.
  2. Use it and improve it — training enhances specific circuits.
  3. Specificity — the nature of the training defines the gains.
  4. Repetition matters — sufficient repetition required for lasting change.
  5. Intensity matters — sufficient training intensity required.
  6. Time matters — different forms of plasticity at different time points.
  7. Salience matters — training must be meaningful to the patient.
  8. Age matters — plasticity is greater in younger brains.
  9. Transference — training can enhance non-trained behaviours.
  10. Interference — maladaptive plasticity (learned non-use) can impair recovery.

Adjuncts to neuroplasticity

  • Pharmacological: dopaminergics for arousal (amantadine — TBI recovery; Giacino NEJM 2012); SSRIs (FLAME 2011 — fluoxetine improved motor recovery; later FOCUS / EFFECTS / AFFINITY trials 2019–2020 did not replicate the motor benefit, but supported reduced post-stroke depression). Avoid drugs that impair plasticity (benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, typical antipsychotics).
  • Non-invasive brain stimulation: rTMS, tDCS — promising in stroke and aphasia; not yet standard of care.
  • Robotics and BCIs: emerging.

4. The multidisciplinary rehabilitation team

  • Rehabilitation physician (physiatrist): medical oversight, prescription of therapies.
  • Physiotherapist: motor recovery, gait, balance, spasticity, mobility, equipment.
  • Occupational therapist: upper limb function, ADLs, splinting, environmental adaptation, cognitive rehab, driving assessment.
  • Speech and language therapist: dysphagia, aphasia, dysarthria, cognitive-communication.
  • Clinical psychologist / neuropsychologist: cognitive assessment, mood, behavioural intervention, family support.
  • Rehabilitation nurse: bowel / bladder management, skin care, education, coordination.
  • Social worker: discharge planning, benefits, community reintegration, family support.
  • Orthotist / prosthetist: bracing, AFOs, splints.
  • Dietitian: nutrition (enteral feeding, swallow-safe diets).
  • Vocational rehabilitation specialist: return to work / education.
  • Family + caregivers are partners in goal-setting and intervention.

5. Stroke rehabilitation

Early phase

  • Very early mobilisation: AVERT trial (Lancet 2015) — frequent, high-dose mobilisation within 24 h of stroke worse than usual care; early but short, frequent sessions are recommended; avoid very high doses in the first 24 hours.
  • Stroke unit care reduces mortality and dependency.
  • VTE prophylaxis; aspiration precautions; positioning; pressure care.

Motor recovery

  • Constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT): EXCITE trial (Wolf JAMA 2006) — constraining the unaffected limb 90% of waking hours + intensive shaping of the affected limb for 2 weeks improves upper-limb function in selected patients (some active wrist + finger extension preserved).
  • Repetitive task-specific training: high-dose, task-relevant practice — modest gains.
  • Robotic-assisted upper limb training: similar gains to intensive matched-dose therapy.
  • Mental practice and mirror therapy: improve upper-limb function and reduce hemi-neglect.
  • Treadmill training with body-weight support: regains gait; modest evidence; consider in selected patients.
  • Functional electrical stimulation (FES): for foot drop; selected upper limb applications.
  • SSRI for motor recovery: FLAME (Chollet Lancet Neurol 2011) showed fluoxetine improved Fugl-Meyer motor scores. Subsequent larger trials (FOCUS UK, EFFECTS Sweden, AFFINITY Australia, 2019–2020) did NOT replicate the motor benefit but supported reduced depression incidence. SSRI not routinely used for motor recovery after these trials, but still used for post-stroke depression (Module 24).

Aphasia

  • Intensive speech-language therapy in subacute phase; CIAT (Constraint-Induced Aphasia Therapy); melodic intonation therapy for non-fluent aphasia; semantic feature analysis for anomia.
  • High-dose intervention superior to low-dose (Brady Cochrane 2016).
  • tDCS over Broca's area + intensive language therapy emerging.

Dysphagia

  • Screen every stroke patient (water swallow test on admission); NPO until cleared.
  • Formal assessment: videofluoroscopy (VFSS) or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES).
  • Compensatory: chin tuck, head turn, smaller bolus, thickened fluids, IDDSI diet classification.
  • Rehabilitative: shaker exercise, effortful swallow, Mendelsohn manoeuvre, neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES — selected).
  • NG / PEG feeding if persistent severe dysphagia.

Hemispatial neglect

  • Right parietal lesion classic; affects rehabilitation engagement.
  • Prism adaptation + scanning training + visual exploration training + limb activation; modest evidence; rTMS to contralesional parietal cortex emerging.

Shoulder complications

  • Shoulder subluxation: very common in flaccid hemiparesis; supportive sling + handling education; FES; neglect of the shoulder leads to pain.
  • Hemiplegic shoulder pain: shoulder support; analgesia; physiotherapy.
  • Adhesive capsulitis: passive range exercises.

Spasticity (see Section 11)

Botulinum toxin A for focal spasticity; oral antispastics; intrathecal baclofen for severe diffuse spasticity.

6. Spinal cord injury rehabilitation

Acute and ICU phase (cross-ref Modules 10 + 22)

  • Stabilisation; pressure care; VTE prophylaxis (LMWH from 72 h after acute injury if no contraindication); bowel / bladder management; psychological support.
  • ASIA assessment serially.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia prevention (above T6 lesions): identify and remove noxious stimuli; sit up; loosen restrictive clothing; nifedipine IR or topical nitrate if persistent.

Inpatient rehabilitation

  • Activity-based therapy: high-dose task-specific motor training; body-weight-supported treadmill training; exoskeleton-assisted walking; epidural stimulation (Harkema, Courtine — selected patients with chronic complete SCI have regained voluntary movement with epidural stimulation + training).
  • Wheelchair training: prescription (manual / powered; tilt-in-space; cushioning), skills training, transfers.
  • Bladder management: intermittent self-catheterisation; suprapubic catheter; baclofen / botulinum toxin to the detrusor (or external sphincter); avoid long-term indwelling catheter (UTI, stones, autonomic dysreflexia).
  • Bowel management: scheduled programmes; digital stimulation; suppositories; gastrocolic reflex use; constipation management.
  • Sexual function: counselling; PDE5 inhibitors; mechanical aids; fertility (electroejaculation for men); pregnancy planning (autonomic dysreflexia risk in T6+ lesions).
  • Pressure injury prevention: regular position changes; pressure-relief cushions / mattresses; skin inspection; nutrition.
  • Spasticity: baclofen (oral / intrathecal), tizanidine, botulinum toxin, gabapentin.
  • Heterotopic ossification: pain + reduced range; bisphosphonate, NSAID; surgical excision when matured.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia education for patient + family (T6 and above lesions).

Long-term issues

  • Cardiovascular risk; respiratory issues (cervical lesions); chronic pain (at-level + below-level + nociceptive); depression; community reintegration; vocational rehabilitation; aging with SCI.
  • Cross-ref Module 10 (SCI), 15 (autonomic dysreflexia), 22 (acute SCI).

7. Traumatic brain injury rehabilitation

Disorders of consciousness

  • Coma → vegetative state (now "unresponsive wakefulness syndrome") → minimally conscious state → emergence to consciousness.
  • Assess with Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R); avoid premature withdrawal.
  • Amantadine (Giacino NEJM 2012): 200–400 mg/day improves recovery in vegetative / minimally conscious states within 4 weeks of injury.
  • Modafinil, methylphenidate emerging.

Post-traumatic amnesia (PTA)

Time-orientation + memory impairment; resolves over days–weeks; orient frequently; reduce stimulation; family education; PTA duration predicts cognitive recovery (longer = worse).

Cognitive rehabilitation

  • Attention training (e.g., APT — attention process training).
  • Memory rehabilitation: external aids (smartphone calendars, alarms), errorless learning, spaced retrieval.
  • Executive function rehabilitation: goal management training, problem-solving training.
  • Compensation strategies + environmental adaptation.

Behavioural & emotional

  • Aggression, disinhibition, apathy, depression, anxiety, PTSD.
  • Behavioural intervention + family / staff education + SSRI for mood; AVOID benzodiazepines (impair recovery) + typical antipsychotics where possible.
  • Post-traumatic seizures: prophylaxis with levetiracetam in first week post-severe TBI; long-term ASMs if seizures occur.

Concussion & mTBI

  • Most resolve within 4 weeks.
  • Graded return to activity (and to sport) protocols (Berlin consensus; CISG): symptom-limited cognitive + physical rest 24–48 h, then gradual return through 6 stages (light aerobic → sport-specific → non-contact → full contact → return); 24 h between stages; relapse → return to previous stage.
  • Persistent post-concussion symptoms (PPCS): multidisciplinary management; treat headache, sleep, mood, vestibular, cervical, autonomic; CBT; gradual return.
  • Second impact syndrome (rare but catastrophic) — diffuse cerebral oedema with second injury before first has resolved.

8. Multiple sclerosis rehabilitation

  • Fatigue: ~80%; energy conservation + scheduled rest + cooling + graded exercise + amantadine / modafinil / methylphenidate (limited evidence — REFAMS trial).
  • Spasticity: baclofen, tizanidine, gabapentin; botulinum toxin focal; intrathecal baclofen for severe (Module 11 cross-ref).
  • Cognitive rehabilitation: attention + memory + executive function strategies; modafinil for cognitive fatigue; computerised cognitive training.
  • Balance and gait: physiotherapy; aquatic exercise (cooling benefit); AFO if foot drop.
  • Bladder: anticholinergics (oxybutynin, solifenacin); intermittent self-catheterisation; mirabegron; botulinum toxin to detrusor.
  • Bowel: laxatives, scheduled programme.
  • Sexual dysfunction: PDE5 inhibitors, counselling, vibrators / fertility specialists.
  • Mood: SSRI / SNRI for depression (Module 24).
  • Multidisciplinary MS rehabilitation in dedicated programmes shows benefit.
  • Cross-ref Module 8.

9. Parkinson disease and movement disorder rehabilitation

  • LSVT BIG: amplitude-focused movement therapy; 16 sessions over 4 weeks; improves bradykinesia and posture.
  • LSVT LOUD: amplitude-focused speech therapy for PD hypophonia; improves loudness and intelligibility.
  • Cueing strategies: visual (lines on floor), auditory (metronome, music), tactile — overcome freezing of gait.
  • Treadmill training: improves gait speed.
  • Boxing programmes (Rock Steady Boxing): community-based; popular; positive anecdotal but limited high-quality evidence.
  • Tai chi: improves balance (Li NEJM 2012).
  • Dance therapy (Argentine tango): balance + gait.
  • Fall prevention: comprehensive falls assessment; home environment; multidisciplinary; DBS programming optimisation; cross-ref Module 6.
  • DBS programming and rehabilitation: rehabilitation continues after DBS to optimise function with new motor windows.
  • Huntington disease rehabilitation (Module 6): exercise + speech + dysphagia management + psychosocial.

10. Neuromuscular disease rehabilitation

  • GBS (Module 9): gradual physiotherapy as strength returns; avoid over-exertion in acute phase; rehabilitation hospital admission for moderate-severe; cardiac monitoring during acute autonomic instability; nutritional support; psychological — long course; vocational rehabilitation.
  • Myasthenia gravis (Module 9): energy conservation; activity scheduling around fatigability; medication timing; avoid contraindicated drugs (Module 9, 20).
  • ALS: multidisciplinary clinics improve survival + quality of life; assistive equipment (AAC for communication; gastrostomy for nutrition; NIV for respiratory function); palliative care integration early.
  • Muscular dystrophies: stretching to prevent contractures; AFOs; pulmonary care; nutritional support; cardiac surveillance; multidisciplinary clinics.
  • SMA (Module 16): modern era with nusinersen + onasemnogene + risdiplam transforming rehabilitation outcomes; pre-symptomatic treatment via newborn screening.
  • Chronic neuropathies: orthoses, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, pain management.

11. Spasticity management

Definition and assessment

Velocity-dependent increase in muscle tone with exaggerated stretch reflex, a positive feature of the upper motor neuron syndrome. Assess with Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) 0–4 or Tardieu Scale (which captures velocity).

Treatment ladder

  1. Identify and remove triggers: pain, infection (UTI), constipation, pressure injury, ill-fitting equipment, mood disturbance.
  2. Physiotherapy: stretching, positioning, splinting, strengthening of antagonists, functional task training.
  3. Oral antispastics for diffuse spasticity:
    • Baclofen: GABA-B agonist; 5 mg TID titrated to 80 mg/day; sedation; avoid abrupt withdrawal (seizures, hyperthermia).
    • Tizanidine: α2 agonist; hypotension; hepatotoxicity (monitor LFTs).
    • Gabapentin / pregabalin: useful especially when neuropathic pain coexists.
    • Diazepam: short-term; sedation.
    • Dantrolene: peripheral; hepatotoxicity; useful in MS, cerebral palsy.
  4. Focal interventions:
    • Botulinum toxin A (BoNT-A) injection: cosmetic + functional; effect 2–4 weeks; lasts 3 months; repeat. Selected muscles (e.g., gastrocnemius for equinovarus foot; finger flexors for clenched fist; hip adductors).
    • Phenol or alcohol nerve block.
  5. Intrathecal baclofen pump: for severe diffuse spasticity not controlled by oral agents (cerebral palsy, MS, SCI). Tightly controlled programmable infusion; complications include catheter problems and abrupt withdrawal (hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, mortality).
  6. Surgical options: selective dorsal rhizotomy (paediatric cerebral palsy); tendon lengthening / transfer; nerve transfer.

12. Dysphagia rehabilitation

(Detail in Section 5 stroke rehab.) Apply principles across stroke, TBI, MS, PD, MND, dementia: bedside screening + formal VFSS/FEES + IDDSI diet + compensatory and rehabilitative techniques + multidisciplinary input. Persistent severe dysphagia → PEG decision in shared discussion with patient + family + palliative care framework where appropriate (especially advanced dementia and end-stage MND).

13. Technology in neurorehabilitation

  • Functional electrical stimulation (FES): foot drop devices; upper limb cycling; cycling for SCI.
  • Robot-assisted training: Lokomat (gait); InMotion / Armeo (upper limb) — similar gains to dose-matched conventional therapy.
  • Exoskeletons: ReWalk, Ekso, EksoNR — selected SCI walking; high cost; selected indications; emerging consumer markets.
  • Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs): motor + communication; implanted electrodes (Neuralink, Synchron, BrainGate); rapidly evolving; selected ALS / paralysis.
  • Non-invasive brain stimulation: rTMS for depression, aphasia, motor recovery; tDCS for motor + cognitive — adjuncts to rehabilitation.
  • Virtual reality: motor retraining, neglect rehabilitation, exposure therapy.
  • Telerehabilitation: video-delivered therapy; effective in selected populations; expanded since COVID-19; cost-effective and access-improving.
  • Wearable sensors and apps: monitor activity; remote monitoring; gamification.
  • Epidural stimulation (Courtine and colleagues): voluntary motor recovery in chronic SCI; selected centres; promising but invasive.

14. Return to work, driving, community reintegration

  • Return to work: vocational rehabilitation specialist; graded return; workplace assessment; reasonable adjustments under disability legislation; job coaching.
  • Driving assessment: post-stroke / TBI / first seizure (Module 3) / advanced dementia (Module 7) / advanced PD (Module 6) — clinical assessment + on-road assessment when needed; reporting obligations vary by country.
  • Home assessment + modifications: ramps, grab rails, wet rooms, lighting, kitchen adaptations.
  • Community reintegration: social, recreation, leisure, peer support groups.
  • Carer education and support: respite care; psychological support; financial planning; advance care planning.
  • Disability benefits: country-specific; social work guidance.

15. Palliative neurorehabilitation

For progressive incurable neurological disease (ALS, end-stage MS, advanced PD, primary brain tumour, advanced dementia): rehabilitation goals shift from restoration → adaptation → maintenance → comfort. Multidisciplinary approach combining symptomatic management + assistive equipment + family support + advance care planning + hospice involvement when appropriate.

16. When to refer

  • Stroke unit + acute rehabilitation: every patient with disabling stroke benefits.
  • Inpatient rehabilitation: moderate-severe disability, multidisciplinary need, expected progress.
  • Specialist outpatient programmes: PD (LSVT clinics), MS, SCI, TBI, neuromuscular.
  • Specialised spasticity / botulinum toxin clinics: focal spasticity, intrathecal baclofen candidates.
  • Vocational rehabilitation: return-to-work planning.
  • Driving assessment: after major neurological event.
  • Palliative care: in progressive incurable disease.
  • Always involve: physiotherapy + OT + SLT + psychology + rehab medicine + neurology + social work + community services + family.
