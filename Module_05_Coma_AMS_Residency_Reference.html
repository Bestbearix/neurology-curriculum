1. The spectrum of consciousness & its anatomy

Consciousness has two components: arousal/wakefulness (driven by the ascending reticular activating system of the upper brainstem and thalamus) and awareness/content (the cortex and its connections). Coma — unarousable unresponsiveness with eyes closed — therefore requires either diffuse bilateral hemispheric dysfunction or a lesion of the brainstem ARAS (or both). A unilateral hemispheric lesion does not cause coma unless it compresses the brainstem. This anatomy is the engine of localization: it tells you a comatose patient has a structural brainstem/bihemispheric process or a global metabolic one.

Do not mistake locked-in syndrome for coma. A ventral pontine lesion (basilar thrombosis) leaves the patient conscious with preserved vertical eye movements and blinking — always test vertical gaze and ask the patient to blink to command.

2. The comatose patient: resuscitate while you localize

Coma is a "treat-and-diagnose-simultaneously" emergency. Stabilize airway/breathing/circulation, then move through reversible causes in parallel with the neurologic exam. A pragmatic stepwise strategy integrates clinical examination, EEG, neuroimaging, and laboratory findings with iterative reassessment.

  1. ABCs and immobilize the cervical spine if trauma is possible.
  2. Fingerstick glucose; give IV thiamine before/with glucose and dextrose if hypoglycemic.
  3. Naloxone for suspected opioid toxidrome; consider other antidotes by toxidrome.
  4. Draw labs: glucose, electrolytes, calcium, renal/hepatic function, ammonia, ABG, osmolality/osmolar gap, toxicology, TSH, cortisol, cultures.
  5. Neuro exam (below) to localize and to grade depth; non-contrast CT head early.
  6. LP if meningitis/encephalitis or SAH suspected (after imaging as needed); EEG to detect nonconvulsive status epilepticus.
  7. Iterate — re-examine as data return and as treatment is given.
Thiamine is a high-value reflex. Wernicke encephalopathy is not limited to alcohol misuse (malnutrition, bariatric surgery, hyperemesis, malignancy); serum levels and MRI are insensitive early, so give empiric parenteral thiamine to any patient with unexplained altered mental status.

3. Coma examination — GCS, FOUR, brainstem reflexes

Quantify depth and localize the lesion. The Glasgow Coma Scale (eye/verbal/motor, 3–15) is the common language but cannot be scored fully in an intubated patient and omits brainstem reflexes. The FOUR score (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness; eye, motor, brainstem, respiration, each 0–4) adds brainstem and respiratory assessment and works in intubated patients.

Motor responses also localize: purposeful localization implies relatively preserved cortex; decorticate (flexor) posturing suggests above the red nucleus; decerebrate (extensor) suggests midbrain/upper pons; flaccidity suggests deep/pontomedullary dysfunction or metabolic depression.

4. Structural vs toxic-metabolic; herniation

The single most useful bedside dichotomy: structural coma (focal or asymmetric findings, asymmetric or fixed pupils, gaze deviation, posturing suggestive of a level) versus toxic-metabolic coma (symmetric findings, preserved and reactive pupils even when deeply unresponsive, tremor/asterixis/myoclonus, often a gradual course). Preserved pupillary light reflexes in a deeply comatose patient strongly favor a metabolic cause (opioids give pinpoint reactive pupils; anticholinergics dilate).

The blown pupil is an emergency. A new fixed dilated pupil with declining consciousness signals uncal herniation — urgent osmotherapy (hypertonic saline/mannitol), head-of-bed elevation, brief hyperventilation as a bridge, and immediate neurosurgical consultation.

5. Disorders of consciousness & cognitive-motor dissociation

Among survivors of severe brain injury, distinguishing the vegetative/unresponsive-wakefulness state from the minimally conscious state matters for prognosis and care decisions, and behavioral scales alone misclassify a meaningful minority. Cognitive-motor dissociation (CMD) — also called covert consciousness — describes patients with no behavioral response who nonetheless reliably follow commands detectable on task-based fMRI or EEG (e.g., "imagine playing tennis"). The resource set surveys multimodal assessment (fMRI, EEG meta-state dynamics, effective connectivity, heartbeat-evoked responses) and emerging interventions (e.g., vagus nerve stimulation) aimed at detecting and promoting recovery of consciousness.

Prognostic humility. Because covert consciousness exists, avoid over-confident early prognostication in disorders of consciousness; multimodal assessment and serial re-evaluation outperform a single bedside behavioral exam.

6. Post-cardiac-arrest care & neuroprognostication

After return of spontaneous circulation in a patient who remains comatose, targeted temperature management and multimodal prognostication are central.

HACA (NEJM 2002). In comatose survivors of out-of-hospital VF arrest, mild therapeutic hypothermia (32–34 °C for 24 h) improved the rate of favorable neurologic outcome versus normothermia — establishing temperature management after arrest.
TTM trial (NEJM 2013). Targeting 33 °C vs 36 °C made no difference in mortality (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.89–1.28) or neurologic outcome — shifting practice toward targeted temperature management with strict fever avoidance rather than deep hypothermia per se.
Neuroprognostication is multimodal and delayed. Avoid early nihilism (and confounding by sedation/hypothermia). Combine the clinical exam (pupillary/corneal reflexes, myoclonus), EEG (very-early EEG patterns and burst-suppression predict poor outcome), somatosensory evoked potentials (bilateral N20 loss), neuron-specific enolase, and MRI — no single test should decide.

7. Brain death / death by neurologic criteria (2023 consensus)

The 2023 consensus practice guideline harmonizes adult and pediatric "brain death / death by neurologic criteria" (BD/DNC) into a single framework: irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. Determination is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis.

Prerequisites

  • An established, irreversible cause (catastrophic brain injury) consistent with BD/DNC.
  • Exclude confounders: correct hypotension, hypothermia, severe metabolic/endocrine disturbance, and clear CNS-depressant drugs/neuromuscular blockade; observe an adequate interval (special guidance after targeted temperature management).

Clinical examination

  • Coma with no responsiveness; absent brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic/oculovestibular, gag, cough); no motor response to noxious stimuli (spinal reflexes may persist and do not exclude BD/DNC).
  • Apnea test: pre-oxygenate and normalize ABG (PaCO₂ 35–45, pH 7.35–7.45), disconnect the ventilator with oxygen delivery, and confirm no respiratory effort with a target PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mm Hg (or ≥ 20 mm Hg above baseline).

Ancillary testing

Required only when components of the clinical exam or apnea test cannot be completed or are confounded (e.g., high cervical injury, severe facial trauma, ECMO). Options include cerebral blood-flow studies and electrophysiology; the guideline addresses special situations (ECMO, targeted temperature management, primary infratentorial injury).

Know your local policy. The provided literature documents persistent variability in institutional brain-death policies and a transatlantic conceptual divide; the 2023 guideline aims to reduce this, but you must follow your institution's protocol and legal requirements, including number of examiners and observation periods.

8. Delirium & the toxic-metabolic encephalopathies

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness — the most common cause of altered mental status in hospitalized and critically ill patients. Screen with validated tools (CAM-ICU/ICDSC), and prioritize non-pharmacologic prevention: the provided ICU meta-review supports multicomponent bundles (early mobilization, sleep, orientation, sensory aids, sedation minimization — the ABCDEF bundle) over routine antipsychotics, which have limited efficacy for prevention/treatment.
