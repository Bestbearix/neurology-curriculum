1. The demyelinating/neuroinflammatory framework

CNS inflammatory demyelinating disease is no longer "MS and variants" — it is a set of immunologically distinct entities with different antibodies, pathology, prognosis, and (critically) different treatments. The three pillars are multiple sclerosis, AQP4-IgG-seropositive NMOSD, and MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), with ADEM as a monophasic (usually paediatric) post-infectious form. The provided pathology update reinforces that these differ at the tissue level, not merely clinically.

Why the distinction is urgent. Several MS disease-modifying therapies (notably interferon-β and natalizumab, and other MS agents) can worsen NMOSD. Antibody status must be established before committing to long-term therapy in anyone with an atypical demyelinating presentation.

2. Multiple sclerosis: diagnosis & the 2024 McDonald criteria

MS diagnosis rests on demonstrating dissemination in space (DIS) and dissemination in time (DIT) while excluding better explanations. Phenotypes: relapsing-remitting (most common at onset), secondary progressive, and primary progressive; the related radiologically/clinically isolated syndromes sit at the edge of the definition.

The 2024 McDonald revisions (provided). Major changes designed to enable earlier diagnosis: (1) the optic nerve is recognised as a fifth DIS location (alongside periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord); (2) DIT is no longer required in selected circumstances; and (3) new paraclinical markers are incorporated — the MRI central vein sign (CVS) and paramagnetic rim lesions (PRL), plus the CSF kappa free light chain index as an alternative to CSF-specific oligoclonal bands.
Real-world performance. In a multicentre Korean cohort of 306 patients, applying the 2024 criteria at onset diagnosed MS earlier and in more people than the 2017 criteria — a difference driven mainly by adding optic-nerve involvement as a DIS site (an additional ~6.7% of participants in the compared cohorts).
The overdiagnosis counter-argument (provided). Commentaries raise substantive concerns that relaxing DIT and broadening paraclinical substitution risks reduced specificity, confirmation bias, and misdiagnosis — particularly when the criteria are applied to people with nonspecific symptoms or incidental white-matter findings / radiologically isolated syndrome. The criteria are validated for patients with a typical demyelinating presentation; applying them to nonspecific headache or incidental MRI findings is misuse.
Red flags against MS (prompting an alternative diagnosis): normal MRI, absent oligoclonal bands with an atypical course, longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis, severe bilateral optic neuritis, area postrema syndrome, prominent systemic features, meningeal enhancement, or progressive myelopathy without dissemination.

3. MRI standards & monitoring

MRI is central to diagnosis, to excluding mimics, and to monitoring. The provided German Society of Neuroradiology expert consensus sets out standardised protocols for diagnosis and monitoring: consistent sequences and slice geometry, appropriate gadolinium use, and reproducible follow-up intervals so that new/enlarging T2 lesions can be reliably detected. Typical MS lesions are ovoid, periventricular (Dawson fingers), juxtacortical/cortical, infratentorial, and short-segment in the cord. The central vein sign and paramagnetic rim lesions improve specificity against mimics and now feature in the 2024 criteria.

4. MS therapy: relapses & disease-modifying therapy

Acute relapse

Treat disabling relapses with high-dose corticosteroids (e.g., IV methylprednisolone, or bioequivalent oral); steroids speed recovery without altering long-term disability. Use plasma exchange for severe steroid-refractory attacks. Always exclude a pseudo-relapse from infection or fever (Uhthoff phenomenon) before escalating.

Disease-modifying therapy (DMT)

DMTs reduce relapses, new MRI lesions, and disability accrual in relapsing MS. Broadly they are stratified by efficacy and risk:

TierExamplesKey monitoring / risks
Modest/moderate efficacyInterferon-β, glatiramer acetate, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarateFlu-like/injection reactions; LFTs; lymphopenia (fumarates, PML risk if severe); teriflunomide teratogenicity
Higher efficacyS1P modulators (fingolimod, siponimod), cladribineFirst-dose bradycardia, macular oedema, infections; rebound after S1P cessation; lymphocyte monitoring
High efficacy (monoclonals)Natalizumab, anti-CD20 (ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, rituximab), alemtuzumabNatalizumab: PML risk (JCV serostatus, duration, prior immunosuppression); anti-CD20 hypogammaglobulinaemia/infections and blunted vaccine responses; alemtuzumab secondary autoimmunity

Ocrelizumab is also used in primary progressive MS. Modern practice increasingly favours early high-efficacy therapy in patients with active disease and poor-prognosis features, balanced against infection and pregnancy planning. Symptomatic care matters as much as DMT: spasticity, fatigue, bladder dysfunction, neuropathic pain, mood, and rehabilitation (cross-links to Module 25).

Before starting any DMT: confirm the diagnosis (not a mimic), check antibody status if atypical, screen for hepatitis B/latent TB/JCV as indicated, update vaccinations, and plan for pregnancy — several agents are contraindicated or need washout.

5. NMOSD (AQP4-IgG) & its biologics

NMOSD is an astrocytopathy driven by AQP4-IgG with complement-mediated injury. Core clinical syndromes: optic neuritis (often severe/bilateral), longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (≥3 vertebral segments), area postrema syndrome (intractable hiccups/nausea/vomiting), acute brainstem, narcolepsy/diencephalic, and cerebral syndromes. Attacks are severe and disability is attack-related — so relapse prevention is the whole strategy. Test with a cell-based assay for AQP4-IgG.

Approved biologics (provided review). Randomised trials support complement inhibitors (eculizumab, ravulizumab), anti-IL-6R (satralizumab; tocilizumab), and anti-CD19 B-cell depletion (inebilizumab), alongside long-standing rituximab and older agents (azathioprine, mycophenolate). Collectively these reduce relapse risk substantially (on the order of 60–90% versus placebo/control in the trial programmes).
Complement inhibitors require meningococcal vaccination (and often antibiotic prophylaxis) because of encapsulated-organism risk. And again — do not treat NMOSD with MS drugs: interferon-β, natalizumab and some others can precipitate severe attacks.

6. MOGAD & the 2023 criteria

MOG antibody-associated disease is a distinct demyelinating disorder: relapsing or monophasic, presenting with optic neuritis (frequently bilateral, with marked disc oedema and good steroid response), myelitis (often longitudinally extensive, conus involvement), ADEM-like presentations (especially in children), and cortical encephalitis with seizures. Attacks are typically steroid-responsive but relapse on steroid withdrawal.

MOG-IgG positivity does not equal MOGAD (provided). Applying the 2023 international MOGAD panel criteria at a referral centre, a real-world audit found that of patients labelled MOGAD, only ~68% actually met criteria on re-evaluation — misapplication driven by testing without a compatible phenotype and by over-reading low-positive titres. The criteria require a compatible clinical syndrome plus a clear-positive cell-based-assay titre (or supporting features when low-positive), and exclusion of better diagnoses.
Test selectively. Order MOG-IgG (and AQP4-IgG) when the phenotype fits — not reflexively on every white-matter abnormality — and interpret low titres with scepticism. The provided paediatric comparison highlights immunopathological and clinical differences between MOGAD and AQP4-IgG NMOSD in children.

7. ADEM & paediatric considerations

Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis is a typically monophasic, post-infectious/post-vaccination demyelinating illness of childhood presenting with encephalopathy plus multifocal deficits and large, poorly demarcated T2 lesions. Encephalopathy is the discriminator from a first MS attack. Many paediatric ADEM cases are MOG-IgG positive. Treat with high-dose corticosteroids, with IVIG or plasma exchange for refractory cases; most recover well, but follow for relapse (which would prompt reconsideration as MOGAD or MS).

8. Autoimmune encephalitis & antibody syndromes

Antibody-mediated CNS autoimmunity is a major residency competency and heavily represented in this resource set. A pragmatic approach: recognise the syndrome, test serum and CSF (CSF is essential for NMDA-R), image and EEG, search for a tumour, and treat early and empirically when criteria for possible/probable autoimmune encephalitis are met (cross-links to Module 7 and to autoimmune epilepsy in Module 3).

Treatment & prognosis in NMDA-R encephalitis (provided cohort). Management is first-line immunotherapy (corticosteroids, IVIG, plasma exchange) plus tumour removal, escalating to second-line agents (rituximab, cyclophosphamide) in non-responders. Early treatment and tumour removal predict better long-term outcome, and many patients improve slowly over months — recovery can continue well beyond the acute illness.
Post-HSV autoimmune encephalitis. New movement disorder, psychiatric change, or deterioration weeks after herpes simplex encephalitis — with negative repeat HSV PCR — should prompt testing for NMDA-R antibodies and immunotherapy, not more acyclovir.

MRI patterns (provided review) support but never exclude the diagnosis: medial temporal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in limbic encephalitis, and frequently normal MRI in NMDA-R encephalitis. EEG may show extreme delta brush (NMDA-R). Predictive models exist for autoimmune epilepsy (e.g., antibody-prevalence scoring) to select patients for antibody testing and immunotherapy trials.

9. Paraneoplastic disease, CNS vasculitis & neurosarcoidosis

Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes

PNS-CARE criteria (2021; provided validation). Diagnosis combines the clinical phenotype (high- vs intermediate-risk), the antibody (high-risk onconeural such as Hu, Yo, CRMP5, Ma2 vs lower-risk), and the presence of cancer, yielding a PNS-CARE score; ≥6 corresponds to probable/definite PNS. In the provided real-world cohort the score performed well, though some patients with clinically convincing PNS scored ≤5 — clinical judgement still matters. Classic phenotypes include rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome, limbic encephalitis, opsoclonus-myoclonus, and sensory neuronopathy.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are an increasingly common cause of antibody-positive paraneoplastic and autoimmune neurologic complications — a rapidly growing differential in oncology patients that requires prompt recognition, drug cessation, and immunosuppression.

Primary CNS vasculitis

A heterogeneous disorder presenting with headache, cognitive change, strokes in multiple territories, and seizures. Angiography can be normal in small-vessel disease; brain and leptomeningeal biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard. Distinguish from reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (thunderclap headache, reversible "string-of-beads") — treatment is diametrically different (immunosuppression vs vasospasm management).

Neurosarcoidosis

Sarcoidosis affects the nervous system in a minority of patients but with protean presentations: cranial neuropathies (especially facial nerve), basal leptomeningeal enhancement, hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction, myelopathy, and peripheral neuropathy/myopathy. Support the diagnosis with systemic evidence (chest imaging, FDG-PET) and histology where feasible; treat with corticosteroids, escalating to steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, mycophenolate) and TNF-α inhibitors (infliximab) in refractory disease.

The neuroimmunology habit: in any atypical inflammatory CNS presentation, ask three questions — Is there an antibody? Is there a tumour? Is there a systemic disease? They restructure the entire workup.
