Why this module?

Many neurological diseases present first as psychiatric syndromes. Anti-NMDA-R encephalitis opens with psychosis; Huntington disease opens with mood disturbance and irritability; frontotemporal dementia opens with disinhibition and apathy; Wilson disease in a young patient opens with personality change. A primary psychiatric diagnosis made too quickly can postpone treatable diagnoses by years. The general physician's job is to keep an open differential, particularly in atypical presentations.

After this module you will:

  • Run a structured workup for new psychiatric presentations to exclude treatable neurological / medical mimics.
  • Recognise anti-NMDA-R encephalitis and other autoimmune encephalitides at the bedside (Graus 2016 criteria).
  • Identify catatonia using Bush-Francis features and use the lorazepam challenge as both diagnostic and therapeutic.
  • Differentiate post-stroke depression, PD depression, MS depression, and TBI mood disturbances from primary psychiatric illness.
  • Recognise neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, tardive dyskinesia, and the VMAT2 inhibitor option.
  • Apply the FDA boxed warning for antipsychotics in elderly with dementia-related psychosis; choose appropriate alternatives.
  • Manage BPSD in dementia with non-pharmacological strategies first; use brexpiprazole (FDA 2023) or careful low-dose alternatives when needed.
The single principle: a new psychiatric syndrome with any of the following demands a neurological / medical workup before settling on a primary psychiatric diagnosis — age > 40 at first presentation; atypical features (movement disorder, autonomic instability, seizures, focal neurology, cognitive decline); rapid onset; refractoriness to standard psychiatric treatment; family history of neurological disease.

2. Autoimmune encephalitis — anti-NMDA-R and beyond
  • Anti-NMDA-R: most common autoimmune encephalitis; young women predominantly; teratoma in 50% aged 18-45. Course: viral prodrome → psychiatric (mood + psychosis with bizarre delusions) → seizures → orofacial dyskinesias → autonomic instability → coma.
  • Workup: CSF anti-NMDA-R antibody + EEG extreme delta brush in adults + pelvic / testicular imaging.
  • Treatment: IV methylprednisolone + IVIG / PLEX + tumour removal; second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide; ICU for autonomic instability; recovery slow (months) but ~80% good outcome.

3. Catatonia recognition and management

Bush-Francis common features: stupor + mutism + negativism + posturing/catalepsy + waxy flexibility + stereotypy + mannerisms + echolalia / echopraxia + excitement (excited catatonia) + autonomic instability (malignant catatonia).

Management:
  • Lorazepam challenge 1–2 mg IV/IM; dramatic improvement within minutes → diagnostic + therapeutic. Continue 3–24 mg/day scheduled.
  • Address underlying cause: rule out anti-NMDA-R encephalitis in young woman, other autoimmune, metabolic, infectious, hepatic, hyponatraemia, drug-induced.
  • ECT for lorazepam-refractory or malignant catatonia (mortality up to 20% untreated).
  • AVOID antipsychotics — they can worsen catatonia and precipitate NMS.

5. Huntington, Wilson, FTD, other movement disorders
  • Huntington: psychiatric symptoms can precede motor by years — irritability, depression, apathy, OCD, psychosis, high suicide risk. Treat depression (SSRI); psychosis (tiapride / olanzapine); avoid haloperidol long-term (TD risk).
  • Wilson disease (Module 13): psychiatric prodrome in young; KF rings; trientine + zinc.
  • Tourette + tics: comorbid OCD + ADHD; CBIT first-line; α2 agonists; dopamine antagonists for severe.
  • FTD (Module 7): bvFTD disinhibition + apathy + change in eating + loss of empathy — frequently misdiagnosed as primary psychiatric.
6. Dementia BPSD & sundowning

BPSD in > 90% of dementia at some point. Drive caregiver burden + institutionalisation + mortality.

Non-pharmacological first

  • Identify causes: pain + infection (UTI common) + medication side effects + sensory deficits + dehydration + electrolytes + sleep + environmental triggers.
  • Structured daily routine; physical activity; music + reminiscence; bright light + dark night for sundowning; calm low-stimulus environment; caregiver education.

Pharmacological when necessary

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors for cognition; may modestly improve BPSD; first-line in DLB.
  • SSRIs for depression / anxiety (sertraline, citalopram — QT caution).
  • Antipsychotics: brexpiprazole (FDA-approved 2023 for AD agitation); risperidone elsewhere but boxed warning; AVOID typicals in elderly dementia.
  • DLB neuroleptic sensitivity: even single dose can cause severe parkinsonism + NMS; use quetiapine or clozapine cautiously.
  • AVOID benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, opioids unless necessary.
FDA black box warning for all antipsychotics in elderly with dementia-related psychosis: increased mortality (CV + infection). Lowest dose, shortest duration, regular review.
7. Post-stroke, MS, epilepsy, TBI neuropsychiatry
  • Post-stroke depression ~30%; SSRI + CBT. Mania (rare, right hemisphere lesions; mood stabiliser). Psychosis (rare; right hemisphere; quetiapine cautiously). Anxiety + PTSD + post-stroke fatigue + pseudobulbar affect → dextromethorphan-quinidine.
  • MS depression ~50% lifetime; suicide risk increased; SSRI/SNRI + CBT. Fatigue 80%; amantadine, modafinil. Cognitive impairment 40-60% subcortical. Pseudobulbar affect.
  • Epilepsy depression ~2× general; SSRIs safe; AVOID bupropion, tramadol, maprotiline (lower seizure threshold). LEV "Keppra rage" 10%. Postictal psychosis 24-72h after cluster + lucid interval. Interictal psychosis (temporal lobe). PNES Module 21.
  • TBI: ~50% psychiatric in first year. Depression + anxiety + PTSD + irritability + impulsivity + executive dysfunction. Post-concussion: CBT + gradual return to activity. AVOID benzodiazepines + typical antipsychotics. CTE in repeated head injury.

9. ECT, DBS, suicide risk
  • ECT: severe depression + psychosis + suicide risk; catatonia after lorazepam trial; mania; refractory NMS; status epilepticus refractory; PD selected for severe depression / motor. Side effects: transient post-ictal confusion + autobiographical memory loss around treatment period.
  • DBS in psychiatric disease: OCD (NAc / ALIC for refractory); MDD investigational (subcallosal cingulate, ventral capsule); Tourette (CM thalamus / GPi). Established neurological: PD (STN, GPi), ET (VIM), dystonia (GPi), epilepsy.
  • Suicide risk: increased in HD, ALS, MS, epilepsy, stroke, TBI, PD, chronic pain. Screen routinely; safety plan; family involvement; treat depression aggressively; ECT in severe / refractory; address access to means.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 22-year-old woman presents with 2 weeks of psychosis, bizarre delusions, and now seizures + orofacial dyskinesias + autonomic instability. CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis. EEG: extreme delta brush. Pelvic US: 4 cm ovarian mass

Diagnosis: Anti-NMDA-R encephalitis with ovarian teratoma.

Plan:

  1. First-line immunotherapy: IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 5 days + IVIG or plasma exchange.
  2. Surgical removal of ovarian teratoma — early tumour removal dramatically improves outcome.
  3. ICU monitoring for autonomic instability (BP swings, arrhythmias, hypoventilation).
  4. Antiseizure medication (LEV preferred).
  5. If inadequate response by 2 weeks: second-line rituximab + cyclophosphamide.
  6. Slow recovery (months); ~80% good outcome with adequate treatment.
  7. AVOID antipsychotics where possible (can worsen autonomic + extrapyramidal features).
  8. Counsel family about long course; psychological support after recovery (PTSD common).
  9. Cross-ref Module 12 (paraneoplastic).
Case 2 — A 70-year-old has been on long-term haloperidol. He develops involuntary tongue and oral movements that persist despite dose reduction. He has Alzheimer disease and mild parkinsonism

Diagnosis: Tardive dyskinesia.

Plan:

  1. Stop offending antipsychotic if possible; switch to clozapine if antipsychotic still needed (lowest TD risk; also for AD agitation but boxed warning for elderly dementia mortality).
  2. VMAT2 inhibitor: valbenazine (KINECT-3) or deutetrabenazine (AIM-TD) — FDA 2017; modestly effective; expensive.
  3. Avoid anticholinergics (worsen TD).
  4. Counsel patient + family: TD may persist after drug withdrawal; long-term management.
  5. Consider non-pharmacological strategies for BPSD (Module 7).
  6. AD agitation: brexpiprazole FDA-approved 2023 — alternative to clozapine; risperidone elsewhere with boxed warning consideration.
Case 3 — A 45-year-old with Parkinson disease develops visual hallucinations and paranoid delusions. Dopaminergic dose has been escalated for motor symptoms

Diagnosis: Parkinson disease psychosis.

Plan:

  1. Review and reduce dopaminergic medication where possible (especially dopamine agonists if any; can also reduce anticholinergic + amantadine which contribute).
  2. Rule out infection (UTI common) and metabolic causes; treat any precipitant.
  3. If antipsychotic needed: pimavanserin (5-HT2A inverse agonist, FDA 2016 for PD psychosis — does not worsen parkinsonism) or clozapine (lowest EPS but blood monitoring); quetiapine commonly used (weaker evidence).
  4. AVOID typical antipsychotics + risperidone + olanzapine + aripiprazole — worsen parkinsonism.
  5. Cholinesterase inhibitor (rivastigmine) may help if PD dementia present.
  6. Multidisciplinary movement disorders + neuropsychiatry input.
Case 4 — A 28-year-old woman with new severe depression and bizarre delusions becomes stuporous, mute, with waxy flexibility and bilateral fixed posturing for 24 hours. Antipsychotic given by ED produced no improvement

Bush-Francis features: stupor + mutism + posturing + waxy flexibility + negativism — > 5 features. Mild autonomic instability.

Diagnosis: Catatonia (likely malignant catatonia).

Plan:

  1. Stop antipsychotic (can worsen catatonia and precipitate NMS).
  2. Lorazepam challenge: 1–2 mg IV — observe for dramatic improvement within minutes to an hour. Continue scheduled lorazepam 6–24 mg/day.
  3. Critical: rule out anti-NMDA-R encephalitis in a young woman with new catatonia — send CSF NMDA-R antibody; pelvic ultrasound; EEG; MRI; LP.
  4. Other workup: rule out NMS (CK), metabolic causes (Na, glucose, ammonia, ABG, infection, hepatic), other autoimmune encephalitis antibodies, lupus.
  5. ECT if lorazepam-refractory or malignant catatonia features worsen (mortality up to 20% untreated).
  6. Multidisciplinary: psychiatry + neurology + ICU + autoimmune neurology consultation.
  7. Treat any underlying psychiatric disorder once acute episode resolves.
Case 5 — A 56-year-old man's family report 18 months of personality change, disinhibition, change in eating preferences (now craving sweets and bingeing), loss of empathy, and minor impulsivity. He has been working but his behaviour has become embarrassing in social situations. MRI: bilateral frontal atrophy. FDG-PET: bifrontal hypometabolism

Diagnosis: Behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD).

Plan:

  1. Confirm diagnosis: detailed cognitive assessment (executive function affected); informant history is critical; rule out alternative causes — psychiatric (depression, mania), metabolic, autoimmune, structural.
  2. Differentiate from primary psychiatric illness: bvFTD often misdiagnosed as primary psychiatric — late-onset bipolar / personality disorder; INSIGHT typically reduced in bvFTD.
  3. Consider genetic testing (C9orf72, MAPT, GRN) — family history; counselling for relatives.
  4. Symptom-targeted management:
    • SSRI for disinhibition (citalopram, sertraline) — modest evidence.
    • AVOID antipsychotics where possible — bvFTD patients can have dopamine receptor sensitivity (MAPT, dopaminergic neuron involvement).
    • Trazodone for sleep / agitation.
    • Caregiver education + support is the foundation.
  5. Multidisciplinary: cognitive neurology + psychiatry + speech and language therapy + occupational therapy + social work + financial planning + driving assessment.
  6. Cross-ref Module 7.
Case 6 — A 65-year-old has Alzheimer disease and severe agitation that has not responded to non-pharmacological strategies. Care home reports increasing aggression endangering staff and other residents

Plan:

  1. Comprehensive review: rule out reversible causes — pain (treat empirically with paracetamol scheduled), infection (UTI common — urinalysis), constipation, dehydration, medication side effects, sensory deficits, sleep disturbance, environmental.
  2. Optimise non-pharmacological strategies: structured routine, music therapy, reminiscence therapy, bright light + dark night for sundowning, calm low-stimulus environment, caregiver education, scheduled physical activity, person-centred care plan.
  3. If pharmacological needed:
    • Brexpiprazole (FDA-approved 2023 for AD agitation) — alternative to off-label antipsychotics.
    • Risperidone widely used outside US; boxed warning for elderly dementia.
    • Citalopram modest evidence for AD agitation.
  4. FDA black box warning: all antipsychotics in elderly with dementia-related psychosis carry increased mortality (CV + infection); use only when necessary at lowest dose for shortest duration; review regularly; discontinue if possible.
  5. AVOID: benzodiazepines (falls, paradoxical disinhibition, delirium); anticholinergics; long-term opioids unless necessary for pain.
  6. If DLB suspected (Module 7): even single antipsychotic dose can cause severe neuroleptic sensitivity reaction with parkinsonism + autonomic + NMS; use quetiapine or clozapine cautiously if absolutely needed.
  7. Multidisciplinary: geriatric psychiatry + dementia care team + care home staff training + family meeting + advance care planning.
  8. Cross-ref Module 7.
