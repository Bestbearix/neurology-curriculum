Beyond "probably a migraine"

The general-physician Module 4 teaches recognition and red flags. This version assumes you exclude the dangerous secondary causes cold, diagnose primary headaches by ICHD-3 criteria, and construct acute and preventive regimens across the modern CGRP era.

By the end you should be able to:
  1. Apply SNOOP to decide who needs imaging/workup.
  2. Diagnose migraine, cluster, and tension-type headache by ICHD-3 criteria.
  3. Explain migraine's trigeminovascular/CGRP pathophysiology and use it to choose therapy.
  4. Build acute regimens (triptans, gepants, ditans) with the right contraindications.
  5. Select prevention including anti-CGRP mAbs, onabotulinumtoxinA, and topiramate — and recognize/treat medication-overuse headache.
Educational use only. Verify drugs, doses, and contraindications against current guidelines and product labeling at the point of care.

ICHD-3 & migraine criteria

ICHD-3 divides disorders into primary (migraine, tension-type, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, other), secondary (attributed to another disorder), and painful cranial neuropathies. Diagnosis is operational — count attacks and features.

DiagnosisKey ICHD-3 criteria
Migraine without aura≥5 attacks, 4–72 h; ≥2 of (unilateral, pulsating, moderate–severe, worse with activity) + (nausea/vomiting OR photo- & phonophobia)
Migraine with auraReversible aura (visual/sensory/speech/motor/brainstem/retinal), each 5–60 min, ± headache within 60 min
Chronic migraine≥15 headache days/month >3 months, ≥8 with migraine features
Tension-typeBilateral, pressing/tightening, mild–moderate, not worsened by routine activity, no migrainous features
Cluster (TAC)Severe unilateral orbital pain 15–180 min, up to 8/day, ipsilateral autonomic features + restlessness

Pathophysiology (why CGRP matters)

Migraine is a disorder of brain excitability: aura reflects cortical spreading depression; pain arises from trigeminovascular activation with CGRP release and neurogenic inflammation, plus central sensitization (allodynia). This is the target of gepants and anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies.

Acute migraine treatment

Treat early, adequate dose, cap frequency to prevent MOH.

ClassExamplesNotes
NSAIDs / analgesicsNaproxen, ibuprofen, aspirinMild–moderate
Triptans (5-HT₁B/₁D)Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptanMainstay; avoid in coronary/cerebrovascular disease
GepantsUbrogepant, rimegepantNo vasoconstriction — use when triptans contraindicated/ineffective
Ditans (5-HT₁F)LasmiditanNo vasoconstriction; sedation/driving caution
Ubrogepant (ACHIEVE). Oral CGRP-receptor antagonist; met co-primary 2-hour pain freedom and most-bothersome-symptom freedom vs placebo — acute relief without triptan vasoconstriction.
Rimegepant. Gepant effective for both acute (75 mg) and preventive (every other day) use; provided data support combining with anti-CGRP mAbs in selected patients.

Preventive treatment

Offer when attacks are frequent/disabling or acute therapy is contraindicated/overused. Success ≈ 50% reduction; trial oral agents ~8 weeks at target dose.

OptionNotes
TopiramateEvidence-based (Cochrane); cognitive effects, paresthesia, weight loss, teratogenic
Beta-blockers, candesartan, amitriptyline, valproateChoose by comorbidity; avoid valproate in pregnancy
Anti-CGRP mAbsErenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab
OnabotulinumtoxinAChronic migraine only (PREEMPT: 155 U / 31 sites q12wk)
Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies. RCTs across episodic and chronic migraine — including FOCUS (fremanezumab in patients with 2–4 prior preventive failures) — reduce monthly migraine days with good tolerability, even in difficult-to-treat populations.
EHF 2022 guideline. Moderate-to-high evidence to recommend eptinezumab, erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab for prevention; increasingly positioned as a first-line preventive option.
OnabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT). For chronic migraine (≥15 headache days/month): 155 U across 31 fixed sites q12wk reduces headache days vs placebo. Not for episodic migraine.
Non-response ≠ class failure. Some initial non-responders convert with continued therapy or a switch between mAbs; reassess at ~3 months.

Cluster, tension-type & medication-overuse headache

Cluster headache (a TAC)

PhaseTreatment
AcuteHigh-flow 100% O₂ (12–15 L/min) + subcutaneous sumatriptan
BridgeGreater occipital nerve block; short steroid course
PreventiveVerapamil (ECG monitoring); lithium, topiramate; galcanezumab (episodic cluster)
Indomethacin-responsive paroxysmal hemicrania must be distinguished from cluster — a therapeutic and diagnostic test.

Tension-type headache

Bilateral, pressing, mild–moderate, no migrainous features. Episodic: simple analgesics. Chronic/frequent: amitriptyline first-line; address sleep, stress, posture, and analgesic frequency.

Medication-overuse headache

Headache ≥15 days/month with acute-medication overuse (triptans/ergots/opioids/combinations ≥10 days/mo; simple analgesics ≥15 days/mo) >3 months. Manage with education, withdrawal, and an effective preventive. Provided evidence: onabotulinumtoxinA and anti-CGRP mAbs help transition out of overuse.

Butalbital and opioids carry the highest MOH/chronification risk — avoid for migraine.

Case-based reasoning

Case 1. A 48-year-old with episodic unilateral throbbing headaches, nausea and photophobia, 3 days/month, wants acute therapy but has stable coronary artery disease.
Acute choice?

Migraine without aura. Triptans and ergots are contraindicated with coronary disease → use a gepant (ubrogepant or rimegepant) or lasmiditan (no vasoconstriction), plus an NSAID/antiemetic. Counsel to cap acute-medication days to avoid MOH.

Case 2. A 35-year-old man with bouts of excruciating strictly left orbital pain lasting ~1 hour, several times daily for weeks, with left tearing, nasal congestion, and pacing agitation.
Diagnosis and treatment?

Cluster headache. Acute: high-flow 100% oxygen + subcutaneous sumatriptan. Bridge with occipital nerve block/steroids; start verapamil for prevention (monitor ECG). Galcanezumab is an option for episodic cluster.

Case 3. A 40-year-old with 20 headache days/month, taking a triptan ~12 days/month and combination analgesics on others for over a year.
What's going on and what do you do?

Chronic migraine with medication-overuse headache. Educate, withdraw the overused acute medication, and start an effective preventive — onabotulinumtoxinA (PREEMPT) or an anti-CGRP mAb, both of which can help transition out of overuse. Avoid butalbital/opioids.
