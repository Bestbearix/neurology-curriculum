Why this module?

Dizziness is one of the commonest ED and clinic presentations — and one of the most over-investigated and under-diagnosed. The traditional "what kind of dizzy?" approach fails because patients describe the same experience in different ways at different times. The modern bedside approach uses timing and triggers to assign the patient to a syndrome, then performs a targeted examination — including HINTS for acute vestibular syndrome, Dix-Hallpike for positional vertigo, and orthostatic vitals for presyncope.

After this module you will:

  • Place every dizziness presentation into one of four syndromes by timing and triggers.
  • Perform and interpret HINTS for acute vestibular syndrome — and know when it does NOT apply.
  • Diagnose and treat BPPV with Dix-Hallpike, supine roll, Epley, Semont, and BBQ-roll manoeuvres.
  • Recognise Ménière disease and vestibular migraine using Bárány Society criteria.
  • Identify PPPD and lead with vestibular rehabilitation + SSRI.
  • Recognise central red flags — inability to walk, central HINTS pattern, brainstem signs, sudden hearing loss with vertigo.
  • Manage acute vestibular neuritis with short-course suppressants and early vestibular rehab.
The single principle: in acute spontaneous continuous dizziness, HINTS+ is more sensitive than early MRI DWI for posterior-circulation stroke (when done correctly by trained examiners). And in episodic dizziness with positional triggers, the bedside answer is usually BPPV — which a Dix-Hallpike followed by an Epley fixes in a single visit.

Stop asking "what kind of dizzy" — ask "how long does each episode last and what makes it happen?" That answers most of the diagnostic question before the exam begins.
2. Vestibular anatomy and the VOR
  • 3 semicircular canals (angular acceleration) + utricle (horizontal + tilt) + saccule (vertical) on each side.
  • Vestibular nerve: superior division → anterior + horizontal canals + utricle; inferior division → posterior canal + saccule.
  • Brainstem → vestibular nuclei → cerebellar flocculus/nodulus (gain), thalamus → PIVC, oculomotor nuclei (VOR), spinal cord (vestibulospinal).
  • VOR: head move → opposite eye move keeps gaze stable. Latency ~10 ms. Peripheral lesion → corrective saccade on affected side during head impulse. Central lesions usually spare the VOR.
  • Peripheral nystagmus = horizontal-torsional, unidirectional, suppressed by fixation, obeys Alexander's law (worse when looking toward fast phase).
3. HINTS exam — the AVS pathway
HINTS is for ONGOING continuous dizziness with nystagmus (AVS). It is NOT for patients who are not actively dizzy at exam, or for episodic dizziness.
TestPeripheralCentral (stroke)
Head ImpulseAbnormal (corrective saccade on affected side)Normal (intact VOR) — counter-intuitive
NystagmusUnidirectional horizontal-torsional, suppressed by fixationBidirectional / direction-changing, vertical, or torsional, not suppressed
Test of SkewNo vertical skew on alternate coverVertical skew deviation

HINTS+: + new unilateral SNHL suggests AICA stroke (>= AICA territory includes internal auditory artery).

When done correctly, HINTS+ is more sensitive than early MRI DWI (~99% vs ~80%) for posterior-circulation stroke (Kattah Stroke 2009). Performed poorly, head impulse can give false-negative "intact VOR".

4. Acute vestibular syndrome — VN vs stroke

Vestibular neuritis

  • Continuous vertigo + nausea/vomiting + gait imbalance for days; peaks within 24 h; resolves over 1–6 weeks. Post-viral inflammation (HSV-1 in some).
  • Usually superior division (spares posterior canal and saccule). Hearing preserved (vs labyrinthitis).
  • Exam: horizontal-torsional nystagmus with fast phase AWAY from affected side; suppressed by fixation; Alexander's law; abnormal head impulse on affected side; otherwise normal HINTS+.
  • Treatment: short course (≤ 3 days) vestibular suppressant (cyclizine, meclizine, dimenhydrinate) or benzodiazepine + antiemetic. Stop within 3 days — delays compensation.
  • Methylprednisolone 100 mg taper over 22 days within 3 days of onset improves caloric recovery (Strupp NEJM 2004).
  • Vestibular rehabilitation — most important long-term intervention; start as soon as tolerated.

Labyrinthitis

VN + cochlear involvement → vertigo + SNHL + tinnitus. Distinguish from AICA stroke (vertigo + sudden SNHL + central HINTS — urgent imaging).

~25% of AVS patients in the ED have a posterior-circulation stroke — PICA (cerebellum / lateral medullary Wallenberg) or AICA. Lateral medullary Wallenberg: vertigo + ipsilateral facial sensory loss + Horner + cerebellar signs + contralateral body pain/temp loss + dysphagia + hoarseness. Early MRI DWI misses 10–20% of small posterior-fossa strokes — HINTS+ is more sensitive in trained hands. Use MRI/MRA; CT misses posterior fossa.
5. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo

Most common cause of vertigo. Otoconia detach from utricle into a semicircular canal — posterior canal ~85% > horizontal > anterior. Canalithiasis (free) vs cupulolithiasis (adherent).

  • Triggers: rolling over, looking up, bending; brief episodes (seconds to < 1 min).
  • Dix-Hallpike — posterior canal — upbeat-torsional nystagmus, 5–20 s latency, < 60 s duration, reverses on sit-up, fatigues on repeat.
  • Supine roll (Pagnini-McClure) — horizontal canal — geotropic (canalithiasis) or apogeotropic (cupulolithiasis).
BPPVManoeuvre
Posterior canalEpley — 80–90% after 1–2 attempts; Semont alternative

Horizontal — apogeotropicConvert to geotropic first (Gufoni or head-shake), then BBQ; forced prolonged position
Anterior (rare)Deep head-hanging / reverse Epley

Recurrence ~30% at 1 year, ~50% at 5 years. Teach modified home Epley. Avoid vestibular suppressants — they don't treat BPPV.

6. Ménière disease (Bárány 2015)

Endolymphatic hydrops. Definite Ménière criteria:

  1. ≥ 2 spontaneous vertigo episodes, each 20 min – 12 h.
  2. Audiometric low/medium-frequency SNHL in affected ear before/during/after a vertigo episode.
  3. Fluctuating aural symptoms (hearing, tinnitus, fullness) in the affected ear.
  4. No better diagnosis.
  • Lifestyle: low salt (< 2 g/day), reduced caffeine/alcohol, sleep, stress.
  • Diuretic (HCTZ ± triamterene) widely used first-line.
  • Acute: vestibular suppressant + antiemetic.
  • Betahistine 16–48 mg/day common outside US; weak RCT evidence.
  • Refractory: intratympanic gentamicin (vertigo control; hearing loss risk) or intratympanic steroid (preserves hearing better).
  • Surgery: labyrinthectomy or vestibular neurectomy for refractory unilateral with non-serviceable hearing. Hearing aids or cochlear implant for hearing.
7. Vestibular migraine (Bárány / IHS 2012)
  1. ≥ 5 episodes of vestibular symptoms (vertigo, head-motion intolerance, positional vertigo, visually induced) lasting 5 min – 72 h.
  2. Current or past migraine (ICHD-3).
  3. ≥ 1 migraine feature with ≥ 50% of vestibular episodes: headache (unilateral, pulsating, mod-severe, aggravated by activity); photophobia + phonophobia; visual aura.
  4. No better diagnosis.

Treat as migraine (Module 4): triggers, sleep, hydration, regular meals. Acute: triptan, antiemetic, short suppressant. Prevention: topiramate, propranolol/metoprolol, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, flunarizine, valproate; emerging CGRP mAb data. Vestibular rehab for residual imbalance.

8. PPPD — persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (Bárány 2017)

Most common cause of chronic vestibular symptoms. ≥ 3 months of non-spinning dizziness / unsteadiness exacerbated by:

  • Upright posture.
  • Active or passive motion (any direction).
  • Moving visual stimuli (supermarket aisles, scrolling screens).

Often follows acute vestibular event (VN, BPPV, VM, panic, post-concussion) that has resolved. Not psychogenic — functional vestibular disorder.

  • Vestibular rehabilitation — foundation.
  • SSRI or SNRI (sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine) — best pharm evidence; start low, titrate slowly.
  • CBT for fear-avoidance and visual dependence.
  • Avoid prolonged vestibular suppressants.
9. Bilateral vestibulopathy, SCDS, perilymph fistula
  • Bilateral vestibulopathy: oscillopsia with head/walking + imbalance in dark + no vertigo. Bilaterally abnormal head impulse; dynamic visual acuity drops ≥ 2 Snellen lines. Causes: aminoglycoside (gentamicin), cisplatin, idiopathic, autoimmune (Cogan), meningitis, superficial siderosis. Stop offending drug + vestibular rehab.
  • SCDS (Minor syndrome): thin/absent bone over superior canal — "third window". Vertigo + oscillopsia triggered by loud sound (Tullio) or Valsalva/pressure (Hennebert); autophony; hearing own footsteps, pulse, eye movements; conductive HL with normal middle ear. Dx: high-resolution CT Pöschl plane + VEMP low thresholds. Tx: avoid triggers; surgical plugging / resurfacing.
  • Perilymph fistula: post-barotrauma / head injury / stapes surgery; fluctuating vertigo + HL; bed rest, surgical repair selected.

Causes: posterior-circulation stroke (PICA Wallenberg, AICA, basilar — Module 2); cerebellar haemorrhage (sudden severe occipital headache + vomiting + ataxia + ↓ LOC — neurosurgical emergency); vertebral artery dissection; MS (Module 8); tumour — vestibular schwannoma at CPA (Module 12); Chiari I (Module 16); episodic ataxia type 2 (CACNA1A — acetazolamide); Wernicke encephalopathy (Module 13 — thiamine before glucose); drugs (phenytoin, CBZ, alcohol, lithium — Module 13).

11. Disequilibrium, gait, presyncope
  • Disequilibrium without vertigo: bilateral vestibulopathy, peripheral neuropathy (Module 9), cerebellar disease (Module 6), spasticity, parkinsonism, NPH (Module 7), severe visual impairment, deconditioning, multisensory deficit of the elderly.
  • Presyncope: orthostatic hypotension, cardiac (arrhythmia, AS), reflex syncope, drugs (Module 15). Not vestibular.
  • Falls in older adults: multifactorial. Multidisciplinary falls clinic; deprescribing; exercise (Otago); home safety; vision and hearing check; bone health.
12. When to refer
  • Same day: suspected posterior-circulation stroke (AVS with central HINTS, inability to walk unaided, sudden hearing loss with vertigo, brainstem signs); cerebellar haemorrhage; vertebral artery dissection; sudden hearing loss (otology emergency within 72 h for steroid trial).
  • Within 1 week: refractory BPPV; suspected Ménière; suspected vestibular migraine; suspected SCDS; chronic dizziness with red flags.
  • Routine: PPPD for vestibular rehab + SSRI; established VM on prophylaxis; bilateral vestibulopathy for rehab.
  • Always involve: ENT / neuro-otology; audiology; vestibular physiotherapy; neurology when central or atypical; falls clinic.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 65-year-old man with hypertension and AF develops sudden continuous vertigo, nausea, and is unable to walk unaided. Exam: bidirectional gaze-evoked nystagmus, normal head impulse, and a vertical skew on alternate cover. Hearing intact

HINTS pattern: normal head impulse + direction-changing nystagmus + skew = central (stroke pattern).

Diagnosis: Posterior-circulation stroke (PICA cerebellar) until proven otherwise.

Plan:

  1. Treat as a stroke pathway — urgent stroke team activation; ABCs; IV access; bedside glucose; ECG.
  2. MRI/MRA brain (not CT — misses posterior fossa); CTA helpful for vertebral / basilar stenosis or dissection.
  3. If MRI confirms acute infarct and within time window: consider thrombolysis (Module 2) per local protocol.
  4. Admission to stroke unit; secondary prevention (anticoagulate for AF; statin; BP optimisation).
  5. Watch for cerebellar oedema and impending herniation in first 24–48 h; neurosurgical consult if mass effect.
  6. Vestibular rehabilitation after acute phase.
  7. Cross-ref Module 2.
"Inability to walk unaided" in an AVS patient is a major red flag for stroke. Normal head impulse + direction-changing nystagmus + skew = central pattern, even if MRI DWI is initially negative. Repeat MRI at 24–48 h if clinical suspicion remains.
Case 2 — A 55-year-old woman has 5 episodes of brief spinning vertigo lasting 20–40 seconds, triggered by rolling over in bed and looking up to a high shelf. No hearing loss or headache

Dix-Hallpike on the right: upbeat-torsional nystagmus with 10 s latency, lasting ~30 s, reversed on sit-up, fatigued on repeat. Left Dix-Hallpike negative. No central features.

Diagnosis: Right posterior canal BPPV (canalithiasis).

Plan:

  1. Perform Epley canalith repositioning manoeuvre for the right posterior canal: Dix-Hallpike (head turned 45° right, lowered) → wait 30 s → rotate head 90° to left → wait 30 s → roll patient onto left side, head turning further so nose points down → wait 30 s → sit patient up. Watch for nystagmus throughout.
  2. Educate: mild lingering disequilibrium for hours to days is normal; gradual return to activity; reassess in 1 week if symptoms persist.
  3. Teach modified home Epley for recurrence (~30% at 1 year).
  4. Avoid vestibular suppressants — they don't treat BPPV and delay compensation.
  5. If unsuccessful after 2 attempts: try Semont liberatory manoeuvre; refer to ENT / neuro-otology.
  6. Investigate for secondary causes if recurrent/atypical: prior head injury, vestibular neuritis, Ménière, vascular insult.
Case 3 — A 45-year-old has episodes of vertigo lasting 1–4 hours with fluctuating left-sided hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness, occurring every few weeks for the past year

Audiometry: low-frequency SNHL in left ear documented during one episode.

Diagnosis: Definite Ménière disease, left (Bárány 2015 criteria met: ≥ 2 spontaneous vertigo 20 min – 12 h + audiometric low-frequency SNHL + fluctuating aural symptoms + no other cause).

Plan:

  1. Lifestyle: low salt (< 2 g/day), reduced caffeine/alcohol, regular sleep, stress reduction.
  2. First-line: thiazide diuretic (hydrochlorothiazide ± triamterene).
  3. Betahistine 16–48 mg/day where available (Europe / Asia; not US).
  4. Acute attacks: vestibular suppressant (cyclizine, meclizine) + antiemetic.
  5. Audiology follow-up; consider hearing aid as hearing loss progresses.
  6. If frequent disabling vertigo despite conservative care: intratympanic gentamicin (chemical labyrinthectomy — controls vertigo but worsens hearing) or intratympanic steroid (better hearing preservation).
  7. Refractory unilateral with non-serviceable hearing: labyrinthectomy or vestibular neurectomy.
  8. Counsel about bilateral involvement (~30% over years) and avoiding driving during active episodes.
Case 4 — A 32-year-old with history of migraine has 10 episodes of vertigo lasting 30 minutes to 4 hours over 6 months. Half are accompanied by unilateral pulsating headache, photophobia, and phonophobia. No hearing loss. Triggers include lack of sleep and red wine

Audiometry normal. No central features. Bárány / IHS 2012 criteria for vestibular migraine met.

Diagnosis: Vestibular migraine.

Plan:

  1. Education and reassurance — VM is common, under-diagnosed, and responds to standard migraine management. Cross-ref Module 4.
  2. Identify and manage triggers: sleep, hydration, regular meals, caffeine consistency, alcohol moderation, stress reduction.
  3. Acute: triptan + antiemetic (prochlorperazine, ondansetron); short course of vestibular suppressant only for severe attacks.
  4. Prevention (≥ 4 episodes/month or significant disability): topiramate or propranolol/metoprolol first-line; amitriptyline, venlafaxine, flunarizine, valproate as alternatives; emerging CGRP mAb data.
  5. Vestibular rehabilitation for residual imbalance between attacks.
  6. Headache diary; review at 3 months for prophylactic response.
Case 5 — A 55-year-old man had vestibular neuritis 8 months ago. The acute spinning resolved within 3 weeks, but he remains dizzy daily — non-spinning, worse upright, worse in busy visual environments (supermarket aisles, scrolling screens). Avoidance and anxiety are escalating

Exam: normal cranial nerves, normal head impulse, normal nystagmus, normal gait. Audiology normal. MRI normal.

Diagnosis: Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD; Bárány 2017 criteria met).

Plan:

  1. Education and validation: PPPD is a recognised, treatable disorder, not "all in your head" — it is a functional vestibular disorder driven by maladaptive postural control and visual dependence after an acute vestibular event.
  2. Vestibular rehabilitation — foundation; gradual exposure, habituation, visual desensitisation; expect months of therapy.
  3. SSRI or SNRI: sertraline 25 mg titrated to 50–100 mg, or escitalopram 5 mg → 10–20 mg, or venlafaxine — best pharmacological evidence; start low and increase slowly because patients with PPPD are sensitive to side effects.
  4. CBT targeted at fear-avoidance and visual dependence.
  5. Avoid prolonged vestibular suppressants — they delay habituation.
  6. Address comorbid anxiety; sleep hygiene.
  7. Reassess at 3 months; expect gradual improvement over 6–12 months.
Case 6 — A 50-year-old septic patient received 12 days of IV gentamicin during admission. After recovery, she complains of bouncing vision when walking, severe imbalance in the dark, and inability to read street signs while moving. No vertigo

Exam: bilaterally abnormal head impulse test. Dynamic visual acuity: Snellen drops ≥ 3 lines with head shake. Romberg positive. Normal cranial nerves.

Diagnosis: Bilateral vestibulopathy from aminoglycoside ototoxicity.

Plan:

  1. Stop any further aminoglycoside exposure; document allergy.
  2. Audiology + formal vestibular function tests (caloric, video head impulse, VEMP).
  3. Vestibular rehabilitation — gaze stabilisation, balance training, walking with head turns; long-term and patient-specific.
  4. Counsel: recovery is variable; complete return of function is rare. Safety measures (no driving without compensation; lighting at night; check vision and proprioception; avoid scaffolds, ladders).
  5. Consider other causes (autoimmune Cogan, cisplatin, idiopathic, meningitis, superficial siderosis).
  6. Caveat for future antibiotic prescribing: aminoglycosides can cause vestibulotoxicity even at "therapeutic" doses; risk increased by renal impairment, duration, concomitant ototoxics, age, mitochondrial m.1555A>G variant — screen if family history of aminoglycoside ototoxicity.
