Why this module?

About 11% of people will have a seizure in their lifetime, and 1–2% will develop epilepsy. As a general physician, you will see first seizures, breakthrough seizures, suspected non-epileptic events, and the occasional status epilepticus. This module covers the bedside reasoning, the workup, the drug ladder, and the protocol that saves brain in status.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Apply the ILAE 2017 classification to any witnessed event.
  • Distinguish seizure from syncope and PNES at the bedside.
  • Decide when to start an AED after a first seizure.
  • Pick a first-line AED appropriate to seizure type and patient.
  • Run the status epilepticus protocol on the clock.
  • Counsel women, older adults, and refractory patients about specific risks.

The clock that matters most in this module: in convulsive status, benzodiazepine within 5 min, second-line agent within 20 min, anaesthetic within 40–60 min if not controlled. Under-dosing and delay are the most common errors.

Lessons

1. Definitions — seizure vs epilepsy vs provoked seizure

A seizure = transient clinical event from excessive synchronous cortical neuronal activity.

Epilepsy (ILAE 2014): ≥ 2 unprovoked seizures > 24 h apart, or 1 unprovoked seizure with > 60% 10-year recurrence risk, or diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.

Acute symptomatic / provoked seizure: caused by an immediate brain insult — stroke, head injury, CNS infection, severe metabolic derangement, drug exposure. Treat the cause; AEDs short-term or not at all.

Pearl: A single seizure with an epileptiform EEG, structural lesion on MRI, nocturnal onset, or focal onset already meets ILAE criteria for epilepsy — start an AED.
2. ILAE 2017 classification — onset, awareness, motor features
  1. Onset: focal vs generalised vs unknown.
  2. Awareness (focal only): aware vs impaired awareness.
  3. Motor features: automatisms, tonic, clonic, atonic, myoclonic, hyperkinetic, behavioural arrest, etc.

Retired terms: "simple partial" → focal aware; "complex partial" → focal impaired awareness; "secondarily generalised" → focal to bilateral tonic-clonic.

Localising auras: epigastric rising / déjà vu → mesial temporal; visual circles → occipital; sensory march → contralateral postcentral; forced head version → contralateral frontal eye field.

4. The first seizure workup
  1. Witness history (mandatory).
  2. Exam — focal deficits, meningism, head injury, neurocutaneous stigmata.
  3. Labs — CBC, electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), glucose, renal/liver, urine toxicology, pregnancy test.
  4. ECG — exclude long-QT / Brugada (convulsive syncope mimic).
  5. Non-contrast CT in ED to exclude acute lesions.
  6. Outpatient MRI with epilepsy protocol.
  7. EEG within 24–48 h (sleep-deprived if normal initially).

Start an AED if recurrence risk > 60%: epileptiform EEG, structural lesion, focal onset, nocturnal seizure, recognised syndrome (e.g. JME morning myoclonus).

5. Epilepsy syndromes worth knowing
SyndromeKey features
Childhood absence4–12 y. 3-Hz generalised spike-wave. Ethosuximide or valproate.
Juvenile myoclonic epilepsyMorning myoclonic jerks + GTC on awakening. Lifelong AED. Avoid Na-channel blockers (carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin) — worsen myoclonus.
Mesial temporal lobe epilepsyMost common adult focal epilepsy. Febrile-seizure history; hippocampal sclerosis. Up to 80% become seizure-free after temporal lobectomy.

Valproate in women of childbearing age: 4–9% major malformation rate and reduced child IQ. Avoid unless no alternative; document careful counselling and contraception plan.
7. AED side effects you must counsel about
  • Lamotrigine — rash; slow titration is essential. Any rash → stop and contact you.
  • Carbamazepine — SIADH/hyponatraemia, leucopenia; Stevens-Johnson if HLA-B*1502 positive (Asian populations — screen).
  • Valproate — weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, hyperammonaemia, teratogen.
  • Phenytoin — gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, cerebellar toxicity with chronic use; zero-order kinetics; many interactions.
  • Levetiracetam — irritability/depression in ~10–15%.
  • Topiramate — cognitive slowing, weight loss, paraesthesia, kidney stones, acute glaucoma.
  • Phenobarbital — sedation, cognitive blunting, dependence, induction.
8. Status epilepticus — the protocol

SE = continuous seizure > 5 min, or 2+ seizures without recovery between. Treat from minute 5.

  1. 0–5 min: ABC, glucose, IV access.
  2. 5–20 min: Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (or IM midazolam 10 mg if no IV).
  3. 20–40 min: Second-line agent — fosphenytoin 20 PE/kg or valproate 40 mg/kg or levetiracetam 60 mg/kg. ESETT trial: similar efficacy (~47%).
  4. 40–60 min: Intubate. Continuous IV anaesthetic — midazolam, propofol, or pentobarbital. cEEG.
  5. > 24 h: Super-refractory SE — add ketamine, ketogenic diet, hypothermia, immunotherapy.

9. Women, older adults, and refractory epilepsy

Pre-conception

  • Avoid valproate when possible.
  • Lamotrigine and levetiracetam are the safest AEDs in pregnancy.
  • Folic acid 0.4–5 mg daily before conception.
  • Lamotrigine levels fall in pregnancy — monitor and uptitrate.
  • Enzyme inducers reduce OCP efficacy and vice versa.

Older adults

Stroke is the leading cause of new-onset epilepsy > 60 y. Use lamotrigine or levetiracetam — clean profiles, minimal interactions. Renal dose levetiracetam.

Refractory epilepsy

Failure of 2 appropriate AEDs at adequate dose = drug-resistant epilepsy. Refer to an epilepsy centre. Up to 80% of selected temporal lobe epilepsy patients become seizure-free after surgery.

10. Red flags & lifestyle counselling
Refer / admit immediately: status epilepticus · first-ever seizure with focal deficit / fever / meningism / suspected SAH · eclampsia · infantile spasms · failure to wake within 20 min of a convulsion (suspect non-convulsive SE).

Lifestyle: Driving restrictions (6–12 months in most jurisdictions); no heights, no swimming alone, supervise bathing infants. Encourage seizure diary. Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) — rare but real, particularly with nocturnal GTC; counsel about treatment adherence and avoiding triggers.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 19-year-old with morning jerks and a single GTC

For 2 years, brief arm jerks on waking that have caused her to drop her cereal bowl. Last week, she had a witnessed GTC the morning after a sleep-deprived night. MRI normal. EEG: generalised 4–5 Hz polyspike-and-wave.

Diagnosis: juvenile myoclonic epilepsy. Lifelong AED required.

AED choice: levetiracetam or lamotrigine (she is a young woman; avoid valproate). Do not use carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, or phenytoin — they worsen myoclonus.

Counsel: sleep regularity, alcohol moderation, OCP/AED interaction, folic acid before any pregnancy, driving restrictions.

Case 2 — A 38-year-old with episodes of déjà vu and lip-smacking

Past medical history includes a prolonged febrile seizure at age 18 months. Brief episodes of déjà vu, epigastric rising, and a "cold feeling", followed by witnessed unresponsiveness with lip smacking and right-hand fumbling, then confused for 5 minutes. MRI: right hippocampal atrophy and FLAIR hyperintensity (mesial temporal sclerosis). EEG: right anterior temporal sharp waves.

Diagnosis: right mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with focal impaired awareness seizures.

Trial AED (lamotrigine or levetiracetam). If two AEDs fail → refer to epilepsy surgery centre; up to 80% become seizure-free after temporal lobectomy.

Case 3 — Status epilepticus in the ED

A 52-year-old known epileptic arrives in active convulsion, started 8 minutes ago per EMS. Glucose 110. IV in place.

0–5 min: ABCs done. 5–20 min: Lorazepam 4 mg IV. Still seizing at 12 min — repeat lorazepam 4 mg IV. 20–40 min: Add fosphenytoin 20 PE/kg IV. If still seizing at 40 min: intubate, midazolam infusion, cEEG.

Search for cause — missed AEDs, infection, low AED level, drugs lowering threshold.

Don't forget: if convulsions stop but the patient doesn't wake within 20 min, suspect non-convulsive SE — order cEEG.
Case 4 — Possible PNES

A 26-year-old with recurrent "seizures" — eyes forced closed, side-to-side head movement, asynchronous limb thrashing lasting > 5 minutes, with full recall afterwards. AED levels therapeutic. MRI normal. Interictal EEGs normal.

Next step: admit for inpatient video-EEG monitoring to capture an event and confirm.

Important: many patients with PNES also have epilepsy. Don't withdraw AEDs without confirmation.

Case 5 — First seizure, normal workup

A 45-year-old man has a single witnessed GTC after a night of poor sleep and alcohol. Examination normal. CT, EEG, and MRI all normal. No prior events.

Recurrence risk: ~24% over 2 years (lower with normal EEG + MRI). Does not meet ILAE criteria for epilepsy after a single unprovoked seizure with negative workup.

Reasonable to not start an AED, with careful counselling about triggers (sleep, alcohol), driving restrictions per local law, and return if a second event occurs (which would meet criteria for epilepsy).
