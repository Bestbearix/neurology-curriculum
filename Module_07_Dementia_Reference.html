1 · Big picture: normal aging → SCD → MCI → dementia

Cognitive decline runs on a spectrum. Knowing where the patient sits decides next steps.

StagePatient's complaintObjective testingFunctional impact
Normal cognitive agingSubjective slowing, occasional name-findingWithin age-adjusted normsNone
Subjective cognitive decline (SCD)Self-perceived declineNormalNone — but elevated future risk
Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)Complaint usually presentObjective deficit in ≥ 1 domainADLs preserved; complex IADLs may be slowed
Dementia (DSM-5: major neurocognitive disorder)Often patient lacks insight; family reportsSignificant deficit in ≥ 1 domainADLs impaired — this is the defining feature

The six cognitive domains (DSM-5): complex attention · executive function · learning & memory · language · visuospatial perception · social cognition. Different diseases prefer different domains, and that profile is the diagnostic clue.

The single decision that defines dementia: can the patient still do their usual independent activities of daily living (cooking, finances, medications, transport, shopping)? If yes → MCI. If no → dementia. Document carefully with the informant.

2 · Approach to the patient with memory complaints

History — always include an informant

  • Tempo and pattern: insidious + steady progression (AD), stepwise (vascular), fluctuating day to day (DLB), rapid < 1 year (CJD, autoimmune, paraneoplastic, NPH, metabolic, depression, drug-related, structural).
  • Domain of onset: short-term memory (AD), behaviour/personality (bvFTD), language (PPA), visual hallucinations + parkinsonism (DLB), gait + incontinence + cognition (NPH).
  • Functional decline: getting lost, money management, driving, medications, missed appointments.
  • Behavioural: apathy, depression, disinhibition, hallucinations, delusions (especially paranoid — common in AD; visual hallucinations very early → DLB).
  • Sleep: REM sleep behaviour disorder (dream enactment) precedes DLB / PD by years.
  • Motor: parkinsonism, falls, myoclonus, asymmetric apraxia.
  • Vascular risks & cardiovascular events.
  • Medications: anticholinergics (oxybutynin, tricyclics, sedating antihistamines, paroxetine), benzodiazepines, opioids, lithium, statins (rare cognitive effect), chemotherapy.
  • Alcohol / substance / nutritional status.
  • Family history of early-onset dementia, Huntington, ALS.
  • Mood & sleep — depression mimics dementia ("pseudodementia").

Adjust for education and language; formal neuropsychological testing when bedside is inconclusive, deficit is subtle, the patient is highly educated, or to characterise the cognitive profile.

Examination

General medical exam + focused neuro exam — look for parkinsonism, apraxia, cortical sensory loss, alien-limb phenomenon, myoclonus, focal weakness, ataxia, downgaze palsy (PSP), upper motor neuron + LMN signs (FTD-ALS), gait abnormalities (NPH magnetic gait; vascular small-step gait; cerebellar; sensory ataxic).

Standard laboratory workup (rule out reversible / contributory)

  • CBC, electrolytes, calcium, glucose, renal & liver function
  • TSH
  • Vitamin B12 (± folate, methylmalonic acid)
  • Syphilis serology where prevalent or in atypical / rapidly progressive cases
  • HIV in at-risk patients
  • Consider: ESR, CRP, ANA, ceruloplasmin (young patient), 24-h urine copper, heavy metals, paraneoplastic / autoimmune encephalitis panel in rapidly progressive disease
  • Structural brain imaging — MRI preferred (CT acceptable). Patterns: medial temporal atrophy (AD); frontal/anterior temporal atrophy (FTD); occipital perfusion deficit (DLB); ventricular enlargement out of proportion (NPH); white-matter disease / lacunes (vascular).

4 · Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)

Objective cognitive deficit in ≥ 1 domain with preserved independent function. Annual conversion rate to dementia is 10–15%. Subtyped by domain affected:

  • Amnestic MCI (memory predominant) → often progresses to AD.
  • Non-amnestic MCI (executive, language, or visuospatial) → may progress to FTD, DLB, or vascular.
  • Multi-domain MCI → variable; often a vascular or mixed substrate.

Management: identify and treat reversible contributors; aggressive cardiovascular risk-factor control; physical activity (best evidence); cognitive engagement; manage sleep, mood, hearing & vision; review medications; counsel on driving and advance planning. No medication is currently FDA-approved for MCI — cholinesterase inhibitors do not prevent or delay progression in MCI. Lecanemab (2023) is approved in the US for early symptomatic AD including MCI due to AD when amyloid-confirmed (see § 11).

5 · Alzheimer disease

What it is

Cortical neurodegenerative disease with extracellular β-amyloid plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau (neurofibrillary tangles). Loss begins in entorhinal cortex and hippocampus and spreads to temporal, parietal, and frontal neocortex. ~80% of dementia in autopsy series, often with co-pathology (Lewy bodies in 20–40%, vascular changes in ~35%).

Genetics

  • Sporadic late-onset AD: APOE ε4 allele is the strongest risk factor (one allele ~3× risk; two alleles ~12–15× risk). APOE ε2 is protective.
  • Early-onset familial AD (< 5%): autosomal dominant mutations in APP, PSEN1, PSEN2.

Clinical features

  • Insidious onset, gradual progression over years.
  • Episodic memory deficit first — cued recall does NOT help (storage failure), distinguishes from frontal/subcortical retrieval failure.
  • Language: word-finding, then naming, then comprehension.
  • Visuospatial: getting lost in familiar places; difficulty drawing.
  • Executive dysfunction later.
  • BPSD: apathy, depression early; paranoid delusions ("things being stolen"), agitation, sleep disturbance later.
  • Late: incontinence, gait apraxia, mutism, dysphagia.
  • Average course from diagnosis to death: 8–10 years (range 3–20).

Atypical AD presentations

  • Posterior cortical atrophy (visual variant) — visuospatial & visual perception problems first, memory later.
  • Logopenic primary progressive aphasia (PPA-L) — word-finding pauses, impaired sentence repetition; usually AD pathology.
  • Frontal/dysexecutive variant — behavioural changes mimicking FTD.

Diagnosis (NIA-AA 2011, updated 2018 ATN framework)

Probable AD: typical amnestic syndrome + insidious onset + progressive decline + no alternative cause + supportive biomarkers (medial temporal atrophy on MRI; CSF Aβ↓ + tau↑; amyloid PET positive). The 2018 ATN framework defines AD by biomarker positivity rather than clinical syndrome:

  • A = amyloid biomarker (CSF Aβ42 or amyloid PET)
  • T = tau biomarker (CSF p-tau or tau PET)
  • N = neurodegeneration (MRI atrophy, FDG-PET hypometabolism, CSF total tau)

A+T+ = Alzheimer biology regardless of symptoms; A+T− = Alzheimer pathological change; A−T+ = non-AD tauopathy.

Symptomatic treatment

Modest, symptomatic benefit (~6–12 month cognitive plateau on average); do not modify disease course. Trial discontinuation when severe end-stage. No evidence that any of these slows MCI progression.

What to avoid

  • Strong anticholinergics — diphenhydramine, oxybutynin, tricyclics, paroxetine, scopolamine. They worsen cognition and may double dementia risk over years.
  • Benzodiazepines — increase falls, sedation, paradoxical agitation; modest evidence for increased dementia risk with chronic use.
  • Typical antipsychotics (haloperidol) — extrapyramidal effects, increased mortality in dementia; FDA black-box warning. Atypicals similar risk but sometimes necessary for severe BPSD.
  • Routine use of aducanumab (controversial, withdrawn from market 2024) and use of newer anti-amyloid agents without ARIA monitoring.

6 · Dementia with Lewy bodies & Parkinson disease dementia

Both are α-synucleinopathies with cortical Lewy bodies. The arbitrary "1-year rule" distinguishes them: if cognitive impairment appears within 1 year of (or before) parkinsonism → DLB; if dementia appears after > 1 year of established Parkinson disease → PDD. Same disease biology; useful clinically because of different presentation.

Core clinical features of DLB (need 2 for probable, 1 + biomarker for probable; 4th edition consortium criteria 2017)

  • Fluctuating cognition with prominent variations in attention and alertness.
  • Recurrent visual hallucinations — typically well-formed, detailed, often non-threatening (people, animals).
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) — acting out dreams, often years before cognitive symptoms.
  • Parkinsonism — symmetric, less tremor-predominant than PD, axial more than appendicular.

Supportive features

Severe neuroleptic sensitivity (dramatic worsening / parkinsonism with first dose), repeated falls, syncope, transient unresponsiveness, autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, urinary problems, constipation), hypersomnia, hyposmia, depression, anxiety, delusions (usually paranoid), apathy.

Biomarkers

  • DaT-SPECT — reduced striatal dopamine transporter (very useful when diagnosis uncertain; positive in DLB / PDD, negative in AD / FTD).
  • MIBG cardiac scintigraphy reduced.
  • Polysomnography confirms RBD.
  • Reduced occipital metabolism on FDG-PET ("cingulate island sign").

Cognitive profile

Early dysexecutive, visuospatial, and attentional deficits. Memory is relatively preserved early. The MMSE often misses early DLB; MoCA is more sensitive.

Treatment

TargetDrug / approach

Always ask about visual hallucinations, fluctuations, RBD, parkinsonism before prescribing any antipsychotic to an older adult.

7 · Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)

Heterogeneous group with frontal and anterior temporal lobe degeneration. The most common dementia under age 60. Pathology: tau, TDP-43, or FUS protein aggregates (FTLD-tau, FTLD-TDP, FTLD-FUS). ~40% familial; genes include C9orf72 (most common — also causes ALS), MAPT, GRN.

Three main FTD phenotypes

VariantHallmarks
Behavioural variant FTD (bvFTD)Insidious change in personality and behaviour: disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, perseverative/stereotyped behaviours, dietary change (carbohydrate craving, hyperorality), executive dysfunction. Memory and visuospatial often preserved early. International consensus criteria require ≥ 3 of 6 features with progression.
Non-fluent / agrammatic primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA)Effortful speech with agrammatism and/or apraxia of speech; comprehension of grammar impaired; single-word comprehension preserved. Left inferior frontal atrophy.
Semantic variant PPA (svPPA / semantic dementia)Loss of word meaning and object recognition (anomia + impaired single-word comprehension); fluent but empty speech. Bilateral (often left > right) anterior temporal atrophy.

The logopenic variant PPA (lvPPA) is usually AD pathology, not FTD: word-retrieval pauses, impaired sentence repetition; left temporoparietal atrophy.

Overlap syndromes (FTD spectrum)

  • FTD-ALS — frontotemporal dementia with motor neuron disease (often C9orf72).
  • Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal syndrome (CBS) — Parkinson-plus syndromes; PSP and CBS pathology is FTLD-tau.

Diagnosis

Clinical + neuropsychological + MRI showing frontal/anterior temporal atrophy (often asymmetric) + FDG-PET showing frontal/anterior temporal hypometabolism. CSF AD biomarkers often negative (helpful to distinguish from atypical AD).

Treatment

  • No disease-modifying therapy. Cholinesterase inhibitors do not help and may worsen behaviour.
  • SSRIs (trazodone, citalopram, sertraline) may reduce disinhibition, repetitive behaviours, dietary excess.
  • Speech and language therapy for PPA.
  • Caregiver support paramount — bvFTD is exceptionally hard on families.
  • Genetic counselling for familial cases.
  • Avoid antipsychotics where possible (extrapyramidal sensitivity, especially in tauopathy).

8 · Vascular cognitive impairment / dementia

Spectrum from vascular MCI through multi-infarct dementia to subcortical ischaemic vascular dementia (Binswanger). 2–10% of pure dementia; mixed vascular-AD pathology is very common.

Clinical patterns

  • Multi-infarct (cortical): stepwise decline with focal events; cognitive profile depends on territories affected.
  • Strategic-infarct dementia: single stroke in critical site (thalamus, angular gyrus, caudate) → sudden cognitive change.
  • Subcortical ischaemic (Binswanger): insidious progression with executive dysfunction, psychomotor slowing, gait apraxia (small-step, wide-based), pseudobulbar affect, urinary urgency.
  • Post-stroke dementia: incidence ~30% at 1 year after major stroke.
  • CADASIL (Cerebral Autosomal Dominant Arteriopathy with Subcortical Infarcts and Leukoencephalopathy) — NOTCH3 mutation; young-onset migraine with aura → recurrent lacunar strokes → vascular dementia; striking white-matter disease with anterior temporal pole involvement.

Diagnosis

Cognitive deficit + neuroimaging evidence of significant vascular disease (large infarcts, strategic infarct, multiple lacunes, extensive white-matter disease) + temporal relationship (deficit onset within 3 months of vascular event for some criteria).

Treatment

  • Aggressive vascular risk-factor control — the most evidence-based intervention. BP < 130/80, statin, antiplatelet, DM & HbA1c control, smoking cessation, exercise.
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine: modest cognitive benefit in vascular dementia (smaller than in AD). Reasonable to trial.
  • Treat depression (very common).
  • Manage stroke risks per Module 2.

9 · Mixed dementia & less common causes

  • Mixed AD + vascular is the most common combination at autopsy.
  • Mixed AD + Lewy body common — consider when AD patient has prominent visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, or fluctuations.
  • Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) — rapidly progressive (weeks-months) dementia + myoclonus + cerebellar / pyramidal signs + akinetic mutism; MRI DWI cortical ribboning, basal-ganglia hyperintensity, pulvinar / "hockey-stick" in variant CJD; EEG periodic sharp wave complexes; CSF RT-QuIC highly sensitive & specific. Always fatal within 1 year.
  • Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) — repetitive head trauma; tau-positive perivascular sulcal deposits; behavioural and cognitive symptoms years after exposure. Diagnosis at autopsy; criteria evolving for in-vivo diagnosis.
  • Huntington disease (Module 6) — subcortical dementia + chorea + psychiatric.
  • Wilson disease — young patient with neuropsychiatric + extrapyramidal features.
  • HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder — spectrum from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment to HIV-associated dementia; subcortical pattern; ART improves.
  • Neurosyphilis (general paresis), Whipple disease, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy.

10 · Behavioural & psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)

BPSD includes apathy, depression, anxiety, agitation, aggression, psychosis, sleep disturbance, sundowning, wandering, eating changes. Drives caregiver burden and institutionalisation more than cognitive decline itself.

Always first: identify and treat triggers

  • Pain (constipation, urinary retention, dental, joint, fracture, infection).
  • Infection (UTI, pneumonia) — classic precipitant of acute behaviour change.
  • Drug-related (anticholinergics, opioids, BZD withdrawal, recent medication changes).
  • Hunger, thirst, bowel/bladder full, fatigue.
  • Environmental: change of place, noise, isolation, sundowning.
  • Communication frustration in PPA, hearing/vision impairment.

Non-pharmacological measures (first-line)

Caregiver education and validation, routine, orientation aids, structured activity, social engagement, music therapy, environmental modifications, sleep hygiene, reduce caffeine, treat sensory impairment, address pain proactively.

Pharmacological options (when non-pharmacological measures fail and there is risk of harm)

SymptomTry
DepressionSSRI (sertraline, citalopram — beware QTc > 20 mg/d in older patients; escitalopram preferred where available)
ApathyMethylphenidate (cautious trial), SSRI, treat depression
Anxiety / agitationSSRI; trazodone; pain control; quetiapine low-dose if severe; AVOID benzodiazepines (paradoxical worsening, falls, delirium)

FDA black-box warning — all antipsychotics in dementia carry a 1.5–1.7× mortality risk (cardiovascular and infectious). Use lowest dose, shortest duration, with documented informed consent and clear target symptom; re-evaluate every few weeks for de-prescribing.

11 · 2023–2024 update: anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies

Note: Merritt's 14th edition (2022) predates these approvals; the content below reflects 2023–2024 developments.

DrugFDA approvalStatus
Aducanumab (Aduhelm)2021 (accelerated)Manufacturer withdrew in 2024 due to lack of evidence of clinical benefit and slow uptake.
Lecanemab (Leqembi)Full approval Jul 2023Approved for early symptomatic AD (MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia) with amyloid confirmation. IV infusion every 2 weeks. CLARITY-AD trial: 27% slowing of cognitive decline on CDR-SB at 18 months.
Donanemab (Kisunla)Full approval Jul 2024Approved for same indication. IV every 4 weeks until amyloid clears (typically 6–18 months). TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial: similar magnitude of benefit, with potential to stop dosing once amyloid cleared.

Eligibility (broadly)

  • Confirmed AD biology (amyloid PET positive or CSF Aβ42/p-tau ratio supportive).
  • MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia (MMSE typically ≥ 22; CDR ≤ 1).
  • Pre-treatment MRI screening for cerebral microbleeds and superficial siderosis.
  • APOE ε4 genotyping recommended — ε4/ε4 homozygotes have higher rates of ARIA.
  • Not on anticoagulation (relative — bleeding risk).

Amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)

  • ARIA-E (oedema) — vasogenic oedema, usually asymptomatic; ~12% with lecanemab, ~24% with donanemab.
  • ARIA-H (haemorrhage) — microbleeds and superficial siderosis; ~17% lecanemab, ~31% donanemab.
  • Higher rates in APOE ε4 homozygotes (up to ~30% ARIA-E with lecanemab).
  • Symptomatic ARIA in ~3% (headache, confusion, focal deficit, seizure) — rare deaths reported, particularly with anticoagulant use or thrombolysis.
  • MRI monitoring required before infusions 5, 7, 14 (lecanemab) or at scheduled intervals (donanemab).

Practical implications for the general physician

  • Refer patients with early symptomatic AD to a memory or neurology clinic to discuss eligibility — access varies by region and reimbursement.
  • Counsel about modest average benefit (slowing not stopping), infusion burden, ARIA risk, MRI monitoring, cost.
  • Stop anti-amyloid mAb before thrombolysis in stroke; if patient on lecanemab/donanemab has an acute ischaemic stroke, tPA carries a notably increased ICH risk.
  • Symptomatic therapies (cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine) remain standard; mAbs are additive, not replacement.

12 · When to refer

Refer to neurology / memory clinic:
  • Diagnostic uncertainty (atypical presentation, age < 65, rapid progression, prominent psychiatric or motor features).
  • Suspected reversible cause requiring specialist workup (NPH for shunt evaluation, autoimmune encephalitis, CJD, paraneoplastic).
  • Family history of early-onset dementia or known autosomal dominant mutation (consider genetic counselling).
  • For consideration of anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody therapy.
  • BPSD refractory to first-line measures.
  • Driving fitness assessment.
Refer to old-age psychiatry: severe behavioural disturbance, psychiatric comorbidity (e.g. depression with suicidal ideation), need for inpatient assessment, complex pharmacological management.
