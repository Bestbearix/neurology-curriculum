▎How to use this module
  • Every section opens with a Key points box — skim those first, then read the detail.
  • Purple boxes mark what is new or changed since the first edition; green boxes are trials with the numbers worth quoting; amber boxes are do-not-miss safety points.
  • §3 is the investigation strategy written for a hospital without on-site MRI, CSF biomarkers or plasma p-tau — which tests to do now, which to batch, and what to treat before the result arrives.
  • The engine of everything: establish that there is real cognitive decline from a previous level, find the treatable contributors in every single patient, then name the syndrome.
The one thing to hold on to. The single highest-value act in a dementia clinic is not naming the degenerative disease — it is finding the reversible contribution sitting on top of it. Depression, drugs, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea, alcohol, hearing loss, delirium and subdural haematoma are all common, all treatable, and all repeatedly missed because the diagnosis of "dementia" closed the question.

1 The cognitive spectrum

▎Key points
  • The sequence runs normal ageing → subjective cognitive decline → mild cognitive impairment → dementia, and the line between each is drawn by function, not by test scores.
  • Dementia requires impairment of independent function. MCI is objective impairment with function preserved. SCD is a complaint with normal testing.
  • Always establish decline from a previous level — a lifelong low score is not dementia.
  • Get a collateral history. A patient who attends alone and reports memory problems is more likely to have depression or SCD than dementia; the patient brought in by a worried family, insisting nothing is wrong, is the more concerning presentation.

Dementia (major neurocognitive disorder)Reported, usually by an informantImpaired in ≥1 domain (usually more)Loss of independenceName the syndrome, treat contributors, plan care
Amnestic versus non-amnestic matters. Amnestic MCI — the patient who genuinely cannot store new information — most often progresses to Alzheimer disease. Non-amnestic MCI (executive, language, visuospatial) points toward vascular, Lewy body or frontotemporal disease. Ask the informant for a specific recent example rather than a general impression: "Tell me about something that happened last week that showed you there was a problem."
The commonest single error in this field is diagnosing dementia in a delirious patient. Delirium is acute, fluctuates through the day, and impairs attention above all else. If the patient cannot hold serial 7s or the months backwards, you are testing attention, not memory, and the result tells you nothing about their baseline. Treat the delirium, then reassess cognition weeks later — and remember that delirium and dementia coexist very often, each being a risk factor for the other.

2 Approach to the patient

▎Key points
  • Five questions in order: Is there true decline? Which domains? How fast? What else is on the exam? What is reversible?
  • Tempo drives everything. Months rather than years puts you into the rapidly-progressive-dementia pathway (§13), which is a different and more urgent workup.
  • A motor sign changes the differential entirely — parkinsonism, ataxia, myoclonus, pyramidal signs or eye-movement abnormality each redirect it.
  • Take a full drug history, including anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, and over-the-counter sleep and allergy preparations.

An algorithmic approach that works in a busy clinic. Establish the presence and severity of impairment; systematically test each domain; then branch on two questions — is it rapidly progressive? and are there motor abnormalities? Those two branches carry almost all the urgent and treatable diagnoses, and a patient who is negative on both can be worked up at a slower and cheaper tempo.

3 Investigation strategy & urgency

▎Key points
  • In a hospital without on-site MRI, CSF biomarkers or plasma p-tau, the useful question is "what changes management today, this week, or at the single referral visit?"
  • Tier A is the reversible-cause screen, and it is not optional in anyone. Every item is available in your own lab and every one can change the outcome.
  • Tempo sets the tier. A dementia evolving over years is a tier-C/D workup. A dementia evolving over weeks to months is a tier-A/B emergency — see §13.
  • Batch tier-D tests into one referral trip. MRI, neuropsychology, LP and the specialist opinion should happen in a single attendance, not four.

When CT is enough, and when it is not. CT will show you a subdural haematoma, a tumour with oedema, hydrocephalus, a large infarct and gross atrophy — which means it rules out the surgical causes, and that is its job. It will not reliably show hippocampal atrophy, the extent of small-vessel disease, cortical microbleeds and superficial siderosis, early cortical ribboning in CJD, or the medial temporal changes of limbic encephalitis. If your clinical question is "is there something surgical or structural here?", CT answers it today. If your question is "which degenerative disease is this?", CT will not answer it and MRI belongs in the batched referral.

Make one referral trip do the work of three. Write the referral so the receiving centre performs the MRI, the neuropsychology, the LP and the specialist review at a single attendance. State in the first line what the syndrome is and how fast it is moving. Send your tier-A results on paper so nothing is repeated. Name the sequences you want — "MRI with coronal hippocampal views and SWI" — because a routine protocol may not include them. For a patient travelling several hours from a district in Isan, a wasted trip is often the reason the diagnosis is never completed at all.
On plasma p-tau217, since you will be asked about it. The evidence is now strong: in a prospective study of over 1200 patients, the plasma APS2 test reached an AUC of 0.97 with PPV 91% and NPV 92% in primary care — against clinical diagnostic accuracy of 61% for primary care physicians and 73% for dementia specialists. A fully automated platform achieved 85% accuracy in primary care and 89–91% in secondary care, rising to 92–94% with a two-cutoff approach that leaves 12–17% of results in an intermediate band. Accuracy fell somewhat over age 80 but was unaffected by chronic kidney disease, diabetes, sex or APOE genotype. Two caveats that matter more than the numbers. First, the test reports amyloid pathology, which is common in the old and does not by itself prove that pathology is causing this patient's symptoms — a positive test in someone whose syndrome is clearly frontotemporal or vascular has told you about a copathology, not a diagnosis. Second, it is licensed for patients already under evaluation for MCI or early dementia, not for screening the worried well.

4 Reversible & contributing causes

▎Key points
  • Fully reversible dementia is uncommon; reversible contribution to an existing dementia is very common, and that is what you are hunting.
  • Run the screen on every patient, including those with an obvious degenerative syndrome.
  • Drugs, depression, sleep and sensory loss account for more treatable disability than all the rare causes combined.
  • Reassess after treating — improvement confirms the contribution; failure to improve is itself useful information.

Do not stop at the first finding. Cognitive impairment in the old is usually multifactorial — the AHA's 2025 statement makes exactly this point, reframing age-related cognitive impairment as a multiple-cause process. Finding a low B12 does not mean you have explained the patient. Treat it, and keep looking.

5 Bedside cognitive testing

▎Key points
  • Pick a test you can administer the same way every time, and record the raw score with the date so change can be measured.
  • MoCA is more sensitive for MCI and for executive and visuospatial deficits; MMSE is weak at both ends and is heavily affected by education.
  • Correct for education and language, and test through the patient's hearing aid and glasses. A Thai-language validated version matters more than the theoretical superiority of any instrument.
  • Test attention before you interpret memory. If attention is impaired, you are probably looking at delirium.

The cueing test that separates two kinds of memory failure. Give three words. At delayed recall, if the patient cannot produce them, offer a category cue ("one was a fruit") and then a recognition list. In Alzheimer disease the cue does not help — the information was never stored. In executive or retrieval-type failure (vascular, depression, FTD, PDD) cueing and recognition rescue the answer, because the material was stored but cannot be retrieved. This single manoeuvre costs thirty seconds and is more informative than the total score.
Digital and self-administered testing. A self-administered digital battery tested in primary care achieved 85% accuracy with a single cutoff — significantly better than the primary care physicians' own assessment at 73%, and better than MMSE, MoCA and Mini-Cog. Combined with a blood biomarker it identified biomarker-verified clinical AD with 90% accuracy, versus 70% for standard of care. This is a signal of where screening is heading, but it is not yet a reason to abandon a validated local paper test that your staff can administer reliably in Thai.

6 Mild cognitive impairment

▎Key points
  • Objective impairment in ≥1 cognitive domain with independence preserved.
  • Outcomes are genuinely heterogeneous — a proportion progress, a proportion stay stable, and a meaningful proportion revert to normal, usually those whose MCI was driven by a treatable contributor.
  • Amnestic MCI predicts Alzheimer disease; non-amnestic points to vascular, Lewy body or frontotemporal disease.
  • There is no drug treatment for MCI — cholinesterase inhibitors do not prevent progression. What works is treating the contributors and managing vascular risk.

What to actually do. Run the full tier-A reversible screen. Review and reduce the drug list. Treat depression, sleep apnoea and hearing loss. Manage blood pressure, diabetes, lipids and smoking. Encourage physical activity, social engagement and cognitive activity. Discuss driving honestly, and review at 6–12 months with the same test, recorded the same way. Explain the genuine uncertainty rather than either reassuring falsely or delivering a pre-diagnosis of Alzheimer disease.

PD-MCI is a specific entity. The MDS Task Force review found that in Parkinson disease, single-domain MCI is more common than multiple-domain, and non-amnestic single-domain is more common than amnestic — the opposite emphasis to the general MCI literature. Executive, attentional and visuospatial deficits dominate. This matters because the pattern of cognitive failure in a parkinsonian patient is not the pattern you screen for with a memory-weighted test: use a MoCA rather than an MMSE.

7 Alzheimer disease

▎Key points
  • The commonest dementia. Typical presentation is insidious, progressive episodic memory loss not rescued by cueing, later joined by language, visuospatial and executive decline.
  • Atypical presentations are not rare, especially in young-onset disease: posterior cortical atrophy (visual), logopenic PPA (language), dysexecutive and behavioural variants.
  • Pathology is amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, with tau burden and spread tracking clinical severity far more closely than amyloid does.
  • Copathology is the rule, not the exception, particularly over 80 — vascular disease, Lewy bodies and TDP-43 commonly coexist.

Tube feeding does not help in advanced dementia. It does not prolong life, prevent aspiration or improve pressure sores, and it adds restraint, discomfort and complications. Careful hand feeding with modified consistency, upright positioning and attention to oral care is the right answer. Have this conversation early, before a crisis admission forces it at the bedside at 2 a.m.

8 Biomarkers & the revised criteria

▎Key points
  • The 2024 Alzheimer's Association revision defines AD biologically, by biomarkers, independent of symptoms — a research-driven change with real clinical consequences.
  • Tau-PET became a core biomarker, and its spatial extent defines biological stages; these are reported separately from clinical stages.
  • Core 1 biomarkers (amyloid PET, CSF Aβ42:40, p-tau217 and equivalents) establish that AD pathology is present. Core 2 (tau-PET, some fluid markers) tracks stage and prognosis.
  • Biological and clinical stage often disagree — and the discordance is informative. Patients more impaired than their tau burden predicts usually have copathology.
The two-axis framework, in plain terms. A specialist letter may now describe a patient as, say, biologically stage B with clinical stage 3. The biological axis runs from initial (amyloid-positive only) through to advanced (amyloid-positive with elevated and widespread tau). The clinical axis runs from cognitively unimpaired through MCI to dementia. A BioFINDER-2 and ADNI analysis of 1218 amyloid-positive participants with tau-PET found that people whose cognitive impairment outstripped their tau burden were more likely to carry copathologies and comorbidities — which is why the authors argue for measuring non-AD biomarkers in exactly those patients. The practical translation: when a patient looks worse than their AD biomarkers say they should, go looking for the second disease rather than assuming aggressive Alzheimer disease.

The central caution about biological diagnosis. Amyloid positivity rises steeply with age and is present in a substantial proportion of cognitively normal older people. A positive biomarker in a patient whose syndrome is clearly frontotemporal, vascular or functional tells you they have a copathology — it does not rename their illness. Biomarkers answer "is AD pathology present?"; only the clinical syndrome answers "is AD pathology causing this?" Do not let a test result overrule a careful history.

9 Anti-amyloid therapy

▎Key points
  • Lecanemab and donanemab hold full FDA approval for early symptomatic AD — MCI or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid pathology. The EMA refused donanemab, and European access remains restricted.
  • They slow decline modestly; they do not stop, reverse or cure. The honest framing is a delay measured in months over 18 months of treatment, whose day-to-day meaning for an individual patient is debated.
  • ARIA is the defining risk, it is common, and it is strongly dose-related to APOE ε4.
  • They require infusions, serial MRI monitoring, amyloid confirmation and a service able to manage ARIA — which is why access, not evidence, is the binding constraint for most of the world.
ARIA with donanemab — pooled analysis of 3030 participants (JAMA Neurology 2025). ARIA-E occurred in 24.4% of donanemab-treated participants in the placebo-controlled trials (19.8% in the open-label addendum) versus 1.9% on placebo. ARIA-H occurred in 31.3% versus 13.0%. Most ARIA-E was mild or moderate; serious ARIA-E 1.5%, symptomatic ARIA-E 5.8% — most often headache and confusion. 58.3% of first ARIA-E events occurred by the third infusion (about month 3). Independent risk factors: APOE ε4 allele number, more microhaemorrhages, cortical superficial siderosis, higher amyloid plaque burden and elevated mean arterial pressure; antihypertensive use was associated with reduced risk.
Lecanemab in real-world practice (JAMA Neurology 2025). 234 patients treated at a single specialty memory clinic over 14 months. Infusion-related reactions in 37%, typically mild. Of 194 patients at risk, 22% developed at least one ARIA finding — 15% ARIA-E with or without ARIA-H, 6.7% isolated ARIA-H. ARIA-E clustered early (11% by 2 months, 13% by 4 months) and was rare after 6 months, while isolated ARIA-H accrued slowly. ARIA-E rates by genotype: 38% in APOE ε4 homozygotes, 15% in heterozygotes. The authors' conclusion was that the adverse-event burden was manageable in a specialist setting — which is precisely the qualifier to carry into any discussion about offering it elsewhere.
What this means for your practice. You are unlikely to be prescribing these drugs, but you will be asked about them — by families who have read the news, and by patients considering paying for private treatment. Three things to say clearly. They are for early disease only: MCI or mild dementia with biomarker-confirmed amyloid, not moderate or advanced dementia, where there is no evidence of benefit and clear evidence of harm. APOE ε4 homozygotes carry substantially higher ARIA risk, and genotyping before treatment is now standard. Anticoagulation is a serious concern given the microhaemorrhage and siderosis risk. A patient several hours from an MRI scanner and a centre that can manage ARIA is not a candidate, however early their disease.

Therapies that did not work — and why that is useful

Semaglutide in early AD — evoke and evoke+ (2026). 3808 participants randomised to oral semaglutide 14 mg or placebo for up to 156 weeks. Mean age 72.2, mean baseline CDR-SB 3.7. Change in CDR-SB at week 104: 2.3 vs 2.3 in evoke (difference −0.08, 95% CI −0.35 to 0.20, p = 0.57) and 2.2 vs 2.1 in evoke+ (difference 0.10, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.38, p = 0.46). Both trials were discontinued for negative clinical outcome. GLP-1 agonists do not treat Alzheimer disease. Say so when asked.
Valacyclovir in early symptomatic AD (JAMA 2026). 120 HSV-seropositive participants. At 78 weeks the change in ADAS-Cog was 10.86 with valacyclovir versus 6.92 with placebo — a between-group difference of 3.93 (95% CI 1.03 to 6.83, p = 0.01) favouring placebo, i.e. greater cognitive worsening on treatment; function (ADCS-ADL) also favoured placebo. Not efficacious, and not recommended. The herpes hypothesis remains biologically interesting and therapeutically empty.
A useful thing to tell families. Lower post-treatment amyloid correlates with slower clinical progression across the donanemab trial population (R² 0.87 for CDR-SB), and with falls in p-tau217, p-tau181 and GFAP — so the biology is real and the target is right. That is genuinely encouraging for the future. It is also compatible with the present reality that the clinical effect size is modest, the monitoring burden is heavy, and for most patients in most of the world the intervention that will change their next five years is treating their blood pressure, their hearing and their depression.

10 Dementia with Lewy bodies & Parkinson disease dementia

▎Key points
  • Four core features: fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, and spontaneous parkinsonism.
  • The one-year rule separates the labels: dementia within a year of parkinsonism (or before it) is DLB; dementia arising in established Parkinson disease is PDD. The biology is the same and the treatment is the same.
  • Severe neuroleptic sensitivity is a supportive feature and a potentially fatal trap.
  • DLB is frequently misdiagnosed, is more disabling than other dementias at equivalent stage, and carries frequent copathology — cerebral microbleeds are actually more prevalent in DLB (36%) than in AD (32%), against 5–15% in controls.
FeatureWhat to ask or look for

Neuroleptic sensitivity can kill. Haloperidol and other typical antipsychotics — and risperidone — can precipitate severe rigidity, irreversible parkinsonism, autonomic collapse and death in DLB. Before you give any antipsychotic to an agitated elderly patient with hallucinations, ask whether this could be Lewy body disease. If you must treat psychosis, the answer is quetiapine or clozapine (or pimavanserin where available), at low dose, after first removing anticholinergics and dopaminergic drugs and looking for a reversible trigger — infection, pain, constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, a new drug.
Psychosis management in Lewy body dementia, in order. First, deprescribe the offending agents — anticholinergics above all, then dopaminergic drugs. Second, look for a reversible trigger. Third, non-pharmacological measures, including light, routine and reduced visual ambiguity (patterned wallpaper and poor lighting genuinely generate hallucinations). Fourth, and only then, drugs: a cholinesterase inhibitor is the most useful pharmacological step and often reduces hallucinations, then quetiapine, clozapine or pimavanserin. Avoid all first-generation antipsychotics.

Treatment beyond psychosis. Cholinesterase inhibitors work better in DLB and PDD than in Alzheimer disease — rivastigmine has the best evidence, and the response can be striking. Treat RBD by making the bedroom safe first, then melatonin, then clonazepam. Manage orthostatic hypotension. Levodopa may help the parkinsonism but can worsen hallucinations, so titrate slowly and accept a lower motor target. Avoid anticholinergics entirely, including for bladder symptoms.

11 Frontotemporal dementia & primary progressive aphasia

▎Key points
  • A leading cause of young-onset dementia. Pooled global prevalence is around 30 per 100,000, though heterogeneity between studies is extreme and that figure should be treated as an order of magnitude, not a precise number.
  • Two broad presentations: disorders of social behaviour (bvFTD) and disorders of language (PPA).
  • Three genes — C9orf72, GRN, MAPT — account for a substantial proportion of familial cases, with major implications for families and a role for presymptomatic counselling.
  • The main differential is psychiatric. bvFTD is repeatedly diagnosed as late-onset depression, bipolar disorder or a midlife crisis for years before the diagnosis is made.

The questions that find bvFTD. Direct them to the informant, not the patient, whose insight is characteristically absent. Has he become tactless or said hurtful things he would never have said? Has she stopped caring about people she used to care about? Any new rigid routines, hoarding, or checking? Any change in what he eats — particularly a new sweet tooth, or eating things he would not previously have eaten? Any new indifference to hygiene or appearance? Apathy plus loss of empathy plus dietary change is a far more specific combination than any single feature.
There is no disease-modifying treatment, and cholinesterase inhibitors do not help — they may worsen behaviour. Management is behavioural and environmental, with SSRIs for compulsions, disinhibition and eating behaviour, and antipsychotics used sparingly and at low dose. The most valuable things you provide are the diagnosis itself (which reframes years of family conflict), carer support, financial and legal protection while capacity allows — these patients make catastrophic financial decisions — and genetic counselling for the family.

12 Vascular cognitive impairment

▎Key points
  • Spans the range from mild vascular cognitive impairment to vascular dementia, and mixed AD–vascular pathology is probably the commonest substrate of dementia in the old.
  • Typical profile is executive and attentional slowing with retrieval-type memory failure that is helped by cueing — different from the storage failure of AD.
  • The AHA's 2025 statement reframes cognitive ageing as a multiple-cause process: reducing vascular risk alone does not fully restore brain health, but it remains the most effective strategy available.
  • Look for the pattern: stepwise or abrupt change, early gait disturbance, urinary symptoms, pseudobulbar affect, focal signs, and vascular risk factors.

What to do. Treat blood pressure — this is the intervention with the best evidence for preventing cognitive decline. Treat diabetes and lipids, stop smoking, treat atrial fibrillation appropriately, and prescribe physical activity, which the AHA statement singles out as the most effective available strategy for preserving brain health. Cholinesterase inhibitors have marginal effects in pure vascular dementia, but where mixed pathology is likely — which is most patients — a trial is reasonable. Treat depression, which is common and adds substantially to disability.

Do not dismiss white-matter hyperintensities as incidental. Their volume correlates with worse cognition, in Lewy body disease as well as in pure vascular disease. A report of "moderate small-vessel ischaemic change" in a patient with executive slowing is a finding, not background noise — and it is the one dementia substrate you can genuinely modify.

13 Rapidly progressive dementia

▎Key points
  • Dementia progressing over weeks to months rather than years. This is a different clinical pathway with a different tempo of investigation.
  • Assume it is treatable until proven otherwise. The prion diagnosis is the one you make last, not first.
  • Mnemonic: VITAMINS — Vascular, Infectious, Toxic-metabolic, Autoimmune, Metastatic/neoplastic, Iatrogenic, Neurodegenerative, Systemic/seizures.
  • Escalate the investigation tier. What is tier D in a slowly progressive dementia becomes tier B here: MRI, LP and EEG need to happen in days, not months, and that justifies transfer.

Two reversible causes that get missed in exactly this scenario. Non-convulsive status epilepticus — a fluctuating, confused, sometimes agitated elderly patient with subtle facial or limb twitching. An EEG takes an hour and changes everything. Autoimmune encephalitis — subacute cognitive decline with seizures, psychiatric symptoms or a movement disorder, where the antibody result takes weeks and the treatment window does not wait. If the clinical criteria are met, treat.

14 Autoimmune encephalitis

▎Key points
  • Subacute (under 3 months) working memory deficit, altered mental status or psychiatric symptoms, plus supporting evidence — focal CNS findings, new seizures, CSF pleocytosis or suggestive MRI — and reasonable exclusion of alternatives.
  • Misdiagnosis is now the recognised problem. It arises from not applying the clinical requirements, from inadequate assessment of MRI and CSF inflammation, and from over-reliance on narrow cell-based antibody panels.
  • "Antibody-negative autoimmune encephalitis" is a restrictive category, not a fallback. It requires that alternatives be excluded and that inflammatory evidence be present — not merely that the panel came back negative in a patient you suspect.
  • Treat on clinical criteria; do not wait for antibodies. But equally, do not treat a patient who does not meet them.

Autoimmune encephalitis-associated epilepsy. A subset of patients develop seizures that persist and resist immunotherapy — temporal lobe epilepsy with GAD antibodies, epilepsy with high-risk paraneoplastic antibodies, and epilepsy following adequately treated surface-antibody encephalitis. These share a mechanism of structural injury and focal neuronal loss, typically cytotoxic T-cell mediated. The practical point: once that damage is established, the treatment goal shifts from immunotherapy to antiseizure management, and continuing to escalate immunosuppression in a patient whose active inflammation has resolved exposes them to harm without benefit.
The discipline that prevents misdiagnosis. Autoimmune encephalitis is reversible, which is exactly why it is over-diagnosed — the temptation to "just try steroids" in an undiagnosed deteriorating patient is strong. Apply the criteria as written. Require genuine evidence of inflammation. Exclude the alternatives, particularly prion disease, CNS lymphoma, infection and a rapidly progressive degenerative dementia. A negative panel in a patient with no inflammatory markers and a normal MRI is evidence against, not evidence for antibody-negative disease.

15 Prion disease

▎Key points
  • Sporadic CJD accounts for 85–90% of prion disease, affecting 1–2 per million per year, with most patients dying within 5–6 months of onset — though survival ranges from weeks to several years.
  • Classic triad: rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, and ataxia, with visual, pyramidal and extrapyramidal signs, evolving to akinetic mutism.
  • MRI DWI is the most useful test — cortical ribboning and basal ganglia hyperintensity. RT-QuIC on CSF has transformed antemortem diagnostic accuracy.
  • A psychiatric prodrome is common and can long precede motor signs, which is why so many patients are first treated for depression.
The prodrome is longer than we taught. An Austrian case-control study linking autopsy-confirmed sCJD cases to national insurance claims found evidence of mood alterations as early as 3 years before symptom onset, with increased psychotropic prescribing compared to matched controls. This is not a screening tool — sCJD remains an extraordinarily rare cause of low mood — but it explains the very common history of a patient treated for depression for months before the neurological picture declared itself, and it argues for vigilance when mood change is followed by any neurological sign.
Atypical imaging happens. The classic pattern is cortical ribboning with basal ganglia involvement, but restricted diffusion can be strikingly focal — an isolated diffuse precentral gyrus signal has been reported in a patient presenting with limb weakness and involuntary movements. If the clinical picture is a rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus, an unusual DWI distribution does not exclude the diagnosis; repeat the MRI, because the pattern typically evolves and spreads.

Practical management. There is no treatment. What you provide is a secure diagnosis so families stop searching, symptom control (clonazepam or levetiracetam for myoclonus), early palliative involvement, and realistic prognostic information. Notify according to national requirements. Standard infection-control precautions apply for routine care, but neurosurgical and ophthalmic instruments require special handling — tell the surgical team before any procedure, including biopsy.

16 Young-onset dementia

▎Key points
  • Onset before 65. The differential is much broader than in late-onset disease, and a far higher proportion is genetic, metabolic, inflammatory, infective or toxic.
  • Diagnostic delay is the defining problem, and the recurring question is "is this young-onset dementia or is this psychiatric?"
  • Presentation is more often behaviour, language, personality change and executive dysfunction than pure memory loss.
  • Investigate more, and more aggressively: neuroimaging, LP, formal neuropsychology and genetic testing are all justified here in a way they are not at 82.

Never diagnose young-onset dementia without excluding Wilson disease, HIV, syphilis, B12 deficiency and thyroid disease — every one of which is on your tier-A or tier-C list and every one of which is treatable. And in a patient under 50 with cognitive and psychiatric change, especially with any movement disorder or abnormal liver function, actively pursue Wilson disease with caeruloplasmin, 24-hour urinary copper and a slit-lamp examination.
The care needs are different, and that is the part most often neglected. These patients are typically in work, often the household earner, frequently with dependent children, and services designed for the elderly fit badly. Practical priorities: employment and income protection before capacity is lost; driving; a plan for talking to children, whose distress is often invisible in the consultation; genetic counselling for the family; and age-appropriate day services rather than a placement among people thirty years older. Most will live a decade or more, largely in the community.

17 Special populations

Down syndrome

Very high lifetime risk of Alzheimer disease, driven by trisomy of chromosome 21 and therefore of APP, with onset typically decades earlier than in the general population. Diagnosis is genuinely difficult because of pre-existing cognitive impairment, so change from the individual's own baseline is the only usable standard — which requires that a baseline was recorded. A systematic review of studies from 2014–2024 found that early dementia in Down syndrome presents with decline in executive function, memory, language and attention together with behavioural change, and that behavioural symptoms — agitation, hallucinations — may be the earliest detectable markers, appearing before measurable cognitive decline. Use informant-based instruments designed for intellectual disability rather than standard cognitive screens, and establish a baseline in adulthood so future change can be measured.

Parkinson disease

See §10 for PDD. For PD-MCI, the MDS Task Force review is the reference point: single-domain impairment is more common than multiple-domain, and non-amnestic is more common than amnestic. Screen with an instrument that tests executive and visuospatial function.

The cerebellum and cognition

Worth knowing because it is counter-intuitive and gets missed. The cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome — impairments of executive control, attention, working memory, visuospatial processing, language fluency and social cognition, with flattened or disinhibited affect — arises from disruption of cerebrocerebellar networks. It is now recognised in Chiari I malformation, where cognitive and affective disturbance is common and correlates poorly with radiographic severity, tonsillar descent or pain. The practical implication: in a patient with a posterior fossa abnormality and cognitive complaints, do not assume the two are unrelated, and do not assume that a "mild" structural finding excludes a real cognitive syndrome.

Focal cortical syndromes

Occasionally a cognitive presentation is genuinely focal rather than diffuse. Auditory agnosia — impaired recognition of sounds with normal hearing — may be global, verbal (strictly associated with language-dominant hemisphere lesions) or non-verbal; global forms usually follow bilateral temporal damage. It is rare, but it is the kind of deficit that gets recorded as "confused" or as deafness. Similarly, apraxia of eyelid opening belongs with PSP, CBS and advanced PD, and should be distinguished from awakening ptosis — a benign, self-limiting entity confined to waking from sleep, resolving within minutes or on mechanical lid elevation, typically in women around 60, usually remitting spontaneously within 18 months.

18 Neuropsychiatric symptoms

▎Key points
  • Apathy is the commonest neuropsychiatric symptom — 45% at baseline in meta-analysis, ahead of irritability and depression at 35% each — and it is the most under-recorded because it does not disturb anybody but the patient.
  • Most symptoms increase over time; agitation (+16%) and apathy (+15%) emerge most at long-term follow-up, while sleep disturbance tends to improve.
  • Always look for a trigger first: pain, infection, constipation, urinary retention, dehydration, a new drug, sensory deprivation, an environmental change.
  • Non-pharmacological measures first, always. Antipsychotics carry increased mortality and stroke risk in dementia, and are contraindicated in DLB.

Before prescribing an antipsychotic in dementia, state the indication and the review date in the notes. They increase mortality and stroke risk, and once started they are rarely stopped. Ask: what is the behaviour, what triggers it, who is it harming, what has been tried, and when will I review? If you cannot answer all five, the prescription is not ready.
Apathy is not depression, and the distinction changes treatment. The apathetic patient has lost motivation and initiation but does not report sadness, guilt, hopelessness or self-criticism. The depressed patient does. Families routinely describe both as "he's given up", and antidepressants prescribed for apathy alone tend to add side effects without benefit. Ask specifically about mood, tearfulness, self-blame and wishes to be dead — and note that apathy in AD also predicts faster functional decline, so recording it matters even when there is no good drug for it.

19 Symptomatic treatment & care

▎Key points
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) give modest symptomatic benefit in AD, and work better in DLB and PDD.
  • Memantine for moderate to severe AD, alone or added to a cholinesterase inhibitor. Not useful in mild disease, FTD or pure vascular dementia.
  • Avoid cholinesterase inhibitors in FTD — they may worsen behaviour.
  • Most of what determines quality of life is not pharmacological: carer support, safety, activity, continence, nutrition, and planning done early.

The non-drug work, which matters more

  • Carer support. Carer strain predicts institutionalisation more reliably than the patient's cognitive score. Ask the carer directly how they are managing, every visit. Arrange respite before crisis.
  • Driving. Raise it early, before it becomes a confrontation. Dementia is not automatically a bar at diagnosis, but progression makes it one; document the discussion.
  • Legal and financial planning. Power of attorney, wills and advance directives while the patient retains capacity. In FTD this is urgent — catastrophic financial decisions are part of the disease.
  • Safety. Gas and cooking, medication supervision, wandering, firearms, scams and financial exploitation.
  • Nutrition, continence and mobility, all of which deteriorate predictably and all of which respond to anticipation.
  • Advance care planning. Hospitalisation preferences, antibiotics, feeding — decided in clinic with the patient, not in the emergency department without them.

20 Risk reduction

▎Key points
  • A substantial proportion of dementia risk is attributable to modifiable factors — which is the most hopeful fact in this module.
  • The Nottingham consensus produced 56 policy recommendations across public health messaging, individual interventions, population interventions and research, with health inequality as a priority across all four domains.
  • The AHA's 2025 position: reducing vascular risk alone does not fully restore brain health, but intensifying control of modifiable vascular risk factors — physical activity above all — remains the most effective available strategy.
  • Risk reduction is worth raising with every patient and every family member, because the family are asking about their own risk even when they do not say so.

The inequality point is the one to carry into local practice. The Nottingham panel made addressing health inequalities a priority across every domain, because prevention campaigns reliably reach the people who least need them. In a district setting the corollary is concrete: hearing aids, blood-pressure control and treating depression are the interventions most likely to be missed in the poorest patients and most likely to help them, and they cost very little.

The tier-A screen, as a checklist

FBC · renal function and electrolytes including calcium · liver function · glucose or HbA1c · TSH · vitamin B12 · HIV serology · syphilis serology · CRP · full drug chart including over-the-counter · depression screen · hearing and vision check · ECG. Plus CT head (tier B) if rapid progression, head injury, anticoagulation, focal signs, seizures, headache, age under 65, or gait and urinary change.

Five things not to do

  1. Do not diagnose dementia in a delirious patient, or during an acute admission.
  2. Do not give haloperidol or risperidone to a patient who might have Lewy body disease.
  3. Do not prescribe a cholinesterase inhibitor in FTD.
  4. Do not offer tube feeding in advanced dementia.
  5. Do not accept a single reversible finding as the whole explanation — keep looking.
