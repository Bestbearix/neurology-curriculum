Why this module?

Dementia is the most common chronic neurological diagnosis in primary care, and the workup is one of the most under-done. Most patients have Alzheimer disease, but a meaningful minority have a reversible contributor or a non-AD diagnosis with very different management (e.g. DLB — never give haloperidol; FTD — cholinesterase inhibitors don't help; NPH — a shunt may transform life). This module covers the structured approach plus the 2023–2024 anti-amyloid antibody update.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Distinguish normal aging, MCI, dementia, depression-related cognitive decline, and delirium.
  • Run the standard dementia workup including the reversible-cause panel.
  • Recognise the cognitive profile that points to AD, FTD (with its variants), DLB, vascular, NPH.
  • Identify the four core features of DLB and avoid catastrophic neuroleptic prescriptions.
  • Prescribe cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine appropriately.
  • Discuss the 2023–2024 anti-amyloid mAb landscape (lecanemab, donanemab) and ARIA.
  • Manage BPSD non-pharmacologically first; deploy antipsychotics judiciously when needed.
The single most important question at every dementia visit: "Can the patient still independently do their usual activities of daily living?" If yes — MCI. If no — dementia. The functional decline is what makes the diagnosis.

Lessons

1. Normal aging → SCD → MCI → dementia
  • Normal cognitive aging — mild slowing, word-finding pauses; tests within age-norms.
  • Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) — the patient feels something is off; testing normal. Slightly elevated future risk.
  • MCI — objective deficit in ≥ 1 domain; ADLs preserved; complex IADLs may be slowed. Annual progression to dementia ~10–15%.
  • Dementia — significant deficit + impaired ADLs. (DSM-5: major neurocognitive disorder.)

The six cognitive domains: complex attention · executive function · learning & memory · language · visuospatial · social cognition.

2. Approach to memory complaints

History from patient + informant: tempo, pattern, domain of onset, functional decline, behavioural/psychiatric features, sleep (RBD!), motor symptoms, vascular risks, medications (anticholinergics!), mood, family history.

Bedside screen: MoCA preferred over MMSE for MCI and executive dysfunction; AD8 or IQCODE if patient lacks insight.

Examination: parkinsonism, apraxia, alien limb, myoclonus, focal weakness, ataxia, downgaze palsy, gait.

Lab: CBC, electrolytes, calcium, glucose, U&E, LFT, TSH, B12, syphilis (where prevalent), HIV (if risk).

Imaging: MRI preferred. Look for medial temporal atrophy (AD), frontal/anterior temporal (FTD), occipital deficits (DLB), big ventricles (NPH), white-matter disease (vascular).

3. Reversible causes — never miss
  • Depression (pseudodementia)
  • Delirium — acute, fluctuating, inattentive (always look for trigger)
  • Medications — anticholinergics, BZDs, opioids, lithium
  • B12, thiamine, thyroid disease, metabolic derangement
  • NPH — magnetic gait + incontinence + cognition; shuntable
  • Subdural haematoma (especially elderly anticoagulated)
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea (CPAP can reverse)
  • Neurosyphilis, HIV-associated, autoimmune encephalitis
Rapidly progressive (<1 year) demands urgent workup: CJD (DWI ribboning + RT-QuIC), autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (subacute + temporal MRI changes + Ab panel), CNS vasculitis, paraneoplastic.
4. Alzheimer disease

Most common dementia. Pathology: β-amyloid plaques + tau neurofibrillary tangles. APOE ε4 is the strongest sporadic risk factor.

Clinical: insidious onset; episodic memory first (cueing doesn't help — storage failure); language and visuospatial decline; BPSD; gait apraxia and incontinence late. Course 8–10 years average.

Atypical presentations: posterior cortical atrophy (visual), logopenic PPA, frontal/dysexecutive variant.

Diagnosis by clinical + MRI medial temporal atrophy; biomarkers (amyloid PET, CSF Aβ42/p-tau, emerging plasma p-tau217) confirm biology — required for anti-amyloid mAb therapy.

Symptomatic treatment: donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine (cholinesterase inhibitors) for mild–severe AD; memantine for moderate–severe AD. Modest benefit; don't slow disease.

5. Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)

Four core features (Lewy Body Consortium 4th criteria, 2017):

  1. Fluctuating cognition with marked variation in attention/alertness
  2. Recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations
  3. REM sleep behaviour disorder
  4. Parkinsonism (symmetric, less tremor-predominant than PD)

1-year rule: if cognitive impairment within 1 year of (or before) parkinsonism → DLB; if > 1 year after established PD → PDD.

Supportive: severe neuroleptic sensitivity (catastrophic — never give haloperidol blindly), repeated falls, autonomic dysfunction, hyposmia, depression, paranoid delusions.

Biomarker: DaT-SPECT reduced.

Treatment: cholinesterase inhibitor (rivastigmine or donepezil) first-line — often dramatic for hallucinations and cognition; low-dose levodopa for parkinsonism; melatonin for RBD; quetiapine / clozapine / pimavanserin (NOT haloperidol or risperidone) for refractory psychosis.

Neuroleptic sensitivity in DLB can precipitate severe parkinsonism, autonomic instability, NMS-like syndrome, or death. Always ask about hallucinations / fluctuations / RBD before prescribing any antipsychotic to an older adult.
6. Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)

Most common dementia under 60. Three main phenotypes:

VariantHallmark
bvFTD (behavioural variant)Disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, perseverative behaviours, dietary changes; executive dysfunction
nfvPPA (non-fluent / agrammatic)Effortful agrammatic speech, apraxia of speech; left inferior frontal atrophy
svPPA (semantic variant)Loss of word meaning, fluent empty speech; bilateral anterior temporal atrophy

lvPPA (logopenic) is usually AD, not FTD. ~40% of FTD is familial; C9orf72 is the most common gene (also causes ALS — FTD-ALS overlap).

Treatment: no disease-modifying therapy. Cholinesterase inhibitors do NOT help (and may worsen behaviour). SSRIs may reduce disinhibition / dietary excess. Speech therapy for PPA. Caregiver support is critical — bvFTD is exceptionally hard on families.

7. Vascular cognitive impairment

Spectrum from multi-infarct dementia (stepwise) to subcortical ischaemic dementia (Binswanger — executive dysfunction, gait apraxia, urgency, pseudobulbar affect).

Best evidence intervention: aggressive vascular risk-factor control (BP < 130/80, statin, antiplatelet, DM control, smoking cessation, exercise). Cholinesterase inhibitors + memantine offer modest benefit, less than in AD.

CADASIL — young patient with migraine with aura → recurrent lacunar strokes → vascular dementia; striking white matter disease with anterior temporal pole involvement; NOTCH3 mutation.

8. BPSD — treat causes first, drugs last

Always look for triggers: pain (constipation, UTI, dental, fracture), infection, drug-related (anticholinergics, opioids, BZD withdrawal), hunger/thirst/elimination, environmental, sensory impairment.

Non-pharmacological first: routine, orientation aids, structured activity, music, environmental modifications, sleep hygiene, treat sensory impairment, address pain proactively.

Pharmacological (when there is risk of harm): SSRI for depression / anxiety / agitation; trazodone; quetiapine or risperidone at lowest dose for psychosis; brexpiprazole (FDA-approved 2023 for agitation in AD). Avoid haloperidol, benzodiazepines, Z-drugs.

All antipsychotics carry an FDA black-box mortality warning in dementia (~1.5–1.7×). Use lowest dose, shortest duration, with clear target and regular review for de-prescribing.
9. 2023–2024 anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies

Lecanemab (Leqembi) — full FDA approval July 2023. Donanemab (Kisunla) — full FDA approval July 2024. Both approved for early symptomatic AD (MCI due to AD or mild AD dementia) with amyloid confirmation.

Magnitude of benefit: ~27% slowing of cognitive decline on CDR-SB at 18 months (CLARITY-AD); similar with donanemab (TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2). Modest — slowing, not stopping.

ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) is the major safety issue:

  • ARIA-E (oedema) — ~12% lecanemab, ~24% donanemab
  • ARIA-H (haemorrhage) — ~17% lecanemab, ~31% donanemab
  • Symptomatic ARIA ~3% — rare deaths reported, especially with concomitant anticoagulation or thrombolysis
  • APOE ε4 homozygotes have much higher rates (up to 30% ARIA-E with lecanemab)

Pre-treatment MRI required for microbleeds and siderosis; serial MRI monitoring required during therapy.

Aducanumab (Aduhelm) was withdrawn in 2024.

Stop anti-amyloid mAb before IV thrombolysis for stroke — ICH risk is dramatically increased. Patients with AIS on lecanemab/donanemab may need to be excluded from tPA.
10. When to refer
Refer to neurology / memory clinic: diagnostic uncertainty · atypical presentation · age < 65 · rapid progression · prominent psychiatric or motor features · suspected reversible cause needing specialist workup (NPH, autoimmune encephalitis, CJD) · family history of early-onset dementia · for anti-amyloid mAb consideration · BPSD refractory to first-line measures · driving fitness assessment.
Refer to old-age psychiatry: severe behavioural disturbance, suicidal ideation, need for inpatient assessment, complex pharmacology.

Case Vignettes

Case 1 — A 72-year-old retired teacher with 2 years of memory complaints

Her daughter brings her in. She forgets recent conversations, misplaces items, repeats questions. She still cooks and drives short familiar routes, manages medications with a pill organiser. MoCA 23/30: lost points on delayed recall (0/5, cueing didn't help), orientation, abstraction. MRI shows medial temporal atrophy. Labs normal including TSH, B12. No depression on screening.

Diagnosis: Probable Alzheimer disease, mild stage (still independent enough that some may classify as MCI due to AD; functional decline tips it toward dementia).

Plan: Start donepezil 5 mg/d, increase to 10 mg after 4–6 weeks if tolerated. Counsel patient and family. Driving evaluation. Advance care planning. Discuss anti-amyloid mAb option at memory clinic if accessible (amyloid PET / CSF confirmation needed; check eligibility incl. anticoagulation, microbleeds).

Case 2 — A 76-year-old with 1 year of fluctuating confusion and "seeing children in the house"

Wife reports good days and bad days. He sees small children, dogs, and "shadow people" several times a week; usually not frightened. Acts out his dreams (kicking, shouting; she sleeps in another room). Slow walking with reduced arm swing. Recent fall. Prescribed haloperidol 1 mg for "agitation" — within hours he became rigid, drooling, mute, with fever.

Diagnosis: Dementia with Lewy bodies with neuroleptic hypersensitivity reaction.

Plan: STOP haloperidol immediately; supportive care (hydration, monitor for NMS). Start rivastigmine patch or donepezil — often dramatic improvement in hallucinations and cognition. Melatonin for RBD; clonazepam if refractory and falls risk is low. Low-dose carbidopa-levodopa for parkinsonism. If psychosis refractory: quetiapine, clozapine, or pimavanserin — never haloperidol, risperidone, or olanzapine. Consider DaT-SPECT if diagnosis uncertain.

Always ask about visual hallucinations, fluctuations, RBD, and parkinsonism before prescribing any antipsychotic to an older patient.
Case 3 — A 58-year-old executive with 18 months of personality change

Family report he has become "rude and selfish": tells colleagues uncomfortable truths, stopped showering, lost interest in his children, eats large amounts of sweets (5 cans of soda a day), repeatedly counts the cars in the parking lot. No memory complaints; MMSE 28/30. MoCA 22/30 (lost executive points). MRI: prominent bilateral frontal and anterior temporal atrophy.

Diagnosis: Behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD).

Plan: Refer to memory clinic / behavioural neurology. Counsel family extensively — caregiver burden is enormous in bvFTD; legal/financial protection (power of attorney). Trial SSRI (sertraline, citalopram, trazodone) for disinhibition and dietary excess. Avoid cholinesterase inhibitors (don't help; may worsen behaviour). Test for C9orf72 expansion (most common genetic cause; also screen for ALS features — FTD-ALS overlap). Driving must be assessed. Workplace and family safety discussion.

Case 4 — A 78-year-old with 6 months of progressive shuffling gait, urinary urgency, and forgetfulness

Gait: short steps, wide base, "feet glued to floor", difficulty initiating; arm swing preserved. Urinary urgency and occasional incontinence. MoCA 21/30 (lost executive and processing speed points; memory relatively preserved). MRI: enlarged ventricles out of proportion to cortical atrophy; preserved hippocampi.

Diagnosis: normal-pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) — classic triad of magnetic gait + cognitive decline + urinary symptoms.

Plan: Refer for neurosurgical evaluation. Diagnostic LP "tap test" with 30–50 mL CSF removal and pre/post gait assessment (timed walk and turns). If gait improves > 10% → ventriculoperitoneal shunt may help; cognition and incontinence improve less reliably.

NPH is one of the few "treatable dementias" — never miss it. Gait improvement is the most reliable post-tap-test predictor of shunt response.
Case 5 — A 65-year-old with 6 weeks of rapidly progressive cognitive decline

Initially noticed by family as "spacing out". Now barely able to converse, with jerky involuntary movements of limbs (myoclonus) precipitated by sudden noise. Ataxic gait. MRI DWI: cortical "ribboning" in the right parietal cortex and bilateral basal ganglia hyperintensity. EEG: periodic sharp wave complexes.

Diagnosis: sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD).

Plan: Confirm with CSF RT-QuIC (highly sensitive and specific). 14-3-3 and total tau supportive. No specific treatment; supportive and palliative care; engage hospice. Counsel family. Disease is universally fatal within 1 year (usually 6 months). Public-health notification per local protocol; standard precautions for surgical instruments in any prior procedures.

Rapidly progressive dementia (< 1 year) demands an urgent extended workup: CJD, autoimmune/limbic encephalitis, paraneoplastic, CNS vasculitis, neurosyphilis, lymphoma, NPH. Don't accept "Alzheimer disease" as the explanation when the tempo doesn't fit.
Case 6 — A 70-year-old man with "memory problems" since a new heart failure regimen

Over 3 months, getting confused, sleeping more, occasional visual hallucinations, hand tremor when reaching. Medications: amiodarone, furosemide, lisinopril, atorvastatin, oxybutynin (new — for urinary urgency), zolpidem nightly, diphenhydramine for "allergies".

Diagnosis: drug-related cognitive impairment, with high anticholinergic burden (oxybutynin + diphenhydramine) and sedative burden (zolpidem) — possibly superimposed on incipient neurodegenerative disease.

Plan: Deprescribe stepwise: stop diphenhydramine; switch oxybutynin to mirabegron (β3 agonist, no anticholinergic effect); taper zolpidem with sleep hygiene support. Reassess cognition in 6–12 weeks. If improved → drug-related (often called "anticholinergic dementia"). If persisting deficits → continue dementia workup (MRI, B12, TSH, MoCA). Many older patients improve dramatically when polypharmacy is reduced.
