Find what is treatable, then name the syndrome

▎What this module is for
  • Establish real decline from a previous level, with a collateral history, before anything else.
  • Run the tier-A reversible screen on every patient — it is the highest-value act in the whole clinic.
  • Separate the syndromes on first domain affected, course, motor signs and hallucinations.
  • Know what changed: plasma p-tau217, the 2024 revised criteria, the anti-amyloid antibodies and their ARIA numbers, and the negative trials you will be asked about.
  • Investigate in urgency tiers, because MRI, CSF biomarkers and antibody panels are not available to you the same day.

The line that defines dementia

Function. MCI is objective impairment with independence preserved; dementia is objective impairment that has cost independence. Test scores do not draw that line — the informant does.

The cueing test

Cue does not help → storage failure → Alzheimer. Cue does help → retrieval failure → vascular, FTD, PDD, depression. Thirty seconds, more useful than the total score.

The delirium rule

Impaired attention means you are testing delirium, not baseline cognition. Never diagnose dementia during an acute admission.

The DLB trap

Haloperidol or risperidone in Lewy body disease can cause irreversible parkinsonism, autonomic collapse and death. Ask before you prescribe.

The apathy fact

45% of patients at baseline — the commonest neuropsychiatric symptom, and the most under-recorded, because it troubles nobody but the patient.

What actually helps

Hearing aids, blood pressure, depression treatment and deprescribing will change your patients' next five years more than any drug in this module.

Five tools here

  • Dementia differentiator — from the first domain affected to a named syndrome, with its workup and its commonest error.
  • Investigation planner — ten scenarios mapped onto the five urgency tiers.
  • Reversible-cause sweep — a tri-state checklist of the tier-A screen, with a live summary.
  • Cognitive screen selector — which instrument for which question, and how to interpret it.
  • Anti-amyloid eligibility checker — walks the criteria and the ARIA risk factors.
How to use this. Run the differentiator and the sweep on real patients from your own clinic list. The flashcards and quiz are for retention. The anti-amyloid tab exists mainly so that when a family arrives having read about a new Alzheimer cure, you can give them accurate numbers in two minutes.

Core lessons

Ten structured lessons. Each opens with what you must be able to do.

1 · The cognitive spectrum
▎You must be able to
  • Place a patient on the normal–SCD–MCI–dementia spectrum using function, not scores.
  • Separate delirium from dementia at the bedside.

Normal ageing — slower recall, normal testing, intact function. SCD — persistent complaint, normal testing, intact function; modest risk increase; screen for depression, sleep, drugs, hearing. MCI — objective impairment in ≥1 domain, independence preserved; heterogeneous outcome and some revert. Dementia — impairment with loss of independence.

Amnestic MCI (true storage failure) predicts Alzheimer disease. Non-amnestic MCI points to vascular, Lewy body or frontotemporal disease.

The commonest error in the field is diagnosing dementia in a delirious patient. Delirium is acute, fluctuates, and impairs attention above all. If serial 7s or months-backwards fail, you are testing attention and the memory result means nothing.
2 · Approach and the domains
▎You must be able to
  • Name the first domain affected and say what it points to.
  • Branch on tempo and on motor signs.

Episodic memory → Alzheimer. Executive/attention → vascular, FTD, DLB/PDD, NPH, depression. Language → PPA variants (logopenic is usually AD). Visuospatial → DLB, posterior cortical atrophy. Behaviour/personality → bvFTD.

Two branches carry almost all the urgent and treatable diagnoses: is it rapidly progressive? and are there motor abnormalities? Myoclonus, ataxia, parkinsonism, vertical gaze palsy, pyramidal signs, gait apraxia and seizures each redirect the differential.

A patient who attends alone complaining of memory loss more often has depression or SCD. The patient brought in by a worried family, insisting nothing is wrong, is the more concerning presentation.
3 · Investigation strategy and urgency
▎You must be able to
  • Recite the tier-A screen from memory.
  • Say when CT is enough and when it is not.

ANow: FBC, renal and electrolytes with Ca, LFT, glucose/HbA1c, TSH, B12, CRP, HIV and syphilis serology, drug chart, depression screen, hearing and vision, ECG.

BToday: CT head if rapid progression, head injury, anticoagulation, focal signs, seizures, headache, age <65, or gait/urinary change; EEG if myoclonus or fluctuating consciousness; LP if fever or rapid progression.

CThis week: autoimmune screen, coeliac serology, sleep-apnoea assessment; thiamine given rather than measured.

DThis month, batched: MRI with coronal hippocampal views and SWI, neuropsychology, CSF or plasma AD biomarkers, antibody panels, genetics, RT-QuIC if prion suspected.

EElective or omit: repeat imaging in stable disease, amyloid-PET where no therapy is accessible, routine EEG in typical slow dementia.

CT rules out the surgical causes — subdural, tumour, hydrocephalus — and that is its job. It will not tell you which degenerative disease this is.
4 · Reversible and contributing causes
▎You must be able to
  • Run the screen on every patient, including obvious degenerative cases.
  • Resist stopping at the first positive finding.

Fully reversible dementia is uncommon; reversible contribution is very common. Drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids), depression, thyroid, B12, thiamine, sleep apnoea, hearing and vision loss, alcohol, subdural haematoma, NPH, HIV and syphilis, metabolic derangement, autoimmune disease.

Test cognition with the hearing aid and glasses in place, or the score is meaningless. Untreated hearing loss is both a major risk factor and a direct cause of apparent cognitive failure in the consulting room.
Cognitive impairment in the old is usually multifactorial — the AHA 2025 statement reframes it explicitly as a multiple-cause process. A low B12 does not explain the patient. Treat it and keep looking.
5 · Bedside cognitive testing
▎You must be able to
  • Pick the right instrument and administer it identically each time.
  • Use the cueing test to classify the memory failure.

Mini-Cog 3 min, minimal education bias. MMSE familiar but weak at both ends with almost no executive testing. MoCA better for MCI, executive and visuospatial — add 1 point if ≤12 years education. Clock drawing and verbal fluency are high-value single tasks. IQCODE/AD8 measure change rather than level, bypassing education bias.

The cueing test. Three words, delayed recall, then a category cue, then recognition. AD: cue does not help — never stored. Vascular/FTD/PDD/depression: cue rescues it — stored but not retrieved.
A self-administered digital battery reached 85% accuracy in primary care versus 73% for the physicians themselves, and 90% for biomarker-verified AD when combined with a blood test versus 70% for standard care. The direction of travel — but not a reason to drop a validated Thai-language paper test your staff can administer reliably.
6 · Alzheimer disease and the revised criteria
▎You must be able to
  • Recognise the atypical presentations.
  • Read a two-axis biological/clinical stage and know what discordance means.

Typical: insidious progressive episodic memory loss not rescued by cueing. Atypical, especially young-onset: posterior cortical atrophy (visual, with normal acuity), logopenic PPA (usually AD pathology), dysexecutive and behavioural variants.

The 2024 revision defines AD biologically. Tau-PET became a core biomarker and its spatial extent defines biological stages, reported separately from clinical stages. Core 1 markers establish pathology; Core 2 tracks stage.

When biological and clinical stage disagree — a patient more impaired than their tau burden predicts — the usual explanation is copathology. The BioFINDER-2/ADNI analysis argues for measuring non-AD biomarkers in exactly those patients. Practically: go looking for the second disease rather than assuming aggressive AD.
Amyloid positivity is common in the old. A positive biomarker in a clearly frontotemporal or vascular syndrome tells you about a copathology, not a diagnosis. Biomarkers answer "is AD pathology present?"; only the syndrome answers "is it causing this?"
7 · Anti-amyloid therapy, honestly
▎You must be able to
  • Quote the ARIA numbers and the APOE effect.
  • Say why semaglutide and valacyclovir are not treatments.
Donanemab ARIA, 3030 participants: ARIA-E 24.4% vs 1.9% placebo; ARIA-H 31.3% vs 13.0%. Serious ARIA-E 1.5%, symptomatic 5.8%. 58.3% of first events by the third infusion. Risk rises with APOE ε4 allele number, microhaemorrhages, cortical superficial siderosis, amyloid burden and mean arterial pressure.
Lecanemab in real-world practice, 234 patients: infusion reactions 37%; 22% had at least one ARIA finding (15% ARIA-E, 6.7% isolated ARIA-H). ARIA-E by genotype: 38% in ε4 homozygotes, 15% in heterozygotes.
evoke / evoke+ semaglutide: 3808 participants, no difference in CDR-SB at 104 weeks (p = 0.57 and 0.46). Discontinued for negative outcome.
Valacyclovir in AD: ADAS-Cog change 10.86 vs 6.92 — a difference of 3.93 favouring placebo. Worse than placebo. Not recommended.
Early disease only — MCI or mild dementia with confirmed amyloid. ε4 homozygotes carry much higher ARIA risk. Anticoagulation is a serious concern. A patient hours from an MRI and a centre that can manage ARIA is not a candidate.
8 · DLB, FTD and vascular
▎You must be able to
  • Recall the four DLB core features and the neuroleptic warning.
  • Ask the questions that find bvFTD.

DLB: fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, RBD, spontaneous parkinsonism. One-year rule separates DLB from PDD; biology and treatment are the same. Cholinesterase inhibitors work better here than in AD — rivastigmine has the best evidence.

FTD: disorders of social behaviour (bvFTD) or language (PPA). C9orf72, GRN, MAPT. Pooled prevalence around 30 per 100,000 with extreme heterogeneity. Main differential is psychiatric.

Vascular: executive and attentional slowing with retrieval-type memory failure. Mixed AD–vascular is probably the commonest substrate in the old. Treat blood pressure; prescribe physical activity.

Never haloperidol or risperidone in possible DLB. And never a cholinesterase inhibitor in FTD — it may worsen behaviour.
9 · Rapid decline, autoimmune and prion
▎You must be able to
  • Escalate the investigation tier when the tempo is months.
  • Apply the autoimmune encephalitis criteria as written.

VITAMINS — Vascular, Infectious, Toxic-metabolic, Autoimmune, Metastatic, Iatrogenic, Neurodegenerative, Systemic/seizures. Assume treatable until proven otherwise; prion is the diagnosis you make last.

Autoimmune encephalitis: subacute (<3 months) memory, mental status or psychiatric change, plus supporting evidence, and reasonable exclusion of alternatives. Misdiagnosis is now the recognised problem. "Antibody-negative" is a restrictive category requiring inflammatory evidence — not a fallback for a negative panel.

sCJD: 1–2 per million/year, most dying within 5–6 months. DWI cortical ribboning; RT-QuIC. A psychiatric prodrome is common — mood changes are detectable up to 3 years before onset.

Two reversible causes missed in exactly this scenario: non-convulsive status epilepticus (an EEG takes an hour) and autoimmune encephalitis (treat on criteria; the antibody takes weeks).
10 · Neuropsychiatric symptoms and care
▎You must be able to
  • Separate apathy from depression.
  • Justify every antipsychotic prescription with five answers.

Meta-analysis of 5509 patients: apathy 45% at baseline, irritability and depression 35% each. Agitation (+16%) and apathy (+15%) increase most over time; sleep disturbance improves.

Apathy is not depression. Apathy is lost motivation without sadness, guilt or self-criticism. Antidepressants for apathy alone add side effects without benefit — but depression in AD is markedly under-treated, and sertraline and moclobemide have RCT support.

Always look for a trigger first: pain, infection, constipation, retention, dehydration, a new drug, sensory deprivation.

Before any antipsychotic: what is the behaviour, what triggers it, who is it harming, what has been tried, when will I review? If you cannot answer all five, the prescription is not ready.
Tube feeding in advanced dementia does not prolong life, prevent aspiration or heal pressure sores. Careful hand feeding is the answer — and the conversation belongs in clinic, not in the emergency department at 2 a.m.

Dementia differentiator

▎How to use it
  • Answer from the first domain to be affected and from what the informant describes — not from the current picture, which converges as disease advances.
  • The result gives the syndrome, the differential, the workup, the treatment and the commonest error.

CT rules out the surgical causes. Subdural, tumour, hydrocephalus, large infarct, gross atrophy — that is its job, and it does it today. Cognitive screen selector

▎Before any test
  • Test through the hearing aid and glasses, or the score is meaningless.
  • Test attention first. If attention fails, you are measuring delirium.
  • Record the raw score with the date and the instrument, so change can be measured later.

Rapidly progressive dementia

▎The rule
  • Decline over weeks to months. Assume treatable until proven otherwise — prion is the diagnosis you make last.
  • Escalate the tier. MRI, LP and EEG move from tier D to tier B, and that justifies transfer.

The sCJD prodrome is longer than taught. Linking autopsy-confirmed cases to national prescription data showed mood alterations detectable up to 3 years before symptom onset. Not a screening tool — but it explains why so many patients are treated for depression for months before the neurological picture declares itself.
Autoimmune encephalitis: apply the criteria as written. Subacute onset under 3 months, plus supporting evidence (focal CNS findings, new seizures, CSF pleocytosis or suggestive MRI), and reasonable exclusion of alternatives. Misdiagnosis is now the recognised problem — it is reversible, which is exactly why "just try steroids" is so tempting. A negative panel with no inflammatory markers and a normal MRI is evidence against, not evidence for antibody-negative disease.

Anti-amyloid therapy — eligibility and honest numbers

▎What to tell a family
  • Lecanemab and donanemab hold full FDA approval for early symptomatic AD; the EMA refused donanemab.
  • They slow decline modestly. They do not stop, reverse or cure.
  • ARIA is common and APOE ε4 dose-dependent. Most first events occur in the first three months.
  • They need infusions, serial MRI, amyloid confirmation and a service that can manage ARIA.

The numbers, from the source papers

Donanemab ARIA (n = 3030)
  • ARIA-E 24.4% vs 1.9% placebo
  • ARIA-H 31.3% vs 13.0%
  • Serious ARIA-E 1.5%; symptomatic 5.8%
  • 58.3% of first events by the third infusion
  • Risk ↑ with ε4 count, microhaemorrhages, siderosis, amyloid burden, MAP
Lecanemab in practice (n = 234)
  • Infusion reactions 37%, usually mild
  • Any ARIA 22% of those at risk
  • ARIA-E 15%; isolated ARIA-H 6.7%
  • ARIA-E 38% in ε4 homozygotes, 15% in heterozygotes
  • ARIA-E clusters early; rare after 6 months
What did not work
  • Semaglutide (evoke/evoke+, n = 3808): no CDR-SB difference at 104 weeks (p = 0.57, 0.46)
  • Valacyclovir: ADAS-Cog difference 3.93 favouring placebo — worse than placebo
  • Do not offer either, and say so plainly when asked
Something genuinely encouraging to add. Lower post-treatment amyloid correlated with slower progression across the donanemab population (R² 0.87 for CDR-SB) and with falls in p-tau217, p-tau181 and GFAP. The biology is real and the target is right. That is compatible with the present reality: a modest clinical effect, a heavy monitoring burden, and — for most patients in most of the world — blood pressure, hearing and depression being the interventions that will change their next five years.

Before any antipsychotic in dementia, answer five questions: what is the behaviour, what triggers it, who is it harming, what has been tried, and when will I review? They increase mortality and stroke risk, and once started they are rarely stopped.
Apathy is not depression. The apathetic patient has lost motivation and initiation but reports no sadness, guilt, hopelessness or self-criticism. Families describe both as "he's given up". Ask specifically about mood, tearfulness and self-blame — and record apathy even when there is no good drug for it, because it predicts faster functional decline.
